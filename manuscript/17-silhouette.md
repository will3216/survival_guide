# Chapter 17: The Silhouette of Belonging

> Rule 49: Decide your silhouette before someone decides it for you.

In my second year Halla Dorn handed me a tin, a brush and a list of everything the Spoke owned, and said to work through it and bring the tin back. The mark was a wheel hub with five spokes on it and a gap where another one should be. I did not design it, I never found out who did, and I assumed it was about bags.

That is genuinely what I thought I was doing: marking property, so bags came back to the right people. Bags, buckets, the two carts, the hand tools, the churn, the water butts, both gates.

Now the paint, which is the part I know something about.

I had two mixes and only one of them was any use. The red-brown was rust and a dug ochre ground fine into boiled linseed oil, and that one held on gate timber through two winters with no attention at all, going a bit chalky along the top edge of the planks where the weather sat and nowhere else. The white was limewash with lamp black in it for grey, beautiful on the stone of the well house, and it came off a canvas bag in a fortnight onto everything the bag touched, including a child. If you take one thing out of this chapter take the first one. An earth colour in oil, on wood, on the face the weather reaches.

I did the gates last. The ladder was not ours and was wanted back, so everything that needed it got done first — the roof peak, the butt rims, the top of the well house — and the gates waited, a gate being the only flat thing in a place you can paint from the ground. So I was up that ladder with my back to the yard most of the week, and came down on the Friday to find the mark on three bags I had not touched.

> **— T.** There is one of these on my gate. I have watched the thing work, from a hill above a river crossing, two parties who could read each other at a distance and arrived with nothing left to settle but their business, so I will not touch a word of the method that follows: it is sound, it is his, and it is still how it is done. What I have of where it came from is six sentences on paint — which mix held two winters on a gate, and which one came off a bag in a fortnight — and a note that he did the gates last on account of the ladder.

## What A Mark Buys You

In my eighth year I found a good grey wool coat in a wardrobe and wore it for two days. I was turned off three gates in those two days without getting a whole sentence out of anybody, and at the third one a stone landed short of me, which is a polite stone and I took it as such. It was somebody's coat — not the dead man's, a group's, and I had put their outline on without knowing there was one to put on. That is what having your silhouette decided for you is like. Nobody tells you, and you cannot see yourself from three hundred yards.

So: three hundred yards. That is not a measurement and I am not going to dress it up as one. It is roughly the distance at which somebody looking at you decides whether to put a hand on something, and at that range they have your outline, your number and your speed, and nothing else whatever.

**Recognition at distance buys you quiet first contacts.** I cannot give you a proportion and I would not believe one if somebody handed it to me. What I have is eleven groups and forty years, and in that count almost everything that went badly between two groups went badly at short notice, between people who did not know who was coming until it was close. Two parties who recognise each other at three hundred yards have had the conversation already. At thirty they are having it with their hands. Chapter 7 is the long version of walking up to strangers; the reason it works is that a frightened person decides what you are inside two seconds, and a mark buys those two seconds from further off.

**A mark says a place is claimed and kept.** A mark obviously repainted says somebody here has that job, which is the same information a greased hinge gives you. And a faded mark is worse than no mark, because it says somebody used to live here.

**And it gives the person wearing it something to be in.** I am not going to file that under soft. I have watched people leave a place with food in it for a worse place with a sign on the gate, and I have been one of them.

## Designing One

Treat it as a technical problem, because it is one. Everything you like about a design is detail, and detail is the first thing distance takes off it. Lettering goes first, then faces, then anything drawn inside an outline. What survives is the outline, the contrast and the count.

So, five things.

1. **Silhouette, not picture.** Decide what shape it is at a glance and design that. Something you could cut out of a board with a saw and still recognise on a skyline.
2. **One high-contrast element, and only one.** Light on dark or dark on light, big, and put it off centre so it does not read as a shadow. Two colours doing the same job cancel at distance and leave you a smudge.
3. **Reproducible by hand, with what you will still have in five years.** The test I use: can a twelve-year-old do it on a wall with a stick of charcoal, badly, and have it still read? If it needs a stencil, a straight edge or a steady hand, you have not designed a group's mark. You have designed one person's mark, and that person will not always be there.
4. **Hard to get wrong.** Count the parts. Four of anything is countable at a glance and seven is not, and a mark with an inside and an outside, or a left and a right, has a wrong version built into it. A mark that can be got wrong hands somebody a thing to be right about.
5. **It has to work on a person, a vehicle, a gate and a grave.** Those four, because they are the four it will be on.

The four want different things. On a person it has to go on and come off: sewn, pinned, tied, painted on a band — not the one coat they own, for reasons in the costs. On a vehicle it wants to read from the front and read moving, which means bigger and higher than looks sensible standing still. On a gate it wants the earth colour in oil and a repainting schedule somebody owns, because of the faded-mark problem above.

A grave is the hard one and the one I would design against, because it has to be legible in twenty years with nobody tending it, and paint will not do twenty years unattended. So: cut, scratched, punched, or built out of the shape of the stone. If your mark cannot be made with a nail and an hour it will not be on the graves, and the graves are the ones still there when the gate has gone.

## How A Mark Gets Picked Up

Here is the part that is actually difficult, and the part I got wrong.

In my seventeenth year I was in a place I am not going to name because the people are still in it, and I decided they needed a mark, and I did nine bags in an afternoon with a brush and a very good opinion of myself. Three came back scrubbed. One came back scrubbed and with the handle gone, because whoever did it went at the canvas with grit and took the stitching out with the paint. Nobody said one word to me about any of it, and that is how I know what it was. People do not argue with a man carrying a tin. They wait until he has gone and then they wash the bag.

**Rule 35. Paint it on your own gate first.** A mark handed to people is a uniform and gets treated like one. A mark people pick up is theirs. The difference is not in the mark; it is entirely in who put it there, and it is not a thing you can fake by asking nicely.

So the method is short. You have one gate. Paint it. Paint your own bag and cart too. Then stop, and do nothing else about it for a season. You do not get a vote on anybody else's gate — not modesty, the whole mechanism: the only thing that makes the mark worth having to somebody else is that nobody made them take it. You will know it has taken when you see it on something you did not paint, done worse than you did it.

**And let the first versions be ugly.** Mine on that well-house wall were nine different shapes and two of them were upside down, and it did not matter in the slightest, because nobody came round and fixed them. The moment somebody comes round and fixes them there is a right one, and once there is a right one you have stopped having a mark and started having a standard, and a standard is a thing a person can fall short of. Ugly and everybody's beats neat and somebody's.

And a group mark is not a scavenging mark. **Rule 47**, mark what you clear, is information for the next person through a building. A group mark is an address. Do not write either one in the other's place.

## When It Stops Being A Mark

You will not spot this from inside by asking whether the place is a good place. Everybody's place is a good place. Look at the yard instead; every one of these you can watch happening.

- Somebody is **told** to wear it. Not handed one, not asked — told, and in front of other people.
- There is a **list of who has one**, or a person whose job is issuing them.
- **Absence gets remarked on.** Someone comes to the fire without it and it is mentioned, lightly, as a joke, and then it is mentioned again the next week.
- **The ugly ones are being fixed.** Somebody is going round correcting them. There is now a right one.
- **It has moved off things and onto people.** A mark on a gate is about the gate. A mark on a coat is about the coat. A mark on skin is not about anything that comes off.
- **One person does not have to have it.** **Rule 77** — a rule that stops applying to one person has stopped.

Any one of those on its own is a bad fortnight. It is the collection, and which direction it has moved in over a season.

The Ninth Hand's handprint started as exactly what is written in this chapter. Somebody put a hand in slip and ash on the cistern wall because it was their wall, and by the time I got there it was the left hand with the thumb folded under, and there was a right hand and a wrong hand, and I used the wrong one for two years and so did most of the valley, and nobody minded that at all because nobody was counting yet. Somebody started counting. So when you are picking a mark, go back to the fourth thing on the list: pick one that is hard to get wrong, because a mark that can be got wrong is a mark somebody can be appointed to be right about.

## What It Costs You

**A mark makes you identifiable to people you would rather were confused.** It is an address, and an address works in both directions. So mark what you want claimed and nothing else: not a cache, not the route in, not a cart you are about to trade away, and not a bag somebody is carrying through a valley where your name is no good.

**It makes leaving visible, which makes leaving cost more.** This is the one to think hardest about, because it falls on the person least able to argue with it. Somebody who goes either walks out wearing where they came from or stands in a yard taking it off, and the second is a thing people watch. So keep it off the one coat a person owns and off the bag they would need in order to go — a tied band, a sewn patch, something they can be out of in ten seconds in their own doorway. **Rule 31** is know the way out before you go in, and chapter 7 is the long version of leaving; nothing I paint on a gate is allowed to make that harder.

**And a group that polices its own dress code has started doing something else.** I cannot tell you what, because it is different every time and the people doing it could not tell you either. What I can tell you is that the mark is not the problem and taking it down will not fix it. The mark is only the part of it you can see from the yard, which is why the six things above are worth watching.

## The Short Version

- Rule 49: decide your silhouette before someone decides it for you. You are read at a few hundred yards whether you chose a mark or not, and at that range they have your outline, your number and your speed and nothing else.
- Design for the outline: one high-contrast element, off centre, no detail and no lettering. Detail is the first thing distance takes off.
- Make it reproducible by hand with what you will still have in five years: a twelve-year-old, a wall, a stick of charcoal, badly. If it needs a stencil or a steady hand it is one person's mark.
- Make it hard to get wrong: countable parts, no left and right, no correct way up. A mark that can be got wrong gives somebody a thing to be right about.
- Test it on all four surfaces — a person, a vehicle, a gate, a grave — because those are the four it will be on. Cut or scratch the grave one; nothing painted lasts twenty years unattended.
- For paint: an earth colour, rust or ochre ground fine into boiled linseed oil, on wood, on the weather face. Limewash chalks off cloth in a fortnight, onto everything it touches.
- Rule 35: paint it on your own gate first, then wait a season and do nothing. A mark handed to people is a uniform and gets resented; a mark people pick up is theirs. You get no vote on anybody else's gate.
- Let the first versions be ugly and do not correct them. A corrector means there is a right one, and a right one means a standard somebody can fall short of. Ugly and everybody's beats neat and somebody's.
- Watch the yard for six things: somebody told rather than asked, a list of who has one, absence remarked on at the fire, the ugly ones being fixed, the mark moving off things and onto people, and one person exempt (Rule 77). Keep it off the one coat and the one bag a person would need in order to leave — Rule 31 and chapter 7 are the long version of that.
