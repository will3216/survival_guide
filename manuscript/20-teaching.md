# Chapter 20: Teaching the Ones Who Never Saw a Lightbulb

> Rule 80: Go to the daft dinner.

Ais and I were carrying a plank up to the back step in the spring when she told me she did not think there had ever been cold boxes.

It was four metres of board and a two-person job and she had the far end, which is how I have taught people anything for twenty-five years: you hand somebody the other end of something and then you talk, because a person carrying a load is already concentrating and will let a sentence in sideways. Say it across a table and they go polite on you and nothing lands.

I had said something about keeping milk. She said, without stopping walking, that she thought the cold box business was probably a story. She was not arguing with me, and that is worse: there is no cold box, nobody she has met has seen one, and nothing she can see would make one, so when I say the words they do not go anywhere. She has nowhere to put them.

That evening I drew one on the clean side of a flour sack and held it up for her — chest high, a door on the front, a motor at the back, cold inside all day and all night for years with nobody watching it. She looked at it a good while. Then she asked which way up it stood, and I said on the floor, like a cupboard, and she said nothing and went back to her papers.

I have written four lines about that box in chapter 13 and I would write them again, and I know what they are worth. Four lines of mine against everything she has ever stood in front of. The four lines lose.

So: handing over what you know to people who have never seen most of what you are talking about. I have got a good deal of it wrong and some of it worked out.

## Teach From What Is In The Room

The rule I go by now is that any explanation resting on a thing they have never seen has already failed, and the dangerous part is that it fails quietly. They nod. A nod is not a receipt, and I have walked away from several of them pleased with myself.

So check every time, in the only way that works: have them do it, or tell it back in their own words with you not helping. If what comes back is your words in your order, you have taught them a noise.

Then build the explanation out of what is in the room. To explain why a siphon runs I put one bucket on the step, one on the ground and the hose in their hand, and they feel the pull before anybody says the word pressure. To explain why a cellar keeps food I start with the north wall they are leaning on, which is cold, which they know already.

I used to spend a great deal of teaching on the old world and I do not any more. It is not knowledge to them, it is a story about me, and a story will not keep anything from going soft. Say in one sentence what the thing did, if the job needs them to know something once did it, then say what we do instead — for the cold box, chapter 13, a rack and a crock, and it works.

## Watch, Assist, Lead, Teach

A procedure explained is not a procedure learned. Everything I can actually do, somebody stood next to me while I did it wrong. This is not my invention: it is how every trade I ever carried for handed things over, and older than any of them.

Four steps, and the order is the whole of it.

1. **Watch.** They stand where they can see your hands, not across the room, and they hold something — the light, the spare, the far end. Say what you are doing and why, in that order, while you do it. If you cannot say it while your hands are going, you do not know it as well as you think.
2. **Assist.** They do the half that cannot hurt them and you do the half that bites. Hand over the fiddly bits, keep the dangerous ones, and say which is which out loud, or they will assume you kept the interesting part.
3. **Lead.** They do all of it. You stand there with your hands in your pockets and do not speak, and step in for one reason only: a thing about to happen that cannot be undone. This is the step everybody skips, including me, and skipping it is how you get somebody who has helped with a job forty times and never once done it.
4. **Teach.** They teach it to somebody else, with you out of their eyeline. Nobody owns a skill until they have taught it. Explaining a job out loud forces it into an order, and the order is where the holes are — they get to the fourth step and stop, and now both of you know what was missing, which neither knew an hour before.

Step 3 is the one that costs, and it is also the only step where they find out what a mistake feels like from the inside: early, on a small thing, with somebody standing there. That is the whole purchase.

And do not start a person at step 1 on the day the job is urgent; teaching in an emergency is you doing the job with somebody in the way. Work out now which step each person is on for each job that matters, keep it on the wall, and move them one step when the quiet comes.

## What Is Worth The Paper

Paper is dear and a page takes an evening to write out fair, so you cannot write everything, and pretending otherwise is how people end up with eleven pages on the one job they found interesting.

**Write down what has an order or a number in it.** Sequences, times, ratios, how long to hold a thing, how far from the water, how many days. Those are what a frightened person loses first, and they do not survive being passed mouth to mouth without drifting, because there is nothing inside a number to tell anybody it has slipped.

**Do not waste paper on judgment.** No page has taught anybody when a wall is about to go, or which of two strangers to let through a gate. I have tried to write both and what came out was either too vague to use or confident enough to get somebody killed. Judgment goes across a plank, by being wrong next to somebody who is not.

**Rank the pages by what kills fastest and comes up most.** Blood, water, cold, then teeth and rot, then food, then everything interesting. If you only ever get four pages written they are the first four, and nobody will thank you for them, because nothing will have happened.

Then the mechanics, which are cheap and get skipped: numbered steps, short lines, a hand somebody else can read, and a copy, because the only reader it will ever have is tired and frightened and holding it in bad light, and because one copy is not a copy.

## The Thing I Am Actually Afraid Of

It is not that this gets lost. Lost is survivable: somebody works it out again the hard way, the way I did, and it costs them, and they get there.

What I am afraid of is it coming through with the reason stripped off.

Chapter 19 has a song in it that teaches three minutes of rolling boil. Three is right. I would not change a word of it, and chapter 4 is the long version: three is chapter 4's own answer for anybody who cannot say how high up they are, and a child cannot. That song will outlive everybody who can read.

Now look at what it costs, because chapter 4 says that part too — water costs you fuel, boiling everything you drink means burning something every day for the rest of your life, and in a hard winter you choose between warm and treated, which is a real choice with a wrong answer. A house down near the sea, singing three, burns two extra minutes of fuel on every pot for as long as it stands. That is fine. It is the price of the song and it is cheap, and I would pay it.

What is not fine is a house that cannot work out it is allowed to stop. They are not wrong about the water; they have the number and the number is good. They simply cannot get at it, because nobody left in the house knows the three was ever about hills, so there is nothing in there to reason with — and the winter that finishes them is the one where the fuel was what mattered and the pot could have come off at a minute. A right answer nobody can adjust is a right answer for exactly as long as the conditions hold still.

So here is the only thing I am going to ask of you. **Write the why next to the what.** Every time, even when it costs paper you wanted for something else. Not an argument and not a page — the clause. *Three minutes because water boils cooler the higher you go.* *Count out loud because your hands are not reliable when you are frightened.* The what can only be obeyed. The why can be checked, and argued with, and put right by somebody who knows a thing I did not, which is the only way any of this gets better instead of only getting older.

The daft business in chapter 19 is the one exception, for the reason chapter 19 gives: a thing with no reason in it cannot be argued out of. A boil time is not that. It has a reason whether you write it down or not, and if you do not, the reason is gone in two lifetimes and the number is still there, and a number on its own is a rule nobody can repair.

> **— T.** I have never seen a lightbulb work. I have been shown the broken ones; I am prepared to believe in the glass and no further. I have read this chapter until the corner of it came away in my hand, and mended it, and read it again. He is explaining a cold box to a girl who has no place to put the idea, and he is right that it will not take, and he writes the reason down beside the instruction anyway, which is the whole of why I can still use the instruction. He is writing to somebody nobody here has heard of, in a hand that cannot be answered. I would have answered him.

## Handing It Over

I am sixty or near enough and my hands are slower than my opinions. These pages are finished, and there is a list of the Rules at the back, which is the ones I could remember — I have lost a good many, and the ones I have are the ones I got wrong first and told myself after so that I did not do it twice.

What they are for, as far as I can tell: I got the first year wrong in about nine ways that nearly finished me, and every one had a reason behind it somebody could have told me in a sentence. Nobody was there to. So this is the sentences, written out for one person, so that when I am not about she has them to hand and does not have to buy them the way I did. It is not an account of anything that happened. I was in the way of a good deal of what happened, and what I kept of it is the weather and the order people arrived in, which is no use to anybody.

One more, and then the list.

The third winter in the Bell Country the lamp oil was short and nothing anybody started that season got finished. What held was a dinner on a Thursday that nobody could explain and that did nothing. The table was a door across two barrels. I set the chairs out, was late most weeks, and went to thirty-one of them. I had been against the whole business for eleven years and would have told you so at length.

I would not have got through that winter without it. And the best evening I have had was two winters before, at the feast, at the end of the table with my foot in a bucket and the barrel I dropped on it lying behind my chair, missing most of what went on at the far end.

## What It Costs You

Teaching properly is slower than doing the job and the cost is paid by whoever is waiting on the job. Step 3 is the dear one: somebody else's hands, twice as long, making the mistakes you already know about, and watching it is genuinely unpleasant if you are any good at the work. You do not get the job and the teaching out of the same hours. A place that says it has no time to teach is a place with one person who can do each thing, which is one bad fortnight from losing a trade for good.

It also costs you your own way of doing it. Anybody you take to step 4 will teach it differently, drop a step you liked and add one you think is daft, and some of what they change will be better than what you had.

Paper costs food. An evening writing a page out fair is an evening off the woodpile, and the triage is brutal: most of what you know goes with you, and you choose what does not knowing you will choose wrong.

And the why costs more paper than the what — roughly half again, in my hand. Pay it. It is the difference between leaving somebody a procedure and leaving them something they can mend.

## The Short Version

- Assume any explanation leaning on a thing they have never seen has already failed, and failed quietly, because they nodded. Check by having them do it, or tell it back in their own words with you not helping.
- Build explanations out of what is in the room — the cellar wall, the bucket, the plank — and hold the vanished world to one sentence, then the job in hand.
- Hand jobs over in four steps and skip none: watch, where they can see your hands and you say what and why as you go; assist, them on the fiddly half, you on the dangerous half, said out loud; lead, them doing all of it while you stand silent, stepping in only for what cannot be undone; teach, them showing a third person with you out of their eyeline.
- Nobody owns a skill until they have taught it: explaining it forces the order, and the order is where the holes show. Know which step each person is on, move them a step in the quiet, and do not start anybody at step 1 on the day the job is urgent.
- Write down what has an order or a number in it — sequences, times, ratios, distances, days — and spend no paper on judgment, which must be taught in person. Rank pages by what kills fastest and comes up most: blood, water, cold, teeth, food, then the interesting things.
- Write in short numbered steps, in a hand somebody else can read, for a reader who is tired, frightened and in bad light. One copy is not a copy: two in two buildings and a third elsewhere.
- Write the why next to the what, every time, even when the paper is short. A number that has lost its reason still gets obeyed, correctly and for ever, by people who can no longer tell when it has stopped applying to them, and a right answer nobody can adjust lasts only as long as the conditions do.
- Rule 80: go to the daft dinner. The useless weekly business with everybody at it holds a place up better than the work you would rather be doing, and you will not see it doing it.
