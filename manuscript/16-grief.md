# Chapter 16: The Winter The Brake Went

> Rule 61: A day off is maintenance.

In my twelfth year I went off the back of a standing flat cart and broke two fingers on the way down.

It was a cart I had been getting on and off for six years. Lever brake, a pawl dropping into a ratchet cut into the hub, and you set it by pushing the lever over and hearing the pawl go in. I pushed the lever over and heard nothing, which is all the information anybody needs, and I got down anyway. The cart ran about four feet down a yard with a fall on it. I came off the tailboard, put my hand out, and the two middle fingers went onto the cobbles with me on top of them. Splinted ten weeks. You can haul water one-handed and it is slower, and you want the full bucket on the good side.

Vera Ott died in the March, of something in her chest that took nine days. She could tell by ear whether a thing had seated and I never could, so I check a brake now by putting my shoulder into the cart and leaning on it before my feet leave the bed. A brake that is holding tells you that through your shoulder. A pawl you only think you heard will not.

> **— T.** Somebody taught her once, the way she taught him, and to those people she must have been— I cannot get to the end of that sentence, and I have tried it twice on paper. I have read his one line about the March more times than I can give any account of.

The fingers were in the May, which is nine weeks after. That gap is the part of this worth writing down. I had been careful for twenty years, and then for about eight months I was not careful, and the worst of it was nowhere near the beginning.

So: grief, having nothing to do, and the daft weekly business that holds a place together. In that order, because that is the order they cost me.

## What It Does To Your Hands

Grief is not my subject and I have nothing to tell a person about their own. What I can tell you is what it does to the work, because the work is where I saw it — on myself, and on nine or ten others since.

It makes you careless in a particular way, which is not the same as slow. The steps that go are the automatic ones. You do not forget how to do a job; you stop checking that the job took. You set the brake and do not listen for it. You stand the ladder up and do not look at the feet. Nobody in that state thinks they are impaired, because from the inside everything feels normal, and that is the part that breaks bones.

And the dangerous stretch is not the first week. The first week there are people in the house, something to be arranged and somebody else cooking, and the arranging holds a person upright. Then the arranging finishes, everybody goes back to their own work, and about three weeks in you have a full round and nothing propping you up. I cannot give you a number for how long that lasts and I will not pretend to — I have my own fingers and nine or ten people I watched, which is not evidence of anything. What I will say is that nobody I have known to come to harm after a death came to harm in the first few days. It was weeks after, every time, and mostly months before their hands were reliable again.

So do not stand the watch down after the burial. That is the week to start it.

There is no treatment in this chapter; I am not a healer and this is not a thing anybody treats. Two practices, and both of them are other people's jobs rather than the grieving person's.

**The first is that somebody notices, out loud, and keeps noticing.** Name one person to keep an eye on them for a season, tell them you have done it, and watch the work rather than the mood. Somebody crying at the barrel is fine. Somebody who has stopped eating with anybody, is awake at three, and takes the high job alone is not, whatever they say when you ask, and they will say they are fine and mean it. Take the heavy and the high work off them for a while without making a speech about it — the roof, the ladder, the night watch, anything with an edge or a drop or a machine in it. And **Rule 4** is worth more to them than to anybody else: count before you move, out loud, every time. It is the only thing I know that puts a check back where the automatic one used to be.

**The second is Rule 36. Give the grieving a duty someone depends on.** Small, daily, at the same time, and sized so it cannot be failed. The half that matters is the second half: somebody has to be waiting on it.

The Bell Country's rota allowed three days for a death. Hollis Dray had me back on the water on the second, four houses, and told me so at the bottom of the path, and I expect she was short of hands. Four houses is about an hour and a half of walking and nobody dies if it is late. But two of them had nobody in who could carry, and those two knew what time I came.

That is the whole mechanism. Not a project, not a job meant to improve anybody, and not an hour of being sat with. An hour of work with a person at the other end gets somebody out of their door at a fixed time, which will not happen on its own. Kindness with no duty in it lets a person stop moving, and once they have stopped for a fortnight it is much harder to start.

Be clear about what it buys, because I have heard people claim more. Nobody's grief gets smaller because the buckets arrived. The buckets arriving only keeps a person on their feet through the stretch where nothing helps. That is a small claim and it is the one I will stand behind.

## Nothing To Do Will Take A Group Apart

More of the groups I have belonged to or watched over a fence came apart from having nothing to do than from anything outside. That is eleven groups and twenty years of looking, and it is not a tidy count, but it is not a close one either.

It runs in an order you can set your watch by. First the work runs out, usually in the second winter, when everything urgent has been done once and the rest is maintenance nobody can see the point of. Then people start taking risks for the interest of it — a building entered that nobody needed entering, a river crossed in spring, a two-day walk for nothing, and the drink, which is a hazard of its own and not one I am writing about. Then the arguing, which is always about the rota and never about the rota. Then people stop turning up, which is the one you cannot fix, because by then there is nothing to turn up to.

I cannot tell you why a person with nothing to do goes looking for something to happen, and I have never heard an account of it I believed. They do, reliably, and in a place like this the something they find is usually somebody going somewhere they should not.

So treat an idle fortnight the way you would a smell of gas. It is not a reward you have earned; it is a condition with consequences attached, and they arrive in that order.

## Morale Is Infrastructure

I thought all of this was nonsense for eleven years. Morale was the word people used when they wanted an evening off, and I was the one carrying the chairs in for whatever they had decided to do with it.

Five things, and the reason for each, because these are the first things a busy group drops.

1. **Eat together, at least once a day, at a stated time.** A meal is the only thing in a settlement everybody attends without being rostered, so it is where you find out who is not there. Chapter 12 makes the same point about soap at the latrine: the things that look like manners are how a group checks on itself.
2. **Work that visibly finishes.** Hauling water never finishes. A wall does. Give people a job with a finished edge on it now and then, something they can walk past afterwards, because a task with an ending does for a person what tying a lace does for panic.
3. **Music, or anything somebody is bad at in front of everybody else.** I cannot sing and I have no opinion about music, and I will still tell you to have some. I have wintered twice in places with it and three times in places without, and I know which were quieter.
4. **A day off that is actually off. Rule 61.** Not a light day. Off: no rota, no jobs, nobody permitted to ask. One day in seven if you can carry it and one in ten if you cannot, named in advance and on the same day each time, so people can spend the week looking forward to it, which is half of what it does. **Rule 55** says gear you do not maintain is gear you do not have. People are the same object and fail the same way, quietly and all at once.
5. **Projects with an end and a date on them.** An orchard is not a project, it is a condition. A bath house is a project. You want one thing going that will be finished by a stated week, and everybody there when it is, doing nothing useful.

Underneath all five are the two cheapest interventions available and the two nobody counts as work: sleep, and doing the same things at the same times. A person short of sleep has worse judgement and slower hands and does not know it, which is the same failure grief causes and stacks on top of it. Routine does most of the rest. Neither costs anything or needs materials, and both are the first things dropped when a place gets busy, which is exactly backwards.

## The Dinner Nobody Could Explain

In the Bell Country's third winter the lamp oil was short, one terrace was sick with something that went round twice, and nothing anybody started that season got finished. What held was a dinner on a Thursday.

It was in the store with the door shut against the wind. The table was a door laid across two barrels. The chairs came out of nine different houses and no two of them matched. Whoever had anything put it in the one pot, and the pot was thin, and I have had better. Nobody there could have told you what it was for. I asked twice, got two answers and stopped asking. Nothing was decided at it and no council sat there.

My part was the chairs. I set them out, and carried the extra ones in from the house at the east end two at a time, and I was late most weeks because the round ran long in the dark. That was the winter the brake went, so I came to the first few of them with a splint on and a poor opinion of brakes. I went to thirty-one of those dinners and had the chair job for good, because nobody else wanted the walk back for the chairs.

The fires had got to three by that winter, and the dinner was still one table. It was the only thing in that place that never got divided. I was outside the door with two chairs.

The next four chapters are about making this kind of thing on purpose: the marks a group wears, the things it kneels to, the days it keeps, and what you hand on to whoever comes after. None of it digs a ditch and all of it looks like time you have not got. I was against the lot of it, and I carried the chairs, and I would get the chairs out again tonight.

## What It Costs You

The day off costs you a seventh of your work, and in a short season you will feel every hour of it. There is no clever answer. It is maintenance, and maintenance gets paid either way — on a stated day, or all at once in a winter when four people stop getting up.

Rule 36 costs somebody else real work, and it has to, or it is not a duty. It must be genuinely depended on and genuinely safe to drop, which means arranging the cover beforehand and not saying you have. And it goes wrong in one direction: a duty too big is a second injury, and the person will take it and say nothing.

The noticing costs privacy and there is no way round it. Being watched for a season is not pleasant, and the right thing is to say plainly that you are doing it, on the day you start. Doing it quietly is how people come to believe a group talks about them, which is true, and which is worse than being asked.

And none of it is a cure, and I will not have it read as one. A person who has lost somebody has still lost somebody in the spring. All any of it does is keep them in one piece through the months when they are not watching their own hands. I got through mine with two broken fingers and a cart that still works.

## The Short Version

- The dangerous stretch after a death is weeks and months later, not days. The first week has people and arranging in it; the fourth has a full round and nothing holding anybody up. Start watching when the visitors stop.
- Grief shows in work as skipped checks rather than slowness — the brake not listened for, the ladder feet not looked at — and nobody in it believes they are impaired. Take the high, heavy and night work off them for a season, without a speech.
- Rule 4: count before you move, out loud. It is the cheapest replacement for a check that has stopped happening by itself.
- Rule 36: give the grieving a duty someone depends on. Small, daily, same time, impossible to fail, with a person waiting at the other end. Arrange the cover in advance without saying so.
- Name one person to notice out loud for a season, tell the grieving person you have, and watch the work and not the mood: eating alone, awake at three, taking the high job alone.
- Treat an idle fortnight as a hazard. Groups come apart from nothing to do far more often than from anything outside, and in order: work runs out, risks taken for interest, arguing about the rota, people stop turning up.
- Morale is infrastructure: a daily meal together at a stated time, work that visibly finishes, music, projects with an end and a date, and a day off that is actually off.
- Rule 61: a day off is maintenance. Rule 55 says gear you do not maintain is gear you do not have, and people fail the same way: quietly, all at once.
- Sleep and routine are the cheapest things available and the first two dropped when a place gets busy. Short sleep damages judgement and hands and hides that it is doing it.
