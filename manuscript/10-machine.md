# Chapter 10: The Machine

> Rule 64: A bicycle you can fix beats a truck you cannot.

The first thing Vera Ott said about me she said to somebody else, with me in the room holding the lamp. I was a pair of hands with an opinion, she said, and she could work with the hands. I wrote it down that night because I thought it was funny. I was still holding the lamp six years later and had stopped having the opinion, which she would have called my education.

I met her a fortnight before that by being hit by a motorcycle in her yard. It was going at a walking pace, ridden by a man I had twice been told to stand clear of, and it put me on my back across a length of angle iron and took the skin off my forearm wrist to elbow. I have never gone off motorcycles. You can lift the whole of one, see every part from the outside, and keep one running for a decade with a cold chisel. I would have one now if anybody made tires.

What I did for her for six years was hold things, fetch things and turn things the direction she said to turn them. I did not understand most of it at the time. She would say *other way*, I would go the other way, and years later find out why. All of this is hers. It comes up because of the fuel.

> **— T.** *A pair of hands with an opinion, and I can work with the hands.* He has set that down word for word, with the lamp in it, and recorded it as a joke at his own expense. The woman who said it taught the man who taught everybody, and this is the only sentence of hers anybody has: in a yard, about him. Her first name appears nowhere within four paragraphs of it. I checked.

## Fuel Is The Whole Problem

An engine will sit in a field for thirty years waiting for you. Fuel is the part with the clock on it.

**Gasoline is the shortest-lived thing in the chain, and it is not close.** Its light fractions evaporate out even through a decent seal, and what is left oxidises into gums and varnish that glue up every narrow passage they touch. The guidance I was handed, which matches twenty years of opening cans, is roughly three to six months for gasoline in a sealed can in the cool, and anything older treated as a maybe rather than a fuel. Stale gasoline does not refuse to work; it half works, which is why a bad can costs you the carburettor and not the morning.

**Diesel keeps longer and has its own problems.** Reckon something like a year in good conditions, longer if clean and cool. Water condenses out of the air in a part-empty drum and sits under the fuel, and that line is where things grow — a slime that blocks filters, which you find by the filter blocking. In the cold, the wax in diesel comes out of solution and turns the fuel to porridge in the lines, which looks exactly like an engine fault.

So, storage. Four words, in her order.

1. **Full.** Air brings the water and the evaporation, so a full container has neither. If you cannot fill the drum, use a smaller drum.
2. **Sealed.** A seal that is actually a seal, checked by hand, because the light fractions leave through a tired gasket and the water comes in the same way.
3. **Cool.** Heat drives evaporation and every reaction in the can, and a drum in the sun runs a pressure cycle twice a day that works a seal loose. Under the ground, under a floor, on the north side of something.
4. **Dark.** Out of the sun for the container as much as the fuel, because sunlight turns plastic brittle, and a failed container is worse than fuel going off.

Paint the contents and the month on the can itself, never a tag, because a tag comes off and then you have a can of something. Use it in order, and never add a doubtful can to a good one: you have not improved the bad fuel, you have ruined the good.

### The part that will kill you, and why

These are the rules a cold man in a hurry wants to break, so I give the reason every time.

**Gasoline vapour is heavier than air, so it does not go up and away — it runs downhill along the floor and pools in the low place.** It travels a long way from an open can, sits unseen in a cellar, a pit or a drain, and it is the vapour and not the liquid that catches, so a pool of it finds a pilot light a room away. **Never pour, pump, drain or decant fuel indoors, in a shed or over any sunken place — do it outside, in moving air, on open ground, with nothing hot or burning near it.** No lamp, no stove, nobody smoking. And never run an engine indoors either, for a different reason: the exhaust is full of carbon monoxide, which kills people who felt fine, and chapter 5 is the long version of that.

**Fuel moving through a hose or a nozzle builds a static charge, and the spark that charge makes is enough.** Stand the container on bare ground, never in a vehicle or on a plastic liner, because a container off the ground has nowhere to put the charge. Touch the metal before you start, keep the nozzle in contact with the neck of the can and your hand on both while it flows, and pour slowly, because a fast splashing pour is what builds the charge.

**Never siphon fuel by mouth** — a mouthful goes into the lungs rather than the stomach and does them real harm; use a pump, a squeeze bulb, or the height of the container. And **never store fuel in a drinking container**, however clearly you write on it, because somebody who cannot read your writing will one day be thirsty in your yard.

## The Order You Check Things In

This is not a repair manual. Nobody can teach you to repair an engine in a chapter. What I can give you is the order to look in, which is most of what a mechanic is and all of what Vera gave me.

Every engine wants **fuel**, **spark**, **air** and **compression**, checked in that order, changing exactly one thing between tries so you know what fixed it. Before any of it comes the step she called step nothing.

**Rule 38. Check the stupid thing first.** It is usually the stupid thing. In six years I watched her open the top of an engine perhaps four times, and watched her find a closed tap, a switch off, a cable off its post, an empty tank or a machine in gear more often than I could count. I spent a morning on a generator that would not turn over, and she walked past, moved a lever six inches and said nothing, which was worse than if she had.

Is there fuel in it, is the tap open, is the switch on, is the kill switch off. Is the battery connected, the terminals clean metal, the leads on, the machine out of gear. Each takes seconds, and the most embarrassing thing comes first.

Then the four.

1. **Fuel.** Is fuel reaching the engine, not just sitting in the tank. Tap open, tank not rusted, filter not blocked, line not perished or kinked, the fuel not four years old and smelling of varnish, which it tells you from a foot away. Old fuel is the commonest reason a machine that was fine when parked is not fine now.
2. **Spark.** On a gasoline engine, is there a spark at all: leads on and dry, points and plug not fouled or wet, nothing corroded or full of water. A diesel has no spark to check, which is one of its honest advantages.
3. **Air.** Is it breathing. The filter is almost always the thing, because it has spent a decade collecting dust and the nest of something small. Blocked air behaves exactly like a fuel fault, which is why it comes after fuel.
4. **Compression.** Is the engine holding pressure. Last, because you cannot fix it at the roadside and getting at it means opening the engine. Fuel, spark and air all present and it still will not run means a workshop, a trained person and parts nobody is making. That is not failure, it is information, and the information is to stop.

## It Will Be The Tires

Everybody plans for the engine and loses the vehicle to the tires, never the other way round.

Rubber ages whether you drive on it or not. Oxygen, ozone and sunlight harden it from the outside until it loses its flex and cracks, and it does that standing in a barn as well as on the road, so the tread depth on a vehicle that has stood twenty years tells you nothing. Look at the sidewalls and the bottoms of the grooves for fine cracks that open when you flex the rubber hard; chapter 8 is the long version of what air and light do to rubber. A tire that cracks when flexed is finished, whatever it looks like.

Nobody you will ever meet can make one. That is the sentence. A tire is cured rubber over steel and fabric in a heated mould under pressure. There is no yard version of it, which is not true of a gasket. We kept two trucks running for the Bell Country out to the far side of the lake in my eleventh year, and what I remember is an afternoon with a sidewall gone on a slope and nothing to put in its place.

So the tires set the clock on the machine. Keep them shaded, because sunlight does more to standing rubber than mileage does. Keep them at pressure, because a soft tire flexes more in the sidewall and heats, and heat kills rubber. Drive slow and light. Take every sound tire off every wreck you pass, note the size, keep it dark, cool and off concrete. And before you commit to a vehicle, count the tires you have for it and be honest about the number.

## Her Actual Position, Which Took Me Years

Vera's position was three sentences long and I resented all three for four years.

**Most vehicles are not repairable by you.** Not by her either, she would add, and she was the best I ever saw. A thing is repairable when you can see how it comes apart and it is made of what is still made. Most of a truck is neither.

**The correct relationship to a machine is maintenance, not heroics.** Oil changed, filter clean, water out of the fuel, grease where the grease goes, on the day it is due and not the day it fails. Every dramatic repair she ever did was somebody else's missed maintenance, and she counted the drama against the owner.

**And a bicycle you can fix will outlast a truck you cannot.** **Rule 64.** You can see all of a bicycle; every part is a tube, a bearing, a cable or a chain; it needs no fuel, which deletes the first half of this chapter; it carries several times what you can on foot; and it breaks where hand tools reach. I have owned one truck and eleven bicycles, and it was the truck that let me down.

## What It Costs You

Maintenance costs you your good days. All of it is done when nothing is wrong, so every hour feels like an hour you did not need to spend, and you only find out it was necessary by not finding out. Hard to keep up, and most people stop.

Storing fuel properly costs you convenience in the moments you least want to pay it. Full, sealed, cool and dark means buried, far off and in the wrong size of container, and decanting outdoors at night in the rain is miserable with a good shed ten feet away. Do it in the rain. The shed is where the vapour collects, and you will not see it collect.

And the honest one: this chapter will not get a dead truck running. It will tell you inside an hour whether the truck is worth your next month, and stop you spending that month on something whose tires went before you were born.

## The Short Version

- Fuel is the part with the clock on it. Assume roughly three to six months for gasoline in a sealed can, about a year for clean diesel, anything older a maybe.
- Store it full, sealed, cool and dark: full because air brings water and evaporation, sealed and cool because heat and a tired gasket let it go, dark because sun wrecks cans.
- Never pour, drain or decant fuel indoors, in a shed or over a pit. The vapour is heavier than air, pools unseen and finds any flame in the building. Outdoors, moving air.
- Fuel flowing builds static. Can on bare ground, never in a vehicle, nozzle touching the neck, pour slow. Never siphon by mouth, never use a drinking container.
- Rule 38: check the stupid thing first. Fuel in it, tap open, switch on, kill switch off, battery connected, leads on, out of gear. Usually it is one of them.
- Then fuel, spark, air, compression, in that order, changing one thing at a time. Old fuel and a blocked air filter are the commonest faults on anything stood for years.
- All three present and it still will not run means compression, and you stop: a workshop, and parts nobody makes. That is information, not failure.
- Tires are the real limit. Rubber cracks standing still and nobody can make one, so keep them shaded and at pressure, flex-test what you find, count them first.
- Rule 64: a bicycle you can fix beats a truck you cannot. Maintenance on the day it is due beats any repair, and dramatic repairs are almost always missed maintenance.
