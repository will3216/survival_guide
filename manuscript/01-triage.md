# Chapter 1: Triage — Decide What You Are

> Rule 4: Count before you move.

My glasses went off the counter in the night. I have worn glasses since I was nine, so on the first morning the job in front of me was plain enough: find the glasses. I got down on the floor at the skirting board under the window and went left to right in squares about the size of a tea tray, finishing each square before I started the next one. That is how you find a thing on a floor. Sweeping your hands about is no good — you do the same foot of boards three times and tell yourself you have done a yard.

There was glass down there with the dust, and both palms opened up on it. I did not notice until an hour later, when I went to hold a cup and the cup went on the floor as well. Blood on a cup handle is slippier than you would think.

Edda Mull lived two doors along the landing. She was eighty-one. She came past my door twice, and the second time she stopped in it and told me that my bath was the largest container I owned and I should be filling it. She had filled hers already. She had filled hers before she looked out of the window, which was the clever thing anybody did that morning and it was hers. "Fill it now," she said. "It stops later." Then she went back to her own door and started on the buckets.

It did stop later. By the time I got to the taps I had four inches of brown water and a long noise out of the pipe. Edda had a full tub, two buckets and a kettle.

Edda was dead before the spring, of being eighty-one and of nothing being made any more. I had the glasses by then. I am wearing them now, writing this. The left lens is still cracked across and nobody makes lenses, so that is how the world looks to me and has for a long time, and you get used to the line.

You will get an hour like mine. Everybody does. What I did not have that morning, and have had since, is the order to do things in. That is this chapter.

## The Order to Think In

There is an old mnemonic and it goes like this. Three minutes without air. Three hours without shelter, in cold or heat that is genuinely trying to kill you. Three days without water. Three weeks without food.

Learn it. Then understand what you have learned, because people get it wrong in both directions.

Those four numbers are not a schedule. They are not a guarantee and nobody promised them to you. They are a way of remembering an order. The order is reliable. The numbers are a bit of rope handed to you so you can hold on to the order while frightened, and that is the whole of their job.

The real figures depend on your body, the temperature, the wind, whether you are wet, how hard you are working, how much blood is still in you and how old you are. Several of them are optimistic in the wrong conditions. Cold and wet and windy can finish a healthy adult in well under three hours, and the people it finishes are generally the ones who were sure they had three hours. Heat and hard work can wreck you in well under three days. Food runs longest and its number misleads the most, because you will be stupid and clumsy and bad-tempered a long time before you are starving, and you will be making your decisions in that state.

So use the order, not the clock.

1. **Air.** Can you breathe? Can the person in front of you breathe?
2. **Temperature.** Is the air you are standing in taking heat out of you faster than you can make it, or putting it in faster than you can shed it?
3. **Water.** Have you got any, and do you know where the next of it is?
4. **Food.** Later. It is almost always later.

What the mnemonic is really for is settling arguments, and not with other people. You will stand in a wrecked room with four problems shouting at you and no way to rank them. That is the ranking. The loudest problem is usually food, because hunger talks constantly and suffocation does not talk at all. Hunger is fourth. It has been fourth every time.

## Panic Is Something That Happens to Your Hands

Panic does not look the way it looks in stories. Hardly anybody screams. What happens is that your body gets you ready for a fight with an animal, which is a fine preparation for a fight with an animal and a poor one for a problem that wants thinking about.

Here is what you lose, and you lose it without noticing, which is the part that costs. Your field of view narrows until you are looking down a pipe. Your hearing goes choosy and you will swear afterwards that nobody spoke to you. Fine motor control goes first and worst, so buckles and knots and latches turn genuinely difficult. Your sense of time comes apart. And the real injury: you stop being able to hold two things in your head at once, so you stop being able to compare them, so you stop choosing.

Panic rarely looks like doing nothing. Mostly it looks like doing one thing with tremendous commitment.

You cannot talk yourself out of this and you should stop expecting to. It is a chemical event and it does not care that it is unhelpful. What it does respect is sequence. So do not try to become calm. Borrow order from outside yourself and run it the way a machine runs.

1. **Stop moving.** Put one hand flat on something solid. A wall, a floor, a vehicle. Something that is not going anywhere.
2. **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Five times. Not big heroic breaths — the long slow push out is the part doing the work.
3. **Say out loud what you are about to do.** Out loud, in words, even alone. Especially alone.
4. **Do that one thing and finish it.** One. Small enough that finishing is certain.
5. **Then ask the three questions below.**

The task in step four does not have to matter. Useful is better, but its actual job is to be a thing with an ending. Tie your laces. Shut the window. Count the people. Put the lid on the bottle. Finishing something hands you back the ability to sequence, and sequence is the faculty you lost. It is the same reason every trade that works under pressure writes its procedures down beforehand and reads them off a card at the time. Those cards are not for fools. They are for good workers who know what their own heads do at the wrong moment.

I asked Edda once how she thought of the bath. She said she had to do something with her hands or she was going to start screaming, and the bath was the nearest big thing. She picked the largest useful job within reach. That is available to anybody.

## The Three Questions

Once you can sequence again, everything else in this book hangs off three questions, asked in this order, every time.

**Am I bleeding?**

This comes before air, and I am putting it there on purpose. A serious bleed is a matter of minutes, and it is one of the very few catastrophes you can fix with your own hands and your own weight and nothing else. So: look. Do not only feel for it — look, and then run your hands over yourself, because badly hurt people routinely do not know they are hurt and a bad morning is a very effective anaesthetic. Dark red spreading on cloth. A boot filling up. A puddle that is yours. Press hard, directly on it, with whatever is already in your hand, and do not keep lifting off to check. Chapter 3 is the long version and you should read it before you need it.

**Can I breathe where I am standing?**

Not *am I breathing*. You are, or you would not be reading. The question is whether the air you are in is quietly doing you harm, because the air that kills indoors is usually invisible and usually has no smell worth the name. Smoke you can see and will respect. Fumes off anything burning in an enclosed space, anything running in an enclosed space, a cellar, a stairwell, a shaft, a room that has been shut a long time — those you cannot. A thickening headache, sickness, and a strange sleepiness that arrived indoors and got worse the longer you stayed: get out into open air and stay out. Take everyone with you. Do not go back in for a thing, and do not go down after somebody who went down before you. A great many of the dead in enclosed spaces are the second person in, and some of them are the third.

**Does anyone know where I am?**

This question decides whether you move, which is the whole of the next chapter. Answer it honestly. If people who care about you know roughly where you are, being findable is worth a great deal and staying put is usually right. If nobody knows — if you are somewhere you told no one about — then nobody is coming, and you are not waiting for a rescue, you are the rescue. Those are two different days and you want to know which one you are having before you spend any of it.

After that, every time anything changes, ask all three again. They cost about ten seconds.

## Count Before You Move

Here is Rule 4, and here is where the glasses were.

Before you leave the spot you are standing on, count. Not look around. Count, in a fixed order, out loud if you can stand the sound of it.

Count yourself first. Hands, arms, legs, head, then every pocket you own, by touch, one at a time. Then count what is hung on you: bag, belt, boots, bottle. Then count the room: exits, people, the one thing you came in here for. Then move.

Counting works for two reasons and both are worth having. The first is that it is a task with an ending, so it does for your panic what tying a lace does. The second is duller and more useful: a sweep misses things and a count does not, because a count has an order and the order tells you when you are finished. I have watched a man search the same vehicle four times for a tool that was in his hand. I have watched a woman take a headcount by looking at faces and get the wrong total twice, because she was looking at who she expected to see.

My glasses were in my breast pocket. I had taken them off to lie down, folded them, put them in my shirt, and then gone over every inch of floor in that flat and not one inch of myself. The crack happened in the pocket, I expect, when I knelt on it.

Count yourself first. There is no second lesson in this chapter.

## What It Costs You

Triage means leaving a real problem alone on purpose while it gets worse. That is the price and it does not come down with practice. You will walk out of a room with somebody's blood on the floor because the air in that room was finishing both of you, and the order will have been right, and you will still be having the argument with yourself about it later. Whatever you rank, everything below the rank is what ranking costs.

Counting costs seconds, and once in a long while the seconds are the thing you have not got. If the building is actively coming apart, get out and count outside. The order in this chapter assumes you have a few minutes of choice. Masonry does not negotiate and is not on the staircase.

The questions cost you comfort, because sometimes the answer is one you did not want. Nobody knows where I am. Nobody is coming. That is a worse morning than not knowing, and it is still better, because the person who knows nobody is coming starts walking a day earlier than the person who hopes.

And standing still while other people run will get you called cold, sometimes by somebody whose day you just arranged. Let them. It is a small bill and it is the cheapest thing in this chapter.

## The Short Version

- Air, then shelter in hostile cold or heat, then water, then food. That is the order, not a timetable.
- The three-minutes-to-three-weeks numbers are a mnemonic only. Wet, wind, heat and hard work all make them shorter.
- Panic looks like doing one wrong thing with total commitment. Assume you are doing it.
- Breathe out longer than you breathe in, five times, then finish one small task you chose on purpose.
- Ask, in order: am I bleeding, can I breathe where I am standing, does anyone know where I am.
- Bleeding outranks everything. Press hard, directly on it, with whatever is already in your hand, and keep pressing.
- A headache with sickness and sleepiness that started indoors means get outside and stay outside.
- Rule 4: count before you move, and count yourself first.
