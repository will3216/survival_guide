# Chapter 1: Triage — Decide What You Are

> Rule 4: Count before you move.

Edda Mull lived two doors along the landing from me, and on the first morning she was already hauling water up the stairs in a plastic crate while I was on my hands and knees in broken glass, looking for my glasses.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the only credential I have. I spent the first hour of the new world patting a floor. Not thinking. Not deciding. Patting. I had worn glasses since I was nine, and the sentence in my head was not *I may die today*, it was *I cannot see properly* — and from the inside those two sentences feel identical. That is the first thing you need to know about your own head. It will hand you the loudest problem and tell you it is the biggest one.

Edda came past my door twice. The second time she stopped in it and said that my bath was the largest container I owned and I should be filling it. She had already filled hers. She had filled hers before she looked out of the window, which I have thought about for forty years. She said, "Fill it now, it stops later," and went back to her own door and started on the buckets.

I did not fill it. I found a shard of mirror and a chair leg and I went over that floor like I was being paid by the square foot. When I finally got to the taps they gave me about four inches of brown water and then a long noise like something being disappointed in me. Edda had a full tub, two buckets and a kettle. I had four inches and, eventually, my glasses, cracked across the left lens. Where I found them is the last section of this chapter. That is where it belongs, because the where is the technique.

Edda was dead before the spring. Nothing in this chapter would have changed that — she was eighty-one, and the world had stopped making the things that kept her alive.

You will get an hour like mine. Everyone does. The job of this chapter is to make yours shorter.

## The Order to Think In

There is an old mnemonic that goes like this. Three minutes without air. Three hours without shelter, in cold or heat that is genuinely trying to kill you. Three days without water. Three weeks without food.

Learn it. Then understand what you have learned, because people get this wrong in both directions and the wrong ones die of it.

Those four numbers are not a schedule. They are not a guarantee and they are not a promise made to you by anyone. They are a way of remembering an order. The order is reliable. The numbers are a bit of rope you are being handed so you can remember the order while frightened, and that is all they are.

The real figures depend on your body, the temperature, the wind, whether you are wet, how hard you are working, how much blood is still in you, and how old you are. Several of them are optimistic in the wrong conditions. Cold and wet and windy can finish a healthy adult in well under three hours, and the people it finishes are usually the ones who were sure they had three hours. Heat and hard work can wreck you in well under three days. Food is the one that runs longest, and the number there is also the most misleading, because you will be stupid and clumsy and bad-tempered long before you are starving, and you will be making your decisions in that state.

So use the order, not the clock:

1. **Air.** Can you breathe? Can the person in front of you breathe?
2. **Temperature.** Is the air you are standing in currently taking heat out of you faster than you can make it, or putting heat in faster than you can shed it?
3. **Water.** Do you have any, and do you know where the next of it is?
4. **Food.** Later. It is almost always later.

What the mnemonic is really for is settling arguments. Not with other people — with yourself. You will stand in a wrecked room with four problems shouting at you and no way to rank them, and this is the ranking. The loudest problem is usually food, because hunger talks constantly and asphyxiation does not talk at all. Hunger is fourth. It has been fourth every single time.

## Panic Is Something That Happens to Your Hands

Panic does not look the way it looks in stories. Almost nobody screams. What actually happens is that your body prepares you for a fight with an animal, which is a fine preparation for a fight with an animal and a catastrophic preparation for a problem that needs to be thought about.

Here is what you lose, and you lose it without noticing, which is the dangerous part. Your field of view narrows until you are looking down a pipe. Your hearing goes selective and you will later swear that someone never spoke to you. Your fine motor control goes first and worst, so buckles and knots and latches become genuinely difficult. Your sense of time comes apart — an hour of patting a floor can feel like four minutes of reasonable searching. And the real injury: you stop being able to hold two things in your head at once, which means you stop being able to compare options, which means you stop choosing.

Panic rarely looks like doing nothing. Mostly it looks like doing one wrong thing with tremendous commitment.

Mine looked like a man thoroughly searching a floor.

You cannot talk yourself out of this, and you should stop expecting to. It is a chemical event and it does not respect being told it is unhelpful. What it does respect is sequence. So you do not try to become calm. You borrow order from outside yourself and run it like a machine runs:

1. **Stop moving.** Put one hand flat on something solid. A wall, a floor, a vehicle. Something that is not going anywhere.
2. **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** Five times. Do not take big heroic breaths; long slow exhales are the part that does the work.
3. **Say out loud what you are about to do.** Out loud, in words, even alone. Especially alone.
4. **Do that one thing, and finish it.** One. Small enough that finishing it is certain.
5. **Then ask the three questions below.**

The task in step four does not have to matter. It is better if it is useful, but its actual job is to be a thing with an ending. Tie your laces. Close the window. Count the people. Put the lid on the bottle. Finishing something, anything, hands you back the ability to sequence, and sequence is the faculty you lost. This is the same reason every trade that works under pressure writes its procedures down beforehand and reads them off a card at the time. Those cards are not for idiots. They are for competent people who know exactly what their own heads do at the wrong moment.

Edda filling a bathtub before she looked out of the window was not foresight. I asked her once. She said she had to do something with her hands or she was going to start screaming, and the bath was the nearest thing. She chose the biggest useful task within reach. That is luck arranged into skill, and it is available to you.

## The Three Questions

Once you can sequence again, everything that follows in this book hangs off three questions, asked in this order, every time.

**Am I bleeding?**

This comes before air, and I want to be clear that I am putting it there on purpose and against the order you may have been taught. A serious bleed is a matter of minutes and it is one of the very few catastrophes you can fix with nothing but your own hands and your whole weight. So: look. Do not feel for it — look, and run your hands over yourself afterwards, because badly injured people routinely do not know they are injured and the shock of the moment is a very effective anaesthetic. Dark red spreading on cloth, a boot filling up, a puddle that is yours. Press hard, directly on it, with whatever is already in your hand, and do not keep lifting it off to check. Chapter 3 is the long version and you should read it before you need it.

**Can I breathe where I am standing?**

Not *am I breathing*. You are, or you would not be reading. The question is whether the air you are in is slowly doing you harm, because the air that kills indoors is usually invisible and almost always odourless enough to miss. Smoke you can see and will respect. Fumes from anything burning in an enclosed space, anything running in an enclosed space, a cellar, a stairwell, a shaft, a long-closed room — those you cannot see. If you have a thickening headache, nausea, and a strange sleepiness that arrived indoors and got worse the longer you stayed, get outside into open air and stay outside. Take everyone with you. Do not go back in for a thing, and do not go down after someone who went down before you; a great many of the dead in enclosed spaces are the second person in, and some of them are the third.

**Does anyone know where I am?**

This question decides whether you move, which is the whole of the next chapter. Answer it honestly. If people who care about you know roughly where you are, then being findable is worth a great deal and staying put is usually right. If nobody knows — if you are somewhere you told no one about — then nobody is coming, and you are not waiting for a rescue, you are the rescue. Those are two different days and you need to know which one you are having before you spend any of it.

From then on, every time anything changes, ask all three again. They cost about ten seconds. I have never regretted the ten seconds.

## Count Before You Move

Here is Rule 4, and here is where I found my glasses.

Before you leave the spot you are standing on, count. Not look around — count, in a fixed order, out loud if you can stand to.

Count yourself first. Hands, arms, legs, head, then every pocket you own, by touch, one at a time. Then count what is attached to you: bag, belt, boots, bottle. Then count the room: exits, people, the one thing you came in here for. Then move.

Counting works for two separate reasons and it is worth knowing both. The first is that it is a task with an ending, so it does for your panic what tying a lace does. The second is more boring and more useful: a sweep misses things and a count does not, because a count has an order and the order tells you when you are done. I have watched a man search the same vehicle four times for a tool that was in his own hand. I have watched a woman do a headcount by looking at faces and get the wrong total twice, because she was looking at who she expected.

My glasses were in my breast pocket. I had taken them off to lie down, folded them, put them in my shirt, and then spent fifty-one minutes examining every inch of floor in that flat and not one inch of myself.

Count yourself first. There is no second lesson in this chapter.

## What It Costs You

Triage means deliberately leaving a real problem alone while it gets worse, and that does not get easier with practice. You will walk out of a room with someone's blood on the floor because the air in that room was killing you both, and the order was right, and you will still be arguing with yourself about it years later. The cost of ranking is everything below the rank.

Counting costs seconds, and once in a long while the seconds are the thing you do not have. If the structure you are in is actively coming apart, run first and count outside. The order in this chapter assumes you have a few minutes of choice. Masonry does not negotiate and is not on the staircase.

The questions also cost you comfort, because sometimes they return an answer you did not want. Nobody knows where I am. Nobody is coming. That is a worse morning than not knowing, and it is still better, because the person who knows nobody is coming starts walking a day earlier than the person who hopes.

And standing still while others panic will mark you. People remember who was calm, and not always fondly. You will be called cold by somebody whose life you arranged. Let them. It is a small bill and it is the cheapest thing in this chapter.

## The Short Version

- Air, then shelter in hostile cold or heat, then water, then food. That is the order, not a timetable.
- The three-minutes-to-three-weeks numbers are a mnemonic only. Wet, wind, heat and hard work all make them shorter.
- Panic looks like doing one wrong thing with total commitment. Assume you are doing it.
- Breathe out longer than you breathe in, five times, then finish one small task you chose on purpose.
- Ask, in order: am I bleeding, can I breathe where I am standing, does anyone know where I am.
- Bleeding outranks everything. Press hard, directly on it, with whatever is already in your hand, and keep pressing.
- A headache with nausea and sleepiness that started indoors means get outside and stay outside.
- Rule 4: count before you move, and count yourself first.
