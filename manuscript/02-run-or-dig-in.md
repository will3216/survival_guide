# Chapter 2: Run or Dig In

> Rule 11: The second floor is not safer. It is only harder to leave.

The Spoke took me in a year after the first morning, and the first thing they taught me was not a knot or a route. It was a sentence. Halla Dorn said it to me in the doorway of the bag room, with her hand on the frame: *decide by noon, say it out loud, and move while the light is still on your side.*

That was the whole policy of the group that kept me alive. Not when to go, not where — only that the decision got made early, got said in front of other people so it could not be quietly un-made, and got acted on with daylight left over.

I thought they were simple. They kept a room with eleven packed bags in it, weighed, labelled and re-checked every month, and twice a year they walked everybody out of the house and back in again for no reason, in the rain, and I rolled my eyes until my head hurt. Down the valley there was a house of genuinely clever people who talked about this properly. They held their nerve for three days, because leaving would have been premature and they were not fools.

The Spoke left on the second morning, on foot, at eight, bored, complaining about the rain.

The Spoke was boring and it was alive. The clever house was neither, and they were not wrong about any single fact. They were wrong about the shape of the decision. They thought it was a question with a correct answer. It is a question with a closing door.

Their plan was written on the kitchen wall in marker, numbered one to nine. We came back through in the spring and read it; it was a good plan to about step four.

Chapter 1 left you holding the third question — *does anyone know where I am?* — and told you it decides whether you move. This is that chapter.

## Staying Still Is Also a Decision

Both failures are common and people are loyal to one or the other. Some run from everything, burn their reserves on the first bad night and arrive somewhere worse with nothing left. Some sit in a house that is filling with water and call it being level-headed. No personality type gets this right. There is only a short list of conditions, checked honestly, as often as things change.

**Stay where you are when all of these are true:**

1. **The structure is sound.** No fire in it, no smoke in it, no visible lean, no cracking you can hear, nothing standing where the floor is not. If you cannot tell, that is not a yes.
2. **You have water, or you have a certain way to get some.** Not food. Water.
3. **The hazard is outside and passing.** Weather, smoke on the wind, people moving through, anything that is a front rather than a flood. Things that pass are things to be behind a wall from.
4. **Staying does not cut you off.** A route out exists now and will still exist later.

Staying is usually right, and I want to say that plainly before I spend the rest of the chapter on leaving. A sound building with water in it is a better machine than you are: warmer, drier, it hides you, it holds your things, and it means a person looking for you has somewhere to look. If the answer to *does anyone know where I am?* is yes, staying put is worth more than most things you could do instead.

**Go when any one of these is true:**

1. **The structure is compromised.** Fire, smoke, water coming in, gas you can smell, anything leaning or shifting, anything above you that is no longer supported.
2. **The hazard is where you are and getting worse.** Not approaching. Present, and on a trend.
3. **You have no water and no certain way to get any.** This one has a clock on it and the clock is shorter than you think.
4. **The thing keeping you there is sunk cost.** Your possessions are not a reason.

Any one. Not all four. And the honest version: most of the time you will have neither a clean yes nor a clean no, only a bad feeling and a thin reason for ignoring it. That is where the Spoke's sentence does its work. Decide early. Say it out loud to another person. Move with light left.

## The Trap, Which Is Always the Same Trap

Here is the part to tattoo on something. Of the people who die of this decision, most of them did not choose wrong. They chose correctly and did it too late.

Think about what the two mistakes actually cost. Leaving too early costs you a wasted day, a sore back, some fuel, and the particular humiliation of walking home past neighbours who stayed. Leaving too late costs you the thing itself. You are in the open, carrying everything, with no light, on the one route everybody else also just thought of, with the hazard now between you and where you were going. Every advantage you had — walls, the daylight, an empty road, a choice of directions — you spent on waiting.

That is why Halla's rule had a time in it.

Leaving late is the default, not the exception, because the pressure to delay comes from sensible places. Information keeps arriving, and each new piece feels like the one that decides things. Leaving feels irreversible and staying feels changeable later, which is backwards. Nobody wants to be the idiot who ran. Everybody waits to see what everybody else does, so everybody moves at once, which is the traffic. And you will be tired and will tell yourself you will go better in the morning. You will not, and there may not be a route.

So: set your trigger before you need it, in advance, in words, as a condition and not a feeling. *If the water reaches the step. If we can smell it inside. If it is not out by dark. If we have one day of water left.* A condition can be checked by a frightened person. A feeling cannot.

## The Bag You Should Already Own

The bag is not for the apocalypse. The bag exists so that leaving does not require packing first, because packing first is how people end up leaving late. Its real job is removing a reason to delay.

Six things, in this order. Everything else is an opinion.

1. **Water.** As much as you can actually carry, in more than one container, because one container is one failure away from none. A litre weighs a kilogram and there is no way around that; water will be most of your weight and it should be. Published emergency guidance puts household storage at roughly four litres per person per day. You will not carry three days of that. Carry what you can and know where the next of it is, which is chapter 4's whole subject.
2. **Light.** Hands-free if you can, and a spare that does not share a battery type with the first. Light is what turns a night of sitting still into a night of walking.
3. **Warmth.** Not a coat — a coat is what you are wearing. Something dry, in a bag that keeps it dry, plus a hat and a windproof layer. Wet and wind kill people who were not expecting to be killed by anything, and chapter 5 is unkind about it.
4. **Documents.** Who you are, who is yours, what you are owed, who to find. Copy them, and keep the copies somewhere other than with the originals. In the first years this mattered more than anyone expected, and it has never stopped mattering.
5. **Medication.** Everything anyone in your household cannot miss, with the written name of each one, not just the pills. A list of names survives being spilled in a ditch. Rotate them so they are not expired.
6. **A knife.** One, good, and sharp. A knife is the tool that makes other tools.

Then, if there is room and only then: a metal cup you can boil in, cord, tape, two ways to make fire, a small roll of what you use to stop bleeding, and food you do not have to cook. Food is last. Food is always last.

Now the part nobody tells you. Your first bag will be too heavy. Not slightly. Everyone's first bag is too heavy, mine included, because you pack it sitting on the floor next to it, where everything weighs nothing.

The fix is free. Put the bag on and carry it up one flight of stairs. One. Then down. Whatever you took out at the top of those stairs was never going to leave the house with you; you were only going to find that out later, in the dark, on a road. A pack much past a third of your body weight degrades you — slower, clumsier, worse at the things you need to be good at — and a flight of stairs will tell you that about yourself more honestly than any number will.

That is Rule 23: **pack the bag for the stairs, not for the road.** A heavy bag feels entirely survivable while you are still standing in the doorway with it. It stops being survivable later, somewhere you cannot put it down.

## Which Way Out

You have decided to go. Now the second decision, which people make by accident.

**Roads are fast and watched.** This is not a conspiracy, it is geometry: a road is the easy line through hard ground, so everything that wants to move goes along it and everything that wants to meet what is moving waits beside it. Roads give you speed, flat footing, bridges and signs. They also give you crowds at the pinch points — bridges, cuttings, junctions — and that is where the trouble, official and otherwise, sits down to wait. Walk beside rather than on, know your next way off, and never let a bridge be your only plan.

**Water courses navigate themselves.** A stream, a drain, a canal, a river — they run one way, they run downhill, and downhill is where people built things. You cannot get lost following water, which matters more than it sounds, because the exhausted lose themselves constantly and then walk in circles using up the day. Water usually means a path beside it, and a landmark you can find again in the dark.

It costs you, and I will not pretend otherwise. Water takes the long way around. The ground beside it is wet, soft, steep in places and sometimes impassable, and being wet is the thing that kills. Water rises, and a channel is the worst place to stand when it does. Do not wade anything moving that you cannot see the bottom of; moving water is far stronger than it looks and will take your feet out at a depth you would have sworn was nothing.

So: roads when you need speed and can accept being seen. Water when you need not to get lost, and have the time.

**And the second floor is not safer.** This is Rule 11 and the one I have had to repeat most often. When something is coming — water, a crowd, smoke, people with intentions — the instinct is to go up. Up feels defensive. What up actually is, is one exit. A second floor is a room with a staircase, and a staircase is a corridor that someone or something else can also use, or fill, or burn. Water does not get bored and go home; the people who drown in a building mostly drown above the ground floor, having moved up once and found there was no second move. If you go up, know the way down and know a second way out of the upper room, and go up only because you decided to, with a trigger for coming down.

Before you step out of the door, ask the three questions once more, in order. Am I bleeding? Can I breathe where I am standing? Does anyone know where I am? If nobody does, tell somebody now — in writing, on the door, in a hand you can read: who you are, how many of you, when you left, and where you are headed. It takes a minute to write. Write it.

## What It Costs You

Deciding early means being wrong in public, repeatedly, at no small expense. You will walk out of a sound house on a bad guess, come back in two days and find that nothing happened, having spent water, daylight and credibility on it. Do it anyway. That is the premium, and the insurance only has to pay out once.

The bag costs you honesty about your possessions. You will look at the six things and understand that the list has no space on it for the photographs. Take one small thing, small enough that it does not change the weight, and leave the rest knowing you are leaving it. People who refuse this trade do not pack lighter. They leave later.

Routes cost you in opposite currencies and you cannot have both: fast means watched, and unwatched means slow, wet and longer than any map suggests. Choose on purpose and do not change your mind halfway, which is how people end up on neither.

And the trigger costs you the comfort of keeping your options open. Once you have said *if it is not out by dark, we go*, you have to go at dark, in the dark, when staying would feel far more reasonable. Write it anyway. Your frightened self is not a better judge than your calm one. It is only louder.

## The Short Version

- Decide early, say it out loud to someone, and move with daylight left.
- Stay if the structure is sound, you have water, and the hazard is outside and passing.
- Go if the structure is compromised, the hazard is where you are and worsening, or you have no water.
- Most people who die of this decision chose correctly and left too late.
- Set the trigger in advance as a condition, not a feeling: *if the water reaches the step, we go.*
- The bag is six things: water, light, warmth, documents, medication, one good knife. Food is last.
- Rule 23: pack the bag for the stairs, not for the road. Carry it up one flight before you believe it.
- Roads are fast and watched; water courses navigate themselves but cost you time and dryness.
- Rule 11: the second floor is not safer, it is only harder to leave. Going up means knowing the way down first.
