# Chapter 2: Run or Dig In

> Rule 11: The second floor is not safer. It is only harder to leave.

A year after the first morning a group called the Spoke took me in, and the only reason I heard Halla Dorn say her sentence is that I was standing in the doorway of the bag room with a bag in each hand and she wanted past me. She said: decide by noon, say it out loud, and move while the light is still on your side. Then she went by me into the room.

The left bag was the problem. Somebody had packed it high and soft — dry clothing at the bottom, water at the top, which is upside down — so the weight sat above my shoulder instead of behind it and the whole thing rolled off me every time I turned my head. I turned my head a good deal that morning, because people kept speaking to me from the stairs. I repacked it in the yard, water at the bottom, pressed close to the back where it could not swing. A bag that stays still weighs less than a bag that does not, and it was the same bag.

> **— T.** I was taught that sentence before I was taught to read. I have said it over a sick child, twice, and once in a bad week to nobody at all. Here is the manuscript's only account of where it comes from, and it is wedged between the observation that he was in the way and a complaint about a strap. We still walk our own house out and back twice a year, as his people did. It still works and I have never once enjoyed it.

I thought the Spoke were simple people. They kept a room with eleven packed bags in it, weighed, labelled and checked over every month, and twice a year they walked everybody out of the house and back in again for no reason at all, in the rain. I rolled my eyes until my head hurt. Down the valley was a house of cleverer people who talked the decision through properly and held their nerve for three days.

The Spoke left on the second morning, on foot, at eight, bored, complaining about the rain.

Chapter 1 leaves you holding the third question. *Does anyone know where I am?* This is the chapter where you move, or do not.

## Staying Still Is Also a Decision

Both failures are common and people are loyal to one or the other. Some run from everything, burn their reserves on the first bad night and arrive somewhere worse with nothing left. Some sit in a house filling with water and call it being level-headed. Nobody gets this right by temperament. There is only a short list of conditions, checked honestly, as often as things change.

**Stay where you are when all of these are true:**

1. **The structure is sound.** No fire, no smoke, no visible lean, no cracking you can hear, nothing standing where the floor is not. If you cannot tell, that is not a yes.
2. **You have water, or a certain way to get some.** Not food. Water.
3. **The hazard is outside and passing.** Weather, smoke on the wind, people moving through — a front rather than a flood. Things that pass are things to be behind a wall from.
4. **Staying does not cut you off.** A route out exists now and will exist later.

Staying is usually right, and I want that said plainly before I spend the rest of the chapter on leaving. A sound building with water in it is a better machine than you are: warmer, drier, it hides you, it holds your things, and it gives a person looking for you somewhere to look. So ask it again here: *does anyone know where I am?* If the answer is yes, staying put is worth more than nearly anything else you could do with the day.

**Go when any one of these is true:**

1. **The structure is compromised.** Fire, smoke, water coming in, gas you can smell, anything leaning, anything above you that is no longer held up.
2. **The hazard is where you are and getting worse.** Not approaching. Present, and on a trend.
3. **You have no water and no certain way to get any.** This one has a clock on it and the clock is shorter than you think.
4. **The thing keeping you there is what you own.** Your possessions are not a reason.

Any one of them. Not all four. And the honest version is that most of the time you will have neither a clean yes nor a clean no, only a bad feeling and a thin reason for ignoring it. That is where the Spoke's sentence does its work.

## The Trap, Which Is Always the Same Trap

Of the people who die of this decision, most did not choose wrong. They chose correctly and did it too late.

The two mistakes are not the same size. Leaving too early costs you a wasted day, a sore back and the embarrassment of walking home past neighbours who stayed. Leaving too late costs you the thing itself. You are in the open, carrying everything, with no light, on the one route everybody else also just thought of, with the hazard now between you and where you were going. Walls, daylight, an empty road, a choice of directions — every advantage you had, you spent on waiting.

That is why Halla's sentence had a time in it.

Leaving late is the usual outcome, not the exception, because the pressure to delay comes from sensible places. Information keeps arriving and each new piece feels like the one that settles it. Leaving feels permanent and staying feels like something you can change later, which is backwards. Nobody wants to be the idiot who ran. Everybody waits to see what everybody else does, so everybody goes at once, which is the crowd. And you will be tired and will tell yourself you will travel better in the morning. You will not, and there may not be a route by then.

So set your trigger before you need it, in advance, in words, as a condition and not a feeling. *If the water reaches the step. If we can smell it inside. If it is not out by dark. If we are down to one day of water.* A condition can be checked by a frightened person. A feeling cannot.

We came back through the valley in the spring. The clever house had their plan on the kitchen wall in marker, numbered one to nine, and it was a good plan to about step four.

> **— T.** He reads a dead household's plan off their own wall and the one remark he thought worth the ink is a mark out of nine. Nothing about the house. Nothing about the three days. He is right about the plan. No. No, that is not the takeaway, and I have no better way to say it than that. I copied this passage out twice and sat with it and I still do not know what to tell you about the man.

## The Bag You Should Already Own

The bag is not for the end of the world. It exists so that leaving does not require packing first, because packing first is how people leave late. Its job is to remove a reason to delay.

Six things, in this order. Everything after them is an opinion.

1. **Water.** As much as you can actually carry, in more than one container, because one container is one failure away from none. A litre weighs a kilogram and there is no arrangement to be made about that; water will be most of your weight and it should be. Published emergency guidance puts household storage at roughly four litres per person per day. Three days of that is not a thing a person carries, and I am not going to hand you a number for what you can. Carry what you can and know where the next of it is, which is chapter 4's whole subject.
2. **Light.** Hands-free if you can, and a spare that does not take the same batteries as the first. Light turns a night of sitting still into a night of walking.
3. **Warmth.** Not a coat — a coat is what you are wearing. Something dry, in a bag that keeps it dry, plus a hat and a windproof layer. Wet and wind kill people who were not expecting to be killed by anything, and chapter 5 is unkind about it.
4. **Documents.** Who you are, who is yours, what you are owed, who to find. Copy them and keep the copies somewhere other than with the originals.
5. **Medication.** Everything anybody in the house cannot go without, with the written name of each one and not only the pills, because a list of names survives being spilled in a ditch. Rotate it so it is not out of date.
6. **A knife.** One, good, and sharp. A knife is the tool that makes other tools.

Then, if there is room and only then: a metal cup you can boil in, cord, tape, two ways to make fire, a small roll of what you use to stop bleeding, and food you do not have to cook. Food is always last.

Your first bag will be too heavy. Not slightly. Everyone's first bag is too heavy, mine very much included, because you pack it sitting on the floor beside it, where everything weighs nothing.

The fix is free. Put the bag on and carry it up one flight of stairs, then down. Whatever you took out at the top was never going to leave the house with you; you were only going to find that out later, in the dark, on a road. A load much past a third of what you weigh degrades you — slower, clumsier, worse at everything you need to be good at — and one flight of stairs tells you that more honestly than any number will.

That is Rule 23: **pack the bag for the stairs, not for the road.** A heavy bag feels entirely survivable while you are still standing in the doorway with it. It stops being survivable somewhere you cannot put it down.

## Which Way Out

You have decided to go. Now the second decision, which people mostly make by accident.

**Roads are fast and watched.** That is not a conspiracy, it is geometry: a road is the easy line through hard ground, so everything that wants to move goes along it and everything that wants to meet what is moving waits beside it. Roads give you speed, flat footing and bridges. They also give you crowds at the pinch points — bridges, cuttings, junctions — and that is where trouble of every sort sits down to wait. Walk beside rather than on, know your next way off, and never let a bridge be your only plan.

**Water courses navigate themselves.** A stream, a drain, a canal, a river. They run one way, they run downhill, and downhill is where people built things. You cannot get lost following water, which matters more than it sounds, because tired people lose themselves and then walk in circles through the daylight they had. Water usually comes with a path beside it and a landmark you can find again in the dark.

It costs. Water takes the long way round, the ground beside it is wet and steep in places, and being wet is the thing that kills. Water rises, and a channel is the worst place to stand when it does. Do not wade anything moving whose bottom you cannot see; moving water is far stronger than it looks and will take your feet out at a depth you would have sworn was nothing.

**And the second floor is not safer.** This is Rule 11, and the one I have had to say most often. When something is coming — water, a crowd, smoke, people with intentions — the instinct is to go up, because up feels defensive. What up actually is, is one exit. A second floor is a room with a staircase, and a staircase is a corridor that something else can also use, or fill, or burn. The people who drown in a building mostly drown above the ground floor, having moved up once and found there was no second move. If you go up, know the way down, know a second way out of the upper room, and have a condition for coming down again.

Before you step out of the door, ask the three questions once more, in order. Am I bleeding? Can I breathe where I am standing? Does anyone know where I am? If nobody does, tell somebody now — in writing, on the door, in a hand that can be read: who you are, how many of you, when you left, where you are headed. It takes a minute to write. Write it.

## What It Costs You

Deciding early means being wrong in public, repeatedly, at your own expense. You will walk out of a sound house on a bad guess, come back in two days and find that nothing happened, having spent water, daylight and credibility on it. Do it anyway. The insurance only has to pay out once.

The bag costs you honesty about what you own. The six things leave no space on the list for the photographs. Take one small thing, small enough that it does not change the weight, and leave the rest knowing you are leaving it. People who refuse that trade do not pack lighter. They leave later.

And the trigger costs you the comfort of keeping your options open. Once you have said *if it is not out by dark, we go*, you have to go at dark, in the dark, when staying would feel far more reasonable. Your frightened self is not a better judge than your calm one. It is louder.

## The Short Version

- Decide early, say it out loud to someone, and move with daylight left.
- Stay if the structure is sound, you have water, and the hazard is outside and passing.
- Go if the structure is compromised, the hazard is where you are and worsening, or you have no water.
- Most who die of this decision chose correctly and left too late.
- Set the trigger in advance as a condition, not a feeling: *if the water reaches the step, we go.*
- The bag is six things: water, light, warmth, documents, medication, one good knife. Food is last.
- Pack the water low and against your back. Rule 23: pack the bag for the stairs, not the road — carry it up one flight before you believe it.
- Roads are fast and watched; water courses navigate themselves but cost time and dryness.
- Rule 11: the second floor is not safer, it is only harder to leave. Go up only knowing the way down.
