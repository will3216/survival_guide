# Chapter 3: The Body Is Your First Vehicle

> Rule 7: Pressure is a place, not a motion.

I put my right arm through a window in my third year, getting hold of somebody on the wrong side of it, and the glass opened the forearm from wrist to halfway to the elbow. That part was straightforward. What I did next is why this chapter exists.

I had a folded cap in my other hand, so I put the cap on the arm and leaned on it, which was correct. Then I lifted the cap off to see whether it had stopped. It had not. I put it back, counted to about ten, lifted it off again. Twenty minutes of that in a doorway, and the arm was still going at the end, because I had taken the only thing that was working off it five or six times to look.

A woman from the Ninth Hand knelt, took my wrist, moved my hand two inches to the left onto one spot, put her weight on it and held it there without saying anything for a long while. That is the whole of Rule 7 and I have nothing to add. The thumb on that hand has not bent properly since, which is the window's doing and not hers.

Now, before the rest of it. I have been more careful with this chapter than with anything else in the book, and would rather say so than have you guess it. I took it to Isak Roon, who mends nets down at the water and has better hands than mine, and asked him to read it as a man who has had to stop bleeding in a hurry. He kept it eight days and gave me four corrections. All four are in. If I am wrong elsewhere in this book it costs you an afternoon. Here it does not, so I got checked.

> **— T.** The great authority on staying alive wrote down how to stop a man bleeding, then carried the pages to the water, handed them to a man who mends nets, and did as he was told. It is the eight days that make me trust this chapter. And so nobody is in doubt: I was taught all of this, in this order, nearly word for word, by people who did not know they were quoting a book. It is correct. Do as it says.

## Am I Bleeding?

Chapter 1 put that question first on purpose, ahead of air. Here is the long version.

A serious bleed is one of the few disasters in this book you fix with your hands and your weight and nothing else. It is also quick. So it goes first — before cleaning, before a bandage, before moving them.

Look as well as feel, and look under things: dark red spreading through cloth, a boot or sleeve filling, a pool nobody can account for. A badly hurt person often does not know where they are hurt.

**Direct pressure, done properly.**

1. **Find the spot.** The exact place it is coming from, not the general area. An inch away is nowhere.
2. **Put something between your hand and it** if a cloth, dressing or shirt is within reach. If not, your bare hand — hunting for cloth is not worth the time.
3. **Heel of the hand on that one spot, second hand stacked on top.** Lock your arms, shoulders over them, so the work is your weight and not your muscles. Muscles give out; weight does not.
4. **Press hard.** Harder than you want to. Hard enough that you believe you are hurting them, because you are, and that is no reason to ease off.
5. **Do not move.** Not your hand, not your weight, not the spot.
6. **Hold it ten minutes at least, by something you can count.** Longer if it is bad.
7. **Do not lift off to look.** Not once. A clot takes minutes to become anything and every look sets it back to the start. If blood comes through, add cloth on top and press again, same place, harder. What is on there stays on.
8. **If you must hand over,** the next pair of hands goes on top of yours before yours come out. The spot never goes unpressed.

That is enough for most of what you will meet. The pressing is the treatment.

**A neck is pressure and only pressure.** Press the one side it is coming from, fingers or the heel of your hand, and never both sides at once: both sides at once stops the blood to the brain. Do not pack it and do not put anything round it — a wrap on a neck is not a dressing, it is a strangle. If the breathing starts going, let them sit up and find the way that works, and keep pressing while they do. A neck swelling shut is not something hands can open, and it is still not a reason to lift your hand.

**Packing a deep wound.** If it is a hole rather than a slice, in an arm or a leg or where one joins the body — thigh, buttock, shoulder, armpit, groin — pressing the outside may not reach what is bleeding. So you pack it. Nowhere else.

1. Push cloth or dressing material **into** the wound, with a finger, down to where it is coming from.
2. Keep feeding more in, firmly, until the wound is full and tight. Most people stop far short of what they should use.
3. Then both hands on top and press as above, a good three minutes without moving before wondering whether it worked. Three minutes is the earliest you may look at a packed wound, not leave to stop pressing at three.
4. Leave the packing in. Whatever happens next, it stays.

Never pack a wound in the chest, the belly, the head or an eye. There is no bottom to press against and you will do harm. The belly and the head are the last heading in this chapter. The chest you can do a little for.

**An open chest wound.** Cover it with cloth, not plastic. Cloth is the point: a cover that seals the hole can make the breathing worse instead of better, and cloth will not seal. Do not pack it. Let them sit or lean whichever way they breathe best, usually sitting up, and never flat to tidy them — and if they start going into shock, they still sit where they breathe. Then watch the breathing against the cover: faster, shallower, more frightened or greyer **after the cover went on**, lift a corner. If lifting helps, leave it loose. A cover you have lifted twice should stay off.

## The Tourniquet, and the Part People Get Wrong

A tourniquet is for an arm or a leg bleeding faster than pressure will hold, or a limb you cannot keep your hands on because you have to move, or because two are hurt and there is one of you. It is for limbs: not a neck, not a shoulder, not a trunk, and there is no version of it that is. Chapter 2's small roll of what you use to stop bleeding is what this is for, and a made tourniquet with a windlass beats anything improvised. Improvise only if you must: a wide strap, never cord or wire, and a rod to twist it with.

1. **High and tight on the limb, between the wound and the body.** A few inches above the wound is right. Higher is acceptable. Too low is not.
2. **Never over a joint.** Not the knee, not the elbow. If the wound is at or near one, go above it.
3. **Tighten until the bleeding stops.** That is the only measure there is — not until it looks tight, not until they tell you to stop. It will hurt a great deal. That is expected and is not a reason to back it off a turn.
4. **Secure the windlass** so it cannot unwind.
5. **Write the time on.** On the strap, on their skin, on a label, in the dirt beside them. The hour and the minute it went on, immediately — you will not remember later, and the next person needs it above anything else.
6. **If it has not stopped, do not fiddle with it.** Put a second one on above the first and tighten that one the same way.
7. **Then leave it alone,** covered and where it can be seen.

And the part people get wrong, which Isak made me say twice: **you do not loosen it.** Not to check, not to rest the limb, not because they are asking you to — and they will ask. Whoever put a tourniquet on is not the person who takes it off. That is a job for somebody with more than hands, and if there is none it stays on. Everybody who loosens one is being kind, and kindness is not the question being asked.

## Then Air

Chapter 1's second question — *can I breathe where I am standing?* — is about the room. This is about the person. If they are awake and talking, the airway is open. If they are not awake:

1. **Clear what you can see and reach out of the mouth.** Only what you can see and reach. Do not go fishing blind.
2. **Tilt the head back and lift the chin.** A hand on the forehead, two fingers under the point of the chin, tip gently back. In an unconscious person on their back the tongue is what blocks the airway and this is what unblocks it. Do it before you decide whether they are breathing — many who look stopped are only folded shut.
3. **Look and listen for ten seconds.** Is the chest moving? Is air going in and out? Normal breathing — not the occasional noisy gasp, which is not it.
4. **If they are breathing, roll them onto their side,** head tilted a little back and down, mouth lowest, and stay with them. Unconscious people on their backs are drowned by their own tongues and by what comes up. Turn them even if you think the neck is broken: an airway outranks a spine, because a broken neck is next year's problem and a blocked airway is the next two minutes'.
5. **If they are not breathing, push on the chest.** Heel of the hand on the centre of the breastbone, other hand on top, arms straight, down hard and fast, letting the chest come all the way back up between pushes. The rate taught is between a hundred and a hundred and twenty a minute, faster than feels decent. Keep going until they breathe, somebody takes over, or you cannot.

It works far less often than anybody wants. Do it anyway.

## Shock, Which Looks Like Being Cold

Shock is the body running out of circulation, and it kills people whose injury you have already dealt with. The signs: skin pale and grey and wet to the touch, cold hands, a weak fast pulse, fast shallow breathing, a thirst they keep mentioning, feeling sick, restless and bothered, then quiet, then confused — a worse sign than the shouting.

1. **Fix the cause first.** Almost always bleeding. Shock is a symptom and you cannot treat it around an untreated bleed.
2. **Lay them flat and keep them still.** On their back if they are awake and breathing well, on their side if not. Do not prop the legs up — it was taught for years, the evidence never arrived, and shifting a hurt person about has a real cost.
3. **Get them off the ground and cover them.** Underneath matters more than on top: the ground takes heat faster than air, so something goes under them even if that means less over. Then cover everything, head included.
4. **Nothing by mouth.** Not water, not tea, not a drop, however much they ask — and they will ask, because thirst is one of the signs. Wet their lips if you must.
5. **Loosen anything tight** that is not a tourniquet.
6. **Stay and keep checking** breathing, colour, whether they still know you, and write down what changes and when.

## Bones

A broken bone is rarely what kills anybody, so it comes last. You will not know whether it is broken and do not need to: treat a bad one as broken.

1. **Splint it in the position you found it.** Do not straighten it, do not pull it into line, do not put a stuck-out bone back in. Whatever shape it is in is the shape you pad and support.
2. **Immobilise the joint above it and the joint below it.** This is the part people miss and the whole of splinting: a splint that only covers the break lets the break move. Lower leg, ankle and knee; forearm, wrist and elbow.
3. **Pad it generously** and make it stiff enough not to bend.
4. **Tie it on above and below the break, never over it.** Firm, not tight.
5. **Then check past the splint.** Can they feel your finger on their toes or fingertips? Can they wiggle them? Is the skin beyond it warm and the normal colour, and does it pink up when you press and let go? Check before you tie, after, and later.
6. **If anything past the splint goes cold, white, blue or numb, loosen it at once.** A splint that cuts off the blood supply costs them the limb, and this is the only thing in this chapter you loosen.
7. **Cover an open break** with the cleanest thing available and treat the bleeding as above, pressing around rather than on it.

My left collarbone sits half an inch high and my right little finger points at the next one along. Both predate point two.

## What I Cannot Fix, and Neither Can You

**Bleeding on the inside.** You cannot press on it. The signs are shock that keeps worsening with no wound to account for it, a belly going rigid and tender, bruising across the trunk, somebody fine for an hour and then not. There is no technique here, and never was one outside a building with a surgeon.

**A head injury that gets worse.** Not the person knocked flat who sits up and complains. The one who seemed all right and then goes downhill — drowsier and drowsier, sick, confused, hard to rouse, one pupil wider than the other, a growing headache. That is pressure building in a box that does not open, and opening it is surgery.

**Anything in the abdomen.** Struck, crushed or punctured. Nothing in there can be pressed on and most of it leaks.

The right answer to all three used to be a telephone and a drive of forty minutes. It is now company. So keep them warm and off the ground and out of the wet, keep somebody's hand on them, nothing by mouth, and do not lie to them, because being lied to frightens people and frightened is worse. Then see to whoever else is hurt, because there usually is somebody else and they are who you can help.

## What It Costs You

This chapter costs you somebody's pain, delivered by you, deliberately, while they ask you to stop. Pressing hard enough hurts. Packing hurts a great deal. There is no gentle version that works, and easing off is treating your own discomfort and calling it mercy.

It costs you time. Ten minutes of not moving your hands, with four jobs shouting, is the longest ten minutes available. People lose bleeds by going to fetch something.

And pressing bare-handed costs you something, because what is in their blood is now on your hands and in whatever cuts you have, and nobody makes the medicines that used to answer for that. Still do not go hunting, but take any waterproof thing within reach — worth two seconds — and wash well afterwards.

And it costs you knowing where the line is. After this you can tell, fairly early, when you have moved from treating somebody to keeping them company — and time spent on what cannot be fixed is time somebody nearby could have used.

## The Short Version

- Severe bleeding first, before air, before anything. Look as well as feel, and look under clothing.
- Direct pressure: one exact spot, heel of the hand, second hand stacked on top, arms locked, your weight not your muscles. Hard, ten minutes at least, never lifting off to look. Blood coming through the cloth means more cloth on top, never cloth taken off.
- **Holes, necks and chests.** A neck is pressure on one side only, never both, nothing wrapped round it. An open chest wound is covered with cloth, never plastic, never packed, sitting however they breathe best even in shock. Pack a deep wound only in an arm, a leg or where one joins the body — right in until full and tight, then both hands on top three minutes at least. Never pack a chest, belly, head, eye or neck.
- Tourniquet for a limb pressure will not hold: wide strap, high and tight above the wound, never over a joint, tightened until bleeding stops. Write the time on. Never loosen it yourself.
- Unconscious: clear only what you can see in the mouth, tilt the head back and lift the chin, then look for normal breathing. Breathing, roll them onto their side and stay — turn them even if you think the neck is broken. Not breathing, push hard and fast on the centre of the chest, a hundred to a hundred and twenty a minute.
- Shock — grey, cold, clammy, weak fast pulse, thirsty, then quiet and confused: fix the bleeding, lay them flat, insulate under as well as over, nothing by mouth.
- Splint in the position you found it, immobilise the joints above and below, and check warmth, colour, feeling and movement past it. Cold, white, blue or numb means loosen at once.
- Internal bleeding, a worsening head injury and abdominal injury cannot be fixed by hands. Keep them warm, off the ground and not alone, and spend the rest of your effort on whoever can be helped.
- Rule 7: pressure is a place, not a motion. Put your hands in one spot and leave them there.
