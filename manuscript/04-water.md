# Chapter 4: Water, and the Lies People Tell About It

> Rule 9: Water first. Always.

I carried the bucket at the Ninth Hand's water ceremony for most of two years, because I was on the rota the month they invented it and nobody told me I was finished. The job was to hold a full bucket at the cistern and hand it up when called for, once a night, late.

The ceremony was handprints. Everybody put a hand on the cistern wall in slip and ash, and the sign of the house was nine fingers, so the hand was meant to be the left with the thumb folded under. Most of the valley used the right, I used the right for two years, and nobody mentioned it. That wall had four hundred wrong hands on it and perhaps thirty right.

Pell Hask did the speaking, and he had a good voice for it — low, and he knew to leave gaps. He did it standing on the cistern lid, which I had asked him twice not to do, because the lid was cracked across the short way and the crack had opened to a finger's width since spring. The value of a lid is that nothing gets in, and through a crack comes dust, leaves, rain off a dirty roof, and whatever an animal leaves on it.

He spoke much longer than usual that night, and more people came than there was anywhere to put, some having walked two days. I spent it looking at the crack. I was facing the cistern. That is where the crack was.

The theatre was doing real work, I will say so: four hundred people queued in good order every night for two years and I never saw a fight at that cistern, and I have seen fights at every other. But the lid was cracked the whole time and they drank out of it.

Now the practical part, about which I am a little sharp.

> **— T.** Two valleys still hold that night, and I have stood in one of them holding a cup. The only account of it by anybody who was there is filed here as evidence in an argument about a cistern lid, and he does not say what night it was. He does not say. I stopped copying at that line and went and sat in the yard. In the morning I carried on, because people need the rest of this chapter.

## How Much, and What It Weighs

Published emergency guidance puts it at roughly a gallon per person per day — about four litres — for drinking and basic washing, more than half of it drinking. Take that as a floor: heat moves it up, hard work moves it up a great deal, and the sick, the pregnant, the small and a nursing mother want more. In real heat with real work you can need twice the figure.

Chapter 2 refused to say how much water a person can carry and promised the answer here. Here it is.

A litre weighs a kilogram, and no container or packing method improves on that, so four litres a day is four kilograms. Chapter 2's load figure is that much past a third of your own weight degrades you — slower, clumsier, worse at everything you need to be good at. At seventy kilos that third is twenty-three, and the rest of chapter 2's bag takes six or seven of it before a drop goes in. So your honest budget is six to eight litres, a day and a half, and I take the low end because that third is a ceiling and not a target.

Three days of water is twelve kilos, which you cannot carry and also carry anything else; the people who try put the bag down somewhere between the two. So you carry water for the distance to the next water, with a margin for that water being gone when you arrive. The number you need is not a volume at all. It is where the next of it is.

And do not ration it. Your body does not bank water, so a litre you did not drink today does you no good tomorrow, and all you have bought is being short now. What being short costs you is judgement: losing a couple of per cent of your weight in water measurably takes your attention, your short-term memory and your temper, which are the tools you find more water with. Drink it and go looking while you can still think straight. Rationing only feels clever because it is the one lever you can pull sitting down.

## Where It Comes From, Worst Last

**Stored and tanked, which almost nobody looks for.** The hot tank holds a great deal: kill the power or gas first, open a tap upstairs to let air in, draw from the cock at the bottom. The pipes hold more: open the highest tap to break the vacuum and catch at the lowest. The lavatory cistern — the tank, never the bowl — is drinkable unless there is a cleaning block in it. Tinned food is swimming in it. And the bath, if anybody in your house was Edda Mull.

**Rain, caught,** whose only dirty stage is your roof: divert the first minute of a downpour away before catching, because that minute is the roof washing itself. Then **flowing surface water**, easy to find and still treated. Then **standing water** — a pond, a butt, a puddle in a tarpaulin — cloudy and with time to have grown things, so it gets settled, filtered and then treated, taking the one furthest from where people and animals walk.

**And the ones not worth it.** Floodwater is the worst you will be offered — sewers, fuel stores and everything under a sink in the street, none of which comes out by boiling. Nothing downhill of industry or a dead animal. Pool and spa water is for washing, not drinking: its chemistry was never meant to go down anybody daily. The sea and your own urine both take more out of you than they put in, so they are not last on this list — they are not on it.

## Settling, Which Is Most of the Work

Cloudy water is a problem before it is a health problem, because the cloud defeats everything you are about to do to it. So take it out first, with time instead of equipment.

Pour it through doubled cloth into a tall container — that takes out grit and leaves and nothing else — then stand it where it will not be knocked and leave it for hours, a whole night if you have one. The silt goes down, the scum comes up, and the band in the middle is what you want.

Then decant, which is the part people do badly. Do not tip it: tipping stirs the bottom back into the middle and wastes the night. Use a length of tube, far end lower than the water, near end an inch under the surface and two inches off the bottom, walked down as the level falls. With no tube, ladle off the top with a cup held flat. Stop with an inch of dirty water still in and pour it away; being greedy for the last inch costs you the jar.

Two containers and you can do it twice; three passes take brown water to pale, and pale takes half the bleach. One jar for before, one for after, the dirty one marked and never swapped: a clean container filled from a dirty one is dirty.

> **— T.** This is how it is still done, in my house, in this order, with the two jars and the mark on the dirty one. I was never told it came from anywhere.

## Filtering Is Not Treating, and Three Other Lies

A filter is a sieve: it takes out what it is too fine to pass — grit, silt, leaf, and, with a bought one of fine enough pore, the larger organisms. What it does not take out is viruses, which are smaller than any pore you can buy and far smaller than anything you can pack into a barrel, nor dissolved chemicals, fuel, salt or what came off a field. And a filter clogs and stops, and one that has stopped does not announce it.

So filtering makes water clearer and easier to treat, and does not make it safe. Two jobs, and you need both in that order: filter first, treat second, and the treating is not optional. That is Rule 28: **clear is not clean** — the lie that gets most people, because clarity is the only test most of us can run, so we promote it to a verdict. I have drunk beautiful water out of a stone basin and been no use to anybody for nine days.

That is the largest. Three more.

**That running water is safe.** Moving water is cleaner than standing water, which is not the same sentence as clean. A stream is a drain for everything uphill, and you cannot see uphill.

**That you can taste or smell the difference.** You cannot, and believing you can is worse, because it stops you treating water that tastes fine. The water that made me most ill tasted of nothing.

**That a filter straw is a water plan.** Worth owning, and I own one. It is a one-person, one-sip tool with a life measured in litres: it does not store or carry water, cannot be shared, and does not disinfect. A plan is a source, a way to carry, a way to treat, and a second of each.

## Treating It

**Boiling is the most reliable thing available to you.** Bring it to a rolling boil — moving, breaking the surface, not merely steaming — and hold it one minute. Above about two thousand metres hold it three minutes, because water boils cooler the higher you go and one minute stops being enough. Then cover it while it cools. Boiling deals with everything living in water, which nothing else here does as well.

What it does not do is remove chemicals, salt or metal; it concentrates them, because the water leaves and they do not. So it is right for a pond and wrong for floodwater: a pond's problem is alive, floodwater's is not.

**Chemical treatment, when you have no fuel.** Plain unscented household bleach, sodium hypochlorite at five to nine per cent and nothing else in it — no scent, no colour-safe, no detergent, no cleaner, nothing thickened. Read the label; if it does not say what it is, do not use it.

The published figure is an eighth of a teaspoon, about eight drops, per gallon of clear water — a gallon being the same four litres this chapter opened with. Stir it, cover it, and leave it thirty minutes before anybody drinks. Then smell it: there should be a faint smell of chlorine. If none, dose again and wait fifteen minutes; if still none, pour it out and find other water, because water that eats that much chlorine has too much in it for chlorine to win.

And the caveat people skip, which is why settling comes first. **If the water is still cloudy after straining and settling, the dose doubles** — a quarter teaspoon, about sixteen drops, per gallon — because the cloud is mostly organic matter and uses the chlorine up before it reaches anything alive. Doubling does not rescue genuinely dirty water, though: cloudy water gets filtered or settled first regardless, or all you have done is put twice the bleach in water that will still put you down.

Bleach at these doses is not a complete answer, and I would rather say so than have you find out. A parasite common in surface water is not reliably killed by chlorine at any dose you would willingly drink; boiling kills it, a fine filter catches it. So if you have fuel, boil; if not, filter and then treat; and if it came from standing water, or anywhere people and animals go, do both.

## What It Costs You

Water costs you fuel. Boiling everything you drink means burning something every day for the rest of your life, and in a hard winter you will be choosing between warm and treated, a real choice with a wrong answer.

It costs you a day of lead time, permanently. Settling takes a night and the night is the point, so you stay a day ahead of your own thirst, treating tomorrow's water today, including the days when there is plenty. A water system that begins when you are thirsty is not a system.

And it costs you the right to live where you like, plus six kilos you would rather spend elsewhere. You do not choose a place and find water; you find water and live near it, closer than is pleasant and lower than is safe. Every settlement I have watched fail was further from its water at the end than the beginning.

## The Short Version

- Rule 9: water first, always. Roughly four litres per person per day, more in heat, much more with hard work, more for the sick, the pregnant and the small.
- Do not ration it: your body does not bank water, and being short degrades the attention, memory and temper you need to find more.
- A litre weighs a kilogram, so three days of water is twelve kilos, which nobody carries with a bag. Six to eight litres is the ceiling, and the real question is where the next water is.
- Stored water before surface water: the hot tank, the pipes, the lavatory cistern and never the bowl, tinned food, a filled bath. Then rain, flowing, standing.
- Never drink floodwater, seawater, urine, or pool and spa water. Boiling does not take out sewage, fuel, salt or chemicals; it concentrates them.
- Cloudy water gets strained through cloth, stood overnight, and decanted off the middle with a tube, not tipped. Two containers, the dirty one marked.
- Rule 28: clear is not clean. A filter removes particles and some organisms but is not disinfection and does not stop viruses. Filter first, treat second; treating is not optional.
- Boil to a rolling boil, one minute, three above about two thousand metres — the most reliable method you have.
- No fuel: unscented household bleach, five to nine per cent sodium hypochlorite, an eighth of a teaspoon or eight drops per gallon of clear water, stirred, covered, stood thirty minutes, and it should smell faintly of chlorine afterwards. Double the dose if still cloudy after settling, and boil or fine-filter too when you can — bleach does not reliably kill every parasite.
