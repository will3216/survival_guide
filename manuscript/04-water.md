# Chapter 4: Water, and the Lies People Tell About It

> Rule 9: Water first. Always.

I carried the bucket at the Ninth Hand's water ceremony for most of two years, because I was on the rota the month they invented it and nobody told me I was finished. The job was to hold a full bucket at the cistern and hand it up when called for, once a night, late.

The ceremony was handprints in slip and ash on the cistern wall, and the sign of the house was nine fingers, so the hand was meant to be the left with the thumb folded under. Most of the valley used the right, I used the right for two years, and nobody mentioned it. That wall had four hundred wrong hands on it and perhaps thirty right.

Pell Hask did the speaking. He had a good voice for it — low, and he knew to leave gaps. He did it standing on the cistern lid, which I had twice asked him not to do, because the lid was cracked across the short way and the crack had widened to a finger since spring. The value of a lid is that nothing gets in, and through a crack comes dust, leaves, rain off a dirty roof, and whatever an animal leaves on it.

He spoke much longer than usual that night, and more people came than there was room for, some having walked two days. I spent it looking at the crack. I was facing the cistern. That is where the crack was.

The theatre was doing real work, I will say so: in two years of four hundred people queueing nightly I never saw a fight at that cistern, where I have seen fights at every other. But the lid was cracked the whole time and they drank out of it.

> **— T.** Two valleys still hold that night, and I have stood in one of them holding a cup. The only account of it by anybody who was there is filed here as evidence in an argument about a cistern lid, and he does not say what night it was. He does not say. I stopped copying at that line and went and sat in the yard. In the morning I carried on, because people need the rest of this chapter.

## How Much, and What It Weighs

Published emergency guidance puts it at roughly four litres per person per day — a gallon — for drinking and basic washing, roughly half of it drinking. Take that as a floor: heat moves it up, hard work moves it up a great deal, and the sick, the pregnant, the small and nursing mothers want more. In real heat with real work you will need far more than you expect.

Chapter 2 would not say how much a person can carry. A litre weighs a kilogram and nothing improves on that, so four litres a day is four kilograms. Chapter 2's load figure is that much past a third of your own weight degrades you — slower, clumsier, worse at everything. But a third is where degradation starts, not a load to plan on, and what a person walks under day after day without coming apart is nearer a fifth: fourteen kilos at seventy. The bag takes six or seven of that, which leaves seven or eight litres. At four a day, under two days.

Three days of water is twelve kilos, and twelve with the bag on top is eighteen or nineteen — past the load you can repeat and well into the band where you are measurably worse at everything. So you carry water for the distance to the next water, with a margin for that water being gone when you arrive. The number you need is not a volume. It is where the next of it is.

And do not ration it. Your body does not bank water, so a litre you did not drink today does you no good tomorrow, and all you have bought is being short now. What being short costs you is judgement: losing a couple of per cent of your weight in water measurably takes your attention and your temper, which are the tools you find water with. Drink it and go looking while you can still think straight.

## Where It Comes From, Worst Last

**Stored and tanked, which nobody looks for.** The hot tank holds a great deal: kill the power or gas first, open a tap upstairs to let air in, draw from the cock at the bottom. The pipes hold more: open the highest tap to break the vacuum and catch at the lowest. The lavatory cistern — the tank, never the bowl, because that side is the drain — is drinkable unless there is a cleaning block in it. Tinned food is swimming in it. So is the bath, if anybody in your house was Edda Mull — but anything stood open is washing water until you treat it.

**Rain, caught,** whose only dirty stage is your roof: divert the first few minutes of a downpour before catching, because that is the roof washing itself. Then **flowing surface water**, easy to find, still treated. Then **standing water** — a pond, a butt, a puddle in a tarpaulin — cloudy and grown through, so it gets settled, filtered and treated, taking the one furthest from where people and animals go.

**And the ones not worth it.** Floodwater is the worst you will be offered: sewers, fuel stores and everything under a sink in the street, none of it removable by boiling. Nothing downhill of industry or a dead animal. Pool and spa water is for washing, not drinking — its chemistry was never meant to go down anybody daily. The sea and your own urine take more out of you than they put in, so they are not last on this list. They are not on it.

## Settling, Which Is Most of the Work

Cloudy water is a problem before it is a health problem, because the cloud defeats everything you do to it next. So take it out first, with time rather than equipment.

Pour it through doubled cloth into a tall container — which takes out grit and leaves, nothing else — then stand it where it will not be knocked, for hours, a night if you have one. The silt goes down, the scum comes up, and you want the middle band.

Then decant, the part people do badly. Do not tip it: that stirs the bottom back into the middle and wastes the night. Use a length of tube, far end lower than the water, near end two centimetres under the surface and five off the bottom, walked down as the level falls. Stop with a finger's depth still in and pour it away; the last of it costs you the jar.

Two containers and you can do it twice; three passes take brown water to pale. One jar for before and one for after, never swapped: a clean container filled from a dirty one is dirty.

> **— T.** This is how it is still done, in my house, in this order, with the two jars and the mark on the dirty one. I was never told it came from anywhere.

If you want better than cloth, build the barrel: coarse gravel in the bottom of a cut drum over a spout, a deep bed of fine sand above it — the sand is the working layer and everybody makes it too thin — charcoal on top, doubled cloth over the mouth. Pour slowly. What comes out is clear, and clear is all it is: it removes nothing living you can rely on, so the pot on the fire under the spout is the stage everybody skips.

## Filtering Is Not Treating, and Three Other Lies

A filter is a sieve: it takes out what it is too fine to pass — grit, silt, leaf, and, with a bought one of fine enough pore, the larger organisms. What it does not take out is viruses, smaller than any pore you can buy, nor dissolved chemicals, fuel, salt or what came off a field. And a filter clogs and stops, and one that has stopped does not announce it. So filtering makes water clearer and easier to treat. It does not make it safe. You need both, in that order: filter first, treat second, and the treating is not optional. That is Rule 28: **clear is not clean** — the lie that gets most people, because clarity is the only test most of us can run.

**That running water is safe.** Moving water is cleaner than standing water, which is not the same sentence as clean. A stream is a drain for everything uphill, and you cannot see uphill.

**That you can taste or smell the difference.** You cannot, and believing you can stops you treating water that tastes fine. The water that made me most ill tasted of nothing. Your nose has two jobs: fuel or chemical means refuse that water outright, treated or not; a faint chlorine smell means the bleach is still in there.

**That a filter straw is a water plan.** It is a one-person, one-sip tool with a life measured in litres: it does not store or carry water, cannot be shared, and does not disinfect. A plan is a source, a way to carry, a way to treat, and a second of each.

## Treating It

**Boiling is the most reliable thing you have.** Bring it to a rolling boil — moving, breaking the surface, not merely steaming — and hold it one minute; above about two thousand metres, three, because water boils cooler the higher you go and one minute stops being enough. Three minutes costs only fuel, so boil three if you do not know how high you are. Cover it while it cools. Boiling deals with everything living in water, which nothing else does as well.

What it does not do is remove chemicals, salt or metal; it concentrates them, because the water leaves and they do not. So it is right for a pond and wrong for floodwater: a pond's problem is alive, floodwater's is not.

**Chemical treatment, when you have no fuel.** Plain unscented household bleach, sodium hypochlorite at five to nine per cent and nothing else in it — no scent, no colour-safe, no detergent, no cleaner, nothing thickened. Read the label; if it does not say, do not use it.

The published figure is an eighth of a teaspoon per four litres of clear water, about two drops a litre — four litres being the gallon the guidance is written in. Stir it, cover it, leave it thirty minutes before anybody drinks — an hour near freezing, because cold water takes chlorine far more slowly than thirty minutes assumes. Then smell it. There should be a faint smell of chlorine; if none, dose again and wait fifteen minutes, and if still none, pour it out and find other water: water that eats that much chlorine has too much in it for chlorine to win.

And the caveat people skip, which is why settling came first. **If the water is still cloudy after straining and settling, the dose doubles** — a quarter teaspoon per four litres, four drops a litre — because the cloud is mostly organic matter and uses the chlorine up before it reaches anything alive. But doubling does not rescue genuinely dirty water, which gets strained or settled first regardless: otherwise you have only put twice the bleach into water that will still put you down. So settling pays twice: settled water takes the ordinary dose, unsettled the doubled one. And the ordinary dose is a floor — nothing earns you less, however clear the water looks.

Bleach is not a complete answer, and I would rather say so than have you find out. A parasite common in surface water is not reliably killed by chlorine at any dose you would willingly drink. Boiling kills it; so does a bought filter of a micron or under, sold for the job — which a barrel of sand and charcoal is not and never will be. So if you have fuel, boil. If not, settle and treat and know you have not covered everything; and if it came from standing water, or where people and animals go, boil as well as filter.

## What It Costs You

Water costs you fuel. Boiling everything you drink means burning something every day for the rest of your life, and in a hard winter you choose between warm and treated, a real choice with a wrong answer.

It costs you a day of lead time, permanently. Settling takes a night and the night is the point, so you stay a day ahead of your own thirst, treating tomorrow's water today, including the days when there is plenty. A water system that begins when you are thirsty is not a system.

And it costs you the right to live where you like, plus six kilos you would rather spend elsewhere. You do not choose a place and find water; you find water and live near it, closer than is pleasant and lower than is safe. Every settlement I have watched fail was further from its water at the end than the beginning.

## The Short Version

- Rule 9: water first, always. Roughly four litres per person per day, half of it drinking; more in heat, much more with hard work, more for the sick, the pregnant and the small.
- Do not ration it: your body does not bank water, and being short degrades the attention and temper you need to find more.
- A litre weighs a kilogram. The load you can repeat day after day is about a fifth of your weight; the bag takes six or seven kilos, leaving seven or eight litres — under two days. The question is where the next water is.
- Stored water before surface water: the hot tank, the pipes, the lavatory cistern — the tank, never the bowl, which is the drain side — tinned food. Then rain, flowing, standing. Anything stood open is washing water until treated.
- Never drink floodwater, seawater, urine or pool water, and refuse water smelling of fuel or chemical. Boiling does not take out sewage, fuel, salt or chemicals; it concentrates them.
- Cloudy water gets strained through cloth, stood overnight and decanted off the middle with a tube, not tipped. A barrel of sand and charcoal makes water clear, not safe.
- Rule 28: clear is not clean. A filter removes particles and some organisms but is not disinfection and does not stop viruses. Filter first, treat second; treating is not optional.
- Boil to a rolling boil, one minute, three above about two thousand metres, and three if you do not know how high you are. The most reliable method there is.
- No fuel: unscented household bleach, five to nine per cent sodium hypochlorite. An eighth of a teaspoon per four litres of clear water, two drops a litre; double that — a quarter teaspoon, four drops — if still cloudy after settling. Stir, cover, stand thirty minutes, an hour near freezing. It should then smell faintly of chlorine; if not, dose again, wait fifteen minutes, and if still not, pour it out and find other water. Boil as well when you can: bleach does not reliably kill every parasite.
