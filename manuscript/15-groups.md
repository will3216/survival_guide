# Chapter 15: How Groups Decide Things

> Rule 44: Write the exile rule before you need it.

In my tenth year a woman called Hollis Dray stopped me at the bottom of the path and told me I was on four houses instead of six.

I asked which four. She told me. I asked what had happened to the other two and she said it had been decided. I asked who by, and she said it had been decided, in the voice of somebody who has already answered, and I went and got the water.

It made for a worse walk. The two I came off were the first you reach at the top of the path, so everything now went another ninety paces across the flat before I put it down, forty-odd times a day, and by the Thursday I could tell you a good deal about my knees. The Bellwright came past and asked me to move the barrow. He was right about the gradient on that path, as he was right about every gradient I ever heard him discuss. He was wrong about people.

Over the next four years the round kept changing and nobody told me why. Jobs stopped being handed out — not cancelled, just never mentioned again, so the second gate watch stopped existing one spring and I could not tell you the week. The rota had been chalked inside the store door for nine years, where anybody could look at it; then it was written up the day after the week started; then it was told to me at the bottom of the path.

So this chapter is how a group settles things, and how you can tell from the yard that it has stopped.

> **— T.** Five years. That is the five years, all of it, handed over as a change to a delivery round — and the dreadful part is that he is not wrong. Four houses, then the door, then the fires, in that order, *is* what happened. I have been twice through looking for the page where he says what it was he carried water through. There is no such page, there was never going to be one, and I — if this book is ever reduced to one chapter, it is this one. This is what it looks like from underneath, every time, and the people at the table cannot see it. Everything below is sound and I would not move a line of his order.

## The Number That Breaks The Table

What breaks a group's talking is arithmetic, not bad faith. Four people are six pairs of people, eight are twenty-eight, fifteen are a hundred and five. Nobody argues with a group, they argue with a person, and the number to be squared climbs far faster than the number of people.

Roughly: **up to seven or eight**, informal works, because everybody hears everything and nobody can be left out of a conversation in one room; build no machinery you do not need, since machinery costs evenings. **By fifteen to twenty** what you lose first is not decisions but jobs — work everybody can see needs doing gets done by nobody, each reasonably assuming somebody else has it — so you need named jobs, a rota written where people can read it, and a stated day it changes. **Around fifty**, nobody can hold a meeting of everybody about anything, so a few begin deciding for the rest whether or not anybody votes on it. **Above about a hundred and fifty**, nobody knows everybody; up to there behaviour is policed by being known, because the people you see tomorrow would know, and past that it has to be written.

So, the part people fight hardest: **a group that has outgrown the table already has a structure.** The only question is whether anyone outside it can see what it is. Writing it down does not create the power; it makes it legible, and legible power is the only kind you can argue with.

## Four Ways To Decide The Same Thing

Four, the way there are four ways to lift the same awkward weight, each with a failure that arrives as a matter of course, nobody being wicked.

**Everybody agrees.** Works small, with equal risk, time to spare, and a no that costs nothing. It hands every person a veto, so you do whatever the most immovable member tolerates, usually nothing — and objecting aloud comes to cost more than wearing it, so people stop, and you get agreement nobody agrees with.

**Count the hands.** Works when the question is one yes-or-no, the count is public, and the losers are told the number. It builds a permanent minority: six of twenty at the far end of the flat lose every water question for ever, and after a year those six stop coming. So settle in advance that a decision falling only on some people needs their agreement, not the room's.

**A few decide.** Works past the table, for routine decisions, with fixed terms and the power to take them off it. The failure is drift, gradual and well meant: they do know more than anybody else, come to treat the knowing as the qualification, and since explaining takes longer than deciding, less is explained and then nothing is.

**One person decides.** This genuinely works, and people who dislike it have not been in a burning building: fire, flood, a stranger at the gate, anywhere a wrong decision now beats a right one in an hour. Its failure is having no end in it, so write the emergency, the person and what ends it before you use it — a decider nobody can stop cannot stop either, and his wrong answer becomes everybody's.

Use all four, keyed to the decision: one for emergencies, a few for routine, counted hands for anything dividing the stores, everybody for anything changing who you are — and say which is which out loud and in advance. Almost no fight I have watched was about the question. They were about who had the right to settle it.

## Short Rations And Who Thinks It Was Fair

Dividing short food looks like a sum and is not one; the sum is the easy half. People will take a smaller share than they want with remarkably little trouble. What they will not take is being unable to see how the share was arrived at, and a group that loses that loses everything else about three months later.

I know it from where the scale stood. In a bad year the measures were done inside the store with the door shut, by two people who were, as far as I ever knew, honest, and everybody complained constantly for a season. Then the measuring moved into the yard, scale on a barrel, anyone welcome to watch. The complaints stopped inside a fortnight. Nobody got more food. Same two people, same scale.

So: **a process people can watch beats a cleverer one they cannot, every time.** Measure in the yard at a time anybody can attend, and then three things.

1. **Post what each household gets and the reason**, so the reason is as public as the amount; an amount without a reason is read as a decision about the person.
2. **Name the exceptions in advance** — the sick, the nursing, whoever is on the heaviest work — and write them into the rule, because an exception settled at the barrel is not an exception, it is a favour, and a favour has a giver.
3. **Rotate the queue order, post the rotation, and let whoever divides take last.** The back of a queue is worse than the front when things run out, so a fixed order is a second ration hidden inside the first; and one cuts while the other picks, whose merit is requiring nobody to be trusted. On queues: one line, one server, nobody holding places.

## Falling Out, And The Two I Walked To The Road

People fall out. That is a consequence of being near each other, not a sign of anything, and the only question is whether there is a route for it — because a quarrel with no route goes sideways, into the rota and the stores and who gets spoken to.

So, in advance: name the person or pair whose job is to hear a dispute, who may not hear one they are in; put a time on it, because an unheard quarrel ferments; and write a ladder of consequences, smallest first — a word said, work owed back, a thing replaced, a job taken away, a stretch outside, and out — because a group with only one consequence uses it for everything or for nothing, and both are how groups come apart.

Then the end of the ladder, which nobody wants to write and everybody needs.

**Rule 44. Write the exile rule before you need it.** Four things on one sheet while everybody is calm and bored: what gets a person sent away, named as specific acts and never as attitudes; who decides; who may never decide; what happens after. A year early, because all four are impossible to settle while the thing is fresh and very nearly easy while it is only a thought.

Of those, **never one person alone** and **never the person it happened to** — not because a wronged person would be unjust, but because nobody alive can tell their own judgement from their own anger, themselves included, and the group has to live with whatever gets decided.

A group that cannot send anybody away at all is not kind. It is run by whoever is least willing to leave.

Nobody wrote it down, so when it came it was settled in an afternoon by three people, two of whom were the two it had happened to, and I was sent to walk two people out to the road. Eleven miles to the crossroads, and we talked about the weather. One of them had taught me to carry two buckets without either swinging, which is done from the shoulders. They went with a day's water, a day's food and their own tools, and the going part was right. The deciding took an afternoon, and that was the trouble.

For the other side of this — what you do when the group deciding is the one you are in — chapter 7 is the long version, and **Rule 31** is the thing to have settled before you walk in.

## Things That Changed On My Round

Five things to watch for. I will not tell you what they mean, because I did not know that while I was looking at them. I can tell you how each looked from the yard, where you will be standing.

**One person holds what everybody needs to know.** You cannot find out how much food is in the store by asking, and the answer changes with who you ask. The repair is dull: the count read out on a fixed day, by a different person each time, to whoever turns up.

**A rule stops applying to one person.** **Rule 77: a rule that stops applying to one person has stopped.** There is a rota, there is a name not on it, and nobody mentions this. At the Ninth Hand everybody turned out for the thing at night and everybody carried water except one man, and I was there two years and took that for a job description. An exempt list one name long is not a rule. It is a habit, and habits do not survive a hard winter.

**What it costs to stay keeps going up.** Ask what was asked of you on arrival and what is asked now, and whether you could have agreed to today's version on the first day. If not, the price moved while you were inside it, which is the only way it moves. Chapter 7 is the route out.

**Doubt gets treated as disloyalty.** Remember the last time somebody disagreed out loud about the rota, and what happened to them in the fortnight after. A group in decent health argues about the rota constantly and nothing happens to anyone.

**A way of deciding quietly stops being used.** This is the one that cost the Bell Country, and what to watch is not the rule that changed but the posting that stopped. The rota came off the door. Nobody announced that and nobody voted on it, and nothing replaced it but a man at the bottom of the path telling me four houses. A group that has stopped using the way it settles things has nothing to announce: you cannot declare what you never decided to do.

And the fires, which I remember best because they changed where I put the buckets down. For nine years there was one. Then a second at the east end, which the people off the lower terraces said saved them walking, and it did. Then a third. Then the council ate at the first and the longest-serving of us at the second, and in the last winter there were five, and one house on nobody's round, and I do not know what those people ate. Somewhere in there a man who had queued every day for nine years walked to the front and nobody said anything. I thought the queue had got shorter.

> **— T.** Set them side by side, reader, since he will not. One man holding what everybody needs to know: a count that stopped being read out. A rule no longer applying to one person: a name missing from a rota. A rising price of belonging: six houses, then four. Doubt made into disloyalty: the season nobody argued about the water. A way of deciding quietly abandoned: a chalked sheet off a door, never mentioned again. And five fires, in the order they were lit. He has no word for one of these things and has handed us the whole of it anyway, because he was the man walking between them with the buckets, and the buckets go everywhere. Learn to watch the chores. Of everything that moves when a group begins to fail, they move first.

## What It Costs You

Structure costs daylight, and a council costs you knowing how things were settled — even a good one you chose and can unchoose. You learn what was decided and not why unless you go and read the sheet, and most people do not. I did not, for nine years, and it was on a door I walked past forty times a day.

The exile rule costs most. Write it a year early and you will one day use it exactly as written, in front of everybody, on somebody you like, for something you would have forgiven. That is not the rule failing. That is the rule being worth having, and it will not feel like it on the day.

Rationing in the open costs efficiency, genuinely: the clever allocation is better by arithmetic, and you will stand in the yard watching food go where it does least good, because it goes where the written rule sends it.

And all of it rests on the group lasting. When the Bell Country scattered I was owed help by about eleven people and collected from none, and not one of them cheated me. A favour is only good money where the group still is, which is the second time that has happened to me and the second time I did not see it coming.

## The Short Version

- Below about eight, talking works and machinery wastes daylight. By fifteen to twenty you need named jobs and a posted rota, or work everybody can see goes undone by everybody.
- Past the table a group has a structure anyway. Writing it down does not create the power, it makes it visible, and visible power is the only kind anyone can argue with.
- Use all four mechanisms, keyed to the decision: one person for emergencies only and with a written end to it, a few for routine, counted hands for the stores, everybody for who you are.
- Anything falling only on some people needs their agreement, not the room's majority, or you have built a minority that loses for ever and then leaves.
- Ration in the open: scale in the yard, amounts and reasons posted together, exceptions written in advance, queue order rotated, whoever divides takes last. The watchable process beats the cleverer hidden one.
- Name in advance who hears a dispute, put a time on it, and write a ladder of consequences from a word to expulsion, because a group with one consequence uses it for everything or nothing.
- Rule 44: write the exile rule before you need it — the acts, the decider, who may never decide, what happens after. Never one person alone, and never the person it happened to.
- Rule 77: a rule that stops applying to one person has stopped. Watch also for one keyholder, a rising price of staying, doubt treated as disloyalty, and a posting that quietly stops appearing.
- Rule 31 is the thing to settle before you walk in, and chapter 7 is the long version of leaving.
