# Chapter 11: Voices Across the Waste

> Rule 14: Agree on a schedule and a place. That is a radio you do not have to carry.

For about four months in my first year I thought I was the last person alive. I was not looking for anybody. I was walking a loop of about nine days — water at one end, a dry store at the other — and writing down each evening where I had been, because that is what you do with a route. If anybody had asked, I would have said I liked the book tidy.

The radio was not mine. It came out of a house in a valley I had no business in, and somebody had taped the case shut and written a name on the tape in paint: Ruda Vilk. I never met him. I carried his radio for two reasons, and neither of them was hope. It was light, and the switch was good.

It was raining in the thin way that does not look like rain until you are wet, and the cells were nearly out — the light on the front had gone the colour of weak tea. On the eleventh evening I sat on the sheltered side of a hill, facing up it, eating, the radio on the ground between my boots.

A woman came through reading a shopping list. Slowly, with gaps in it, the way you read a thing out when the reading out is the point. She was not calling anybody and gave no name. She was saying it for the company of saying it.

It is the most beautiful thing I have ever heard.

I wrote down the weather and that the cells were nearly gone, because the cells were what I was going to need in the morning.

> **— T.** That list is in a frame in two settlements I can name. It is recited at weddings — not the words, you understand, the *saying* of it; a woman stands and names six things and the room holds its breath. And the one man alive who heard it wrote down *the weather*. You had the pencil in your hand. You had the pencil in your hand, and you used it on the rain. He is right about the cells; a dead set tells you nothing at all. Nothing below is in doubt, and I have had two of its headings read back to me by people who cannot read.

Chapter 1 leaves you holding the third question. *Does anyone know where I am?* This chapter is the other half of it: how anybody finds out. The cheap methods first, because they work.

## The Cheap Methods, Which Are The Good Ones

**Leave a message where it will be found.** At the door a person would actually use, at eye height, on the sheltered face, in pencil or charcoal rather than anything that runs. Who you are, when, where you went, and when to stop expecting you. Date it even if you have to guess the month, because an undated note is a note about an unknown year, and somebody will read it in the spring and walk a week to meet a man who left in October. Leave the same note wherever you stop next: a trail of dated notes can be followed, and one note can only be stood in front of.

**A dead drop is a message that does not need the two of you in the same place.** A tin, a jar, a capped pipe, somewhere neither of you lives, off a path, reachable without standing about in the open. Waterproof it, because the one certain thing to happen to a note in a tin is water.

The mark is the part people skip. Without one, finding out whether the drop was worth the walk means making the walk, and nine times out of ten it was not. So agree two marks — one meaning *there is something in it*, one meaning *I have taken it* — and put them on the way in rather than on the drop, low, small, and the sort of scratch a place might have. Date them, and rub out the old one each time, or you end up with a wall of marks and no information.

**And then the schedule, worth more than everything else in this chapter.** Two people who agree to check the same place on the same day of the week have solved most of their problem with no equipment at all. **Rule 14.** Say it out loud and have the other say it back: *the broken stile, every seventh day, in the morning.* Pick a day you can count to without a calendar and a place you can find in bad light, and agree what happens when one of you is not there — same place, same day, next week, and a number of weeks after which the other stops coming. It has found me more people than any radio, needs no cells, cannot be overheard, and does not break when you drop it.

## Being Seen On Purpose

Three things read as a person at a distance, and nothing else reliably does.

**Contrast.** Something that is not the tone of what it sits on: dark on snow, light on heather, a straight shadow on flat ground. Tone matters more than colour, because what the eye finds is the edge.

**Movement.** The eye is built for it. Waving something large and slow beats waving your arms, and both beat standing still being correct about where you are.

**Regularity.** A straight line, a square, three of a thing spaced evenly. Weather and ground make shapes but very rarely a straight edge or an even repeat, so a regular shape reads as made by hands, which is the only question a searcher is asking.

**Three of anything means distress.** Three whistle blasts, three flashes, three fires in a triangle. It is a convention rather than a law of nature, but it is the one nearly everybody was taught, and three because one of anything is a noise and two is a coincidence. Space them, repeat, and listen between repeats, because a signal you never stop making is one you never learn worked. A whistle beats shouting — louder for less effort, carrying where a voice does not, and not finished after ten minutes as your voice is. **Rule 6: two methods, not two objects.** A whistle and something bright that stays put, not two whistles.

**And point the signal at where people actually are.** A fire in a hollow is a fire nobody sees; the same fire on the shoulder of the hill is visible for an evening's walk in three directions. Signals go high, onto open ground, and across what people travel along — a track, a river, a ridge, the one road everybody still uses. Make it bigger than feels sensible, because a mark meant to be read from far off has to look absurd close up.

## Radio, Honestly

Radio is what everybody wants and what I plan for last.

Almost every handheld you will find is essentially **line of sight**. With a hill, a ridge or a building between you and the other set, the signal does not bend round it in any useful way, and that fact governs more than the equipment does. Two people a short walk apart with a hill between them may get nothing; two a long way apart in sight of each other's ground may be clear. The terrain decides, not the dial.

**Height does more than power**, and it is the one I would keep over the rest. Doubling a set's power buys a small fraction more distance and flattens the cells doing it; getting the aerial up — the top of the rise, onto a roof, up a pole, out from under the trees — changes what the set can see, and what it can see is the whole problem. Climb the hill before looking for a bigger radio. And the sets people remember reaching across a county were not doing it on their own: that reach came off kit on high ground, running on power somebody maintained, and that kit is gone.

**Batteries are the real constraint and the end of every radio story.** Transmitting takes many times the current listening does, so the set that talked all afternoon is flat by evening while the set that listened still works tomorrow. Found cells are aged cells and hold a fraction of the label. Keep them out of the set when not in use, cool and dry, and mark which you have charged: **Rule 55**, and a radio you cannot keep in power is a radio you do not have.

**A listening schedule, not a talking one.** Agree the times in advance — a few minutes at a chosen hour, morning and evening, sets off between — because two sets switched on at different times might as well be in different centuries. Write it on the set.

**And listen longer than you transmit. Rule 72.** Listening is nearly free and transmitting is not. A radio has no address on it, so everybody in range hears you, including the people you would have chosen not to meet, and you cannot take it back; listening first tells you who is out there and what they want before you have told them anything. And the part that cost me a winter: most of what you want to know is already being said by somebody, and you cannot hear it with your thumb on the switch.

## Maps, And Writing Down Where You Were

**A map is a consumable.** Paper dies at the folds, and the fold that fails is always across the bit you use. Copy the piece you need onto something you do not mind losing, keep the original flat and dry, and never mark it in anything but pencil. Copying by hand is also the only way I know to learn ground.

**Keep a route log.** Every time the ground makes you choose — a junction, a river, a change of direction — one line: the time, which way you went, and the one thing that would let you recognise the spot again. A line takes ten seconds. I have kept one every day for twenty-odd years because I like the book tidy.

It is the log that makes pacing worth anything. Work out your own pace over ground you have measured, counting every second step, and write the number in the log rather than your head; then measure it again loaded, and uphill, because both change it and neither by an amount you can guess. Marta Lunn paced a length of track with me twice in one afternoon and we disagreed both times, which is why you measure your own.

A direction, a pace count and a time give you roughly where you are, and the error grows the whole way, so reset it at anything you can positively identify — a bridge, a confluence, a summit — and write the reset down. An estimate corrected at noon is useful; one uncorrected since Tuesday is a story. **Follow a long thing rather than aiming at a small one**: a river, a wall, a ridge or a road cannot be missed in fog, and when what you want sits on one of them, aim deliberately to one side, so that striking the river tells you which way to turn instead of leaving you to guess.

Because getting unlost is almost never a matter of knowing where you are. It is a matter of knowing where you *were*, and being able to walk back to it. With a log you are one bad decision from a known position. Without one you are lost from the moment you stop recognising things, and you will not be able to say when that was.

## What It Costs You

The cheap methods cost you discipline on the days there is nothing to report, which is most of them. A schedule works only if you keep walking to a stile in the rain to find nothing, week after week, for the one week in forty that there is something, and the week you skip is the week the other person came. I have missed two, and one of them mattered.

Dated notes and marked drops cost you privacy: a note says a person came through, a mark says somebody works this ground, and there is no finding without some of the being found.

Radio costs you cells, weight, and a kind of hope that is worse than the weight. I have watched people carry a dead set for a year because putting it down felt like a decision about other people still being out there. Carry it if it works; if it does not, take the aerial for wire and keep the schedule. And the route log costs ten seconds at every junction, forever, against never having to tell anybody you do not know where you came from.

## The Short Version

- Leave a dated note at the door anybody would use: who, when, where you went, when to stop expecting you. Undated is useless. Leave the same note at your next stop.
- Use a dead drop neither of you lives near: off the path, waterproof, with two agreed marks on the way in — something inside, and collected — dated, old ones rubbed out.
- Rule 14: agree a schedule and a place. Same spot, same day, said out loud and repeated back, with an agreed fallback week. No cells, nothing to break, nothing overheard.
- Contrast, movement and regularity are what read as human at distance. Three of anything means distress: three blasts, three flashes, three fires. Space them, repeat, listen between.
- Point signals where people actually are — high ground, open ground, across a track or ridge — and make them far bigger than feels sensible. A whistle beats shouting on effort and range.
- Handheld radio is line of sight: a hill between you beats any set, and height beats power, so get the aerial up first. Found cells are weak: keep them out of the set, mark them charged, and agree listening times.
- Rule 72: listen longer than you transmit — transmitting drains many times what listening does, everybody in range hears you and you cannot take it back, and most of what you need is already being said.
- Copy the piece of map you need and keep the original flat and dry. Log time, direction and one landmark at every junction, measure your own pace, and reset at anything you can identify.
