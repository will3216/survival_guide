# Chapter 6: Food Matters Later Than You Think

> Rule 19: Salt is money.

Arno Bligh paid me to carry four hundred pounds of dried beans up an outside stair, a fortnight of afternoons, two sacks a trip. He had a pantry the length of the room and by the end you could not see the wall.

He starved that winter with the lot still stacked.

The best I can do with it is this: he had one pot, no fuel, and a dried bean wants hours. Nobody in that building knew that, and I did not know myself for another two years. I was the man who put them there and I never once asked whether anybody upstairs could cook a bean. I counted sacks, and stacked them a hand's gap off the wall for air, mouths turned in, oldest at the front.

That is why I am particular about sacks. My back has not been right since the second week of that job, and it was not the weight — it was the stair, which turned at the top, so the last four steps went up with the load on one shoulder and the body twisted. Carry on the inside shoulder going up a turning stair.

In the first week, food is a morale problem and not a survival problem. Water first, always — Rule 9, and the only thing here that will kill you inside three days; a fit person with water goes weeks without food. People go into the water for food, and into buildings for food, on day two, before they are properly hungry. The hunger is never what gets them. Eat what is in front of you, keep the sacks dry, and go and do the water.

## What Work Costs to Feed

A grown person lying still and warm needs somewhere around two thousand to two and a half thousand calories a day to hold their weight. That is a figure for a person in a chair. Dig, haul and walk all day and you are into three to four and a half thousand, and people working hard in cold weather under a load have been measured higher again.

So the arithmetic of a bad month is not really about food. **A working survivor needs something like half again to twice what a hiding one needs**, so if the stores are short the lever you pull is the work: fewer jobs, shorter days, the heavy ones dropped. A crew that keeps digging on half-feed is spending its own muscle, and muscle does not come back in a week.

## What Keeps, and in What Order It Betrays You

Dry, cool, dark, sealed, off the floor. Every loss I have had was one of those going missing.

**Whole dry grains and whole dry legumes are the best thing in any pantry.** Kept properly they last years — long enough to outlast your ability to keep mice out of them. Damp beats you quietly: a sack slightly wet in one corner heats, then smells, then is a sack of nothing.

**Milled things go sooner**, because the oil in flour and meal has been opened to the air: months, where whole grain kept years. Store whole and grind what you need.

**Fats go first, and fat is what you will want most.** Oil, rendered fat, nuts and seeds all carry their own oil and all go rancid — months rather than years, faster in warmth, light and air. Keep it cold, dark and in a small container filled to the top, and eat it rather than admire it.

**Salt keeps forever, and that is only the second-best thing about it.** You cannot do heavy work in heat without replacing it, it makes dull food eatable, and it is the only preservative you can carry and trade. The year I understood that I swapped nine pounds of good beans for a fist of salt and walked away certain I had been robbed. By the next summer I would have doubled it. **Rule 19. Salt is money.** Nobody can make it at home in a hurry, which is why it is priced like that.

Rotate: oldest at the front, or a pantry loses its oldest third without anybody noticing. Growing food and putting it up yourself is a later chapter; this one is what is already in sacks and tins.

## Tins

Most tins are still fine. The ones that are not can kill a healthy adult, so this is the part I am stiff about.

**Do not open a tin that is bulging, domed or springy at the ends** — gas inside means something living in there has been making it. **Do not open one that is leaking, sticky or rusted through.** **Do not open one with a damaged seam:** a dent crossing it, a sharp crease, a cracked or pulled seam, anything where the sealed edge has been hit. A dent in the flat of a tin is a dent. A dent on the seam is a door.

And if you have it open before you noticed: **if it spurts or foams when the seal goes, if the liquid has gone milky or the contents slimy or discoloured, or if it smells wrong at all, stop there.** Do not taste it to check. Tasting is not a test: the amount that hurts you is smaller than the amount you can taste, and what would fix you afterwards is not being made any more.

A suspect tin does not go on the fire, because a sealed tin in a fire bursts, and it does not go to the dog or the hens, because it will do to them what it would have done to you. Bury it deep, away from water and from anywhere people or animals dig, unopened if it still is, and wash your hands and whatever it touched.

**A tin that looks fine is not evidence that it is safe, only evidence that it has none of the faults you can see.** The worst thing in a tin is invisible, has no smell and does not change the look of the food, so there is no inspection that clears a tin, only one that condemns it. Throw the doubtful ones out while you are not yet desperate, because desperate is when the arithmetic starts favouring a chance, and the arithmetic is wrong.

## Wild Plants, and Why There Are No Pictures of Them Here

I am not going to teach you to recognise a single plant, and be suspicious of anybody who does it in a book. There are no pictures here, I do not know what grows where you are standing, and a drawing of a leaf from somebody else's country is worse than nothing, because it gives you confidence.

Two flat facts. The return is poor: wild greens are mostly water and fibre, and you can pick all day and come back with less than the walk cost you. What carries real calories is nuts, seeds, roots and tubers, and those are seasonal, local and mostly already known about by whoever lives there. And getting it wrong is occasionally fatal — the common way to die of a plant is eating something that closely resembles something edible, in the wrong season, in a hurry. Nibbling a corner to see is not a safe test; some of the bad ones work in an amount you would not notice swallowing.

A man on our crew ate a root in the fourth spring that looked exactly like the right root and we buried him on the Tuesday. So I learned mine off people after that, in the ground, because a plant in flower and the same plant in autumn are not the same picture.

**Rule 42. Learn three plants completely before you learn twenty badly.** Three that grow where you live, from somebody who has eaten them for years: what it looks like in every month, what grows beside it that resembles it, which part you eat and which you do not, and how it has to be cooked. I once had twenty out of a book and could not confidently find one in my own valley. Three is a skill. Twenty is a way of feeling prepared.

## Soaking, a Lid, and a Box of Hay

This is the useful part of the chapter.

A dried bean is hard because the water has been taken out of it, and cooking has to put the water back before it can do anything else. Soaking does that part for free, with no fuel, while you are asleep.

Then the lid, which is not a nicety. An open pot spends most of its fuel turning water into steam; a lid keeps the heat where you paid for it, and a clamped lid beats a loose one. I taught this on a back step in the eleventh year to a girl called Hesper Vane, whose lid sat proud of the rim by the thickness of a nail, and the lid is what we fixed.

> **— T.** We sell beans by the half-vane, and every kitchen in this country owns a straw box we call by her name as well. Children are taught it before they are trusted with a fire, in this order, with the hard boil before the box, and it is still exactly how it is done. The only account anybody has of where she learned it is a subordinate clause in a paragraph about pot lids. He fixed her lid. That is what he thought was worth the ink.

Here is the whole of it. It converts fuel into time, the best trade in this book.

1. **Soak.** Cover beans or whole grains with two or three times their depth of water, eight to twelve hours, overnight. In hot weather soak shorter or change the water: a long warm soak sours. **Throw the soaking water away** and cook in fresh — what came out of the beans is in it, wind-making sugars and some of what makes kidney beans poisonous.
2. **Fill the pot and put the lid on.** Fresh water a couple of fingers above the food. A full pot holds heat far better than a half-empty one, so fill it or use a smaller pot. Keep acid — tomato, vinegar, sour fruit — out until the beans are soft, or the skins stop giving.
3. **Boil hard for ten minutes at the least.** A real rolling boil, not a simmer. Some dried beans, red kidney beans worst of all, are genuinely poisonous until they have had that hard boil, and holding them warm but below the boil makes that worse rather than better. **This is the step you may not shorten, and it is why the rest of the method is safe at all.**
4. **Into the box, straight off the boil.** Have it built and waiting: a crate or tight-lidded barrel packed with dry hay, straw, wool or crumpled paper. Nest the pot with a hand's depth of packing all round and as much underneath as over, for the same reason the ground takes your heat at night. Press it into every gap, blanket on top, lid down.
5. **Leave it alone.** Beans three to five hours, whole grains one to two, judged by the food and not the clock. **Do not open it to look.** Every look costs about an hour's worth of heat, and the method works only because the pot stays near the boil for the first hours.
6. **Finish on the fire if it needs it.** A few minutes back at a boil costs almost nothing, and is not a failure.
7. **Eat it that day.** It comes out warm rather than hot, having cooled slowly for hours, which is the condition in which food goes bad fastest. This cooks a meal; it does not keep one. Anything left over is boiled hard again before eating, once.

A fire lit twice a day for two hours is wood somebody walks out for every afternoon forever. A fire lit once for twenty minutes is not.

## What It Costs You

Salt costs more than it should and you pay it. The people who waited for a better rate did heavy summer work without it and could not think straight by the third week.

The box costs you foresight, which is harder to find than fuel: you decide at breakfast what you eat at dusk, and set the soak the night before. A hungry person wants to cook now, and this method has no now in it.

Storage costs a standing job — dry-stacking, turning the stock, checking for damp, setting traps — and the week nobody does it is not the week you find out.

Being strict about tins costs you food; some of what you bury would have been fine. That is cheap, because the alternative is finding out which ones were not, with nothing to treat it with.

And restraint about plants costs you the feeling of doing something: walking past greens you cannot name feels like laziness.

## The Short Version

- In the first week food is a morale problem, not a survival problem. Water first — Rule 9, the only thing here that kills inside three days. People drown looking for lunch.
- Calories follow work: roughly two to two and a half thousand a day doing nothing, three to four and a half thousand at heavy labour. Short stores mean cutting work, not rations.
- Dry, cool, dark, sealed, off the floor. Whole grains and legumes keep years, flour months, fats soonest — and fat is what you will crave. Oldest at the front.
- Rule 19: salt is money. It never spoils, you cannot work hard in heat without replacing it, and nobody makes it at home in a hurry. Pay what it costs.
- Never open a tin that is bulging, leaking, rusted through or damaged across the seam. If it spurts, foams, looks milky or smells wrong on opening, stop. Never taste to test.
- A sound-looking tin proves only that it has no fault you can see. Nothing clears a tin. Bury the doubtful ones deep and unopened, away from water.
- Rule 42: learn three local plants completely, from someone who eats them, in every season. Wild greens return few calories, and the usual way to die of a plant is a lookalike.
- Soak legumes and grains eight to twelve hours, throw the soak water away, then boil hard a full ten minutes — not a simmer. Some beans, kidney beans especially, are poisonous without it.
- Then nest the full, boiling, lidded pot in a hand's depth of dry packing, above and below, three to five hours unopened. Eat it that day; it cools too slowly to keep.
