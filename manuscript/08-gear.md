# Chapter 8: Gear That Earns Its Weight

> Rule 55: Gear you do not maintain is gear you do not have.

In my third year Benno Kell sent me down a ladder into a cache a man had built under a garage before the Collapse, with a sack and a list, and I made four trips. Going down a ladder with your arms full is the error. I knew that at the top of the first trip and did it anyway, and on the fourth my arms were finished and I came down the last three rungs mostly on my elbows and opened my shin on the bracket.

Everything in that hole was magnificent. Racked, labelled, in rows, bought by a man who had thought hard about all of it. Kell had the folder of receipts out as well and it came to something over eleven thousand dollars, and dollars had been nothing for three years, so the number was only a way of saying how much had been cared about.

And not one item in it had been maintained. The lamps had their cells left in them and the cells had wept out into the housings and crusted on the contacts, so they were dead with nothing whatever wrong with the lamps. The canvas had been folded away damp twelve years earlier and came apart in my hands in sheets, like wet bread. The fuel had turned to varnish in the cans.

The only thing I carried out that I still have is the inventory list, laminated, in somebody's small careful handwriting. I have been writing on the back of it in pencil for twenty-odd years — what I actually carry, scratched out and put back — and the back is the front now. This chapter is what earns a place on the back of that card.

## Rent, in Grams

Here is the whole principle and the rest is bookkeeping. **Every object you own is a loan against your future strength, and it pays rent in grams** — taken off you every day you carry it, including all the days you do not use it. So the question is never whether a thing is useful, because everything is useful. A thing earns its weight three ways: it does what your hands cannot, it does more than one job, or it saves you carrying something heavier. Anything doing none of those is luggage.

I am not going to improve on chapter 2's test: put the loaded bag on, carry it up one flight of stairs and down again, and leave behind whatever you took out at the top. And chapter 4 has the two figures, which are easy to mix up, so keep them straight. **A fifth of your body weight is what you can walk under day after day without coming apart**, and **a third is where degradation starts to show** — slower, clumsier, worse at everything you need to be good at. The third is a cliff edge and not a plan.

## The Six That Always Earn It

Six categories, not six objects. The categories are fixed; what goes in them is whatever you can get.

1. **A container you can put on a fire.** Metal, lidded, as near one piece as you can manage. It boils water, which chapter 4 says is the stage everybody skips, and it cooks, carries and measures. A container that cannot go on a fire is a bag with ambitions.
2. **A cutting edge.** One, good, sharp, and the same one always, so your hand knows it. Chapter 2 is right that a knife is the tool that makes other tools.
3. **Cordage.** More than you think, because it is what everybody runs out of, and good cord is slow to make and quick to spend. Carry one long piece and a dozen short ones, and keep the short ones reachable without undoing the long one.
4. **A means of making fire that needs no fuel of its own.** A struck spark off a rod does it thousands of times and does it wet, which no match will and no lighter will once its fuel has gone off. And what you strike it into has to be dry, which means wood still standing — a dead branch snapped off at head height — rather than anything off the floor, because the floor is where the water is.
5. **Light.** Light turns a night of sitting still into a night of working, and hands-free light into a night of working with both hands. Be honest about what runs it: a lamp that eats cells is on a clock running out everywhere at once, and a wick in fat is not.
6. **A way to carry water that is not the pot.** The pot makes water safe; this moves it. More than one vessel, because one container is one failure away from none — and chapter 4's answer on how much is that you carry to the next water, not for three days.

## Two Methods, Not Two Objects

I carried two lamps for a year once, because somebody told me one is none, and I was pleased with myself about it. Same lamp, bought the same week, on the same cells, used the same amount. They went out four days apart in the same November, and I spent that winter being handed other people's light.

**Rule 6. Two methods, not two objects.** Two of the same thing fail of the same thing, because they share the weakness that is coming for both: the same age, the same fuel, the same water in the same seam. Doubling an object doubles the weight and does almost nothing to the chance of having none.

So pair unlike things. A lamp and a stub of candle. A rod and a few matches sealed dry in a tin. A metal pot and a tin can with a wire through it. A bottle and a bladder. Chapter 2's spare light on different cells is this rule with one foot in the door; the whole of it is that the spare should not be a lamp. And the second method is allowed to be worse — lighter, dumber, more annoying — because its job is not to match the first but to fail of something else.

## Rust, Rot, and the Other Half of Owning Anything

Nearly everything I have ever picked up off a dead man's shelf failed of neglect and not of use. Worn-out gear is rare and I am always pleased to see it; seized, rusted, rotted, perished, leaked-in gear is most of what is lying about. That is my tally and not a measurement, but it is twenty years of tallies and it is not close.

**Edges.** Hone a little and often rather than a lot and late, because every time you bring a properly dull edge back you take off steel that does not come back. A blade touched up for a minute a week outlasts one ground twice a year, and it cuts the whole time instead of a fortnight either side of the grinding.

**Rust.** Iron needs water and air and nothing else, so dry it, wipe it, and keep the thinnest film of oil or clean fat on it. **Do not store a blade in a leather sheath** — leather holds damp against the steel, and what the tanning left in it works on the metal. Take a bloom of rust off while it is still a bloom, because once it pits the pit is permanent, and a pit is where the thing will one day break.

**Canvas, rope and leather.** Rot is alive, it eats the cloth itself, and it needs only damp and time, so nothing goes away wet or folded damp — dried through, in air, then folded. Sun costs you too, quietly: a season standing in direct sunlight takes real strength out of cloth and rope without changing how either looks, which is why the tent that stood all summer tears in the autumn. Wax the canvas, feed the leather, and check the stitching first, because thread goes before cloth.

**Rubber.** It hardens and cracks from the air and the light, first and worst wherever it has been held stretched, so store it slack, dark, cool and away from a running motor. Tyres and belting were given something like six to ten years when there was anybody to ask, and after that the tread tells you nothing useful.

**Cells.** Take the batteries out of anything you are not using today, because a cell left in runs itself down and then leaks, and what comes out crusts the contacts. Store them cool, dry and apart from what they run. The good dry ones were sold as keeping years rather than decades, and anything you find now is past whatever date was on the side of it.

**Fuel.** The shortest-lived thing in any cache, and it dies quietly. Petrol goes gummy in months rather than years even sealed, and stops being able to start an engine long before it stops being able to burn down a shed. Diesel keeps longer and then grows things in the water at the bottom of the drum. Lamp oil keeps best — which is the argument for method four: the fire you can rely on in ten years never needed a liquid.

The best kit I ever saw in one place was in that hole under the garage, and the man who built it never came back for any of it, which is the only reason I know what twelve years does to folded canvas. **Rule 55. Gear you do not maintain is gear you do not have.** You own half an object when you buy it. The other half is a standing job, and the job pays in nothing happening.

## Can You Mend It

Three tests before you take a thing on, and almost nobody applies them while there is still a choice. **Can you see how it comes apart?** Screws, stitches, pins and rivets are an invitation; moulded, glued and sealed means the maker decided you would buy another one, and there is no another one. **Does it fail gradually or all at once?** A fraying rope and a thinning sole tell you in advance and let you pick your afternoon, where a sealed thing works perfectly and then does not, usually on the day it mattered. **Is it made of what is still being made?** Steel, cloth, cord, wood and wire still come out of somebody's shed; anything whose working part is a sealed cell or a ground lens you are borrowing from a world that stopped.

So carry the means of mending what you carry — a needle, waxed thread, a small file, a length of wire, a patch of canvas and a patch of rubber — because a repair kit is the only gear that makes other gear lighter: it is how one of a thing becomes enough.

## What Is On the Back of the Card

A quart pot with a lid and a wire bail. A knife and a small file. An awl, which is the most useful thing in the bag and the thing nobody asks about. My glasses. A spare lace. A long coil and a dozen short lengths. A rod, and a tin with matches and a candle stub in it. A lamp. Two bottles and a bladder. Needle, thread, wire, two patches. A hat.

The glasses have the left lens cracked right across, so anything bright comes through it as a smear. The hat is not vanity: I keep the brim between that lens and wherever the light is coming from, and I work with the lamp beside me or behind me and never in front. That is the whole of what can be done about it and it took one evening to work out.

That is the list, and it is embarrassingly little. Laid out flat it fits on a coat with room to spare, and people who watch me lay it out ask what else I have. That is what else I have.

> **— T.** That is the children's list. Container, edge, cord, fire, light, water, in that order — it is the first thing a child in this country is taught to pack, and most of us are taught it before we can read it back. I have mine in a drawer, in my own hand, aged seven, and I have now checked it against his page twice. The order is his order. The only difference I can find is that mine has no glasses on it. He calls the list embarrassing. I would like it on the record that it works.

It took twenty years to get the list down to that, and nearly all of it went on learning what some other object was for and then finding something already on the card did it, a bit worse, for no extra grams.

## What It Costs You

Maintenance costs half an hour a day for the rest of your life and shows you nothing for it. The whole return is an absence: the thing works, the way it worked yesterday. Nobody has ever thanked anybody for that, which is why most people stop.

Carrying little costs comfort, and I will not pretend otherwise. I am colder and wetter than I need to be, and I have sat out rain that a sheet of canvas would have ended, on the grounds that I was not carrying the canvas up the next four hills to find out.

Two methods costs you the better version of one: the candle is worse than the lamp in every way and still takes its grams, and twice a year I resent it and once every few years it is the only light I have.

Buying what you can mend costs performance, because the mendable thing is usually the heavier, slower, uglier thing. And a small kit makes other people's kit your problem: you are visibly the man who did not bring one, so you get handed the wrong tool and you borrow more than you lend.

## The Short Version

- Gear is a loan against your future strength, paying rent in grams every day, including the days you never touch it. Ask what it does that nothing else in the bag does.
- A fifth of your body weight is what you walk under day after day; a third is where degradation starts to show. Rule 23: pack for the stairs, not the road.
- Six categories always earn it: a container you can boil in, a cutting edge, cordage, a means of fire needing no fuel, light, and a way to carry water.
- Rule 6: two methods, not two objects. Identical spares share one weakness and go together, so pair a lamp with a candle, a rod with sealed matches, a bottle with a bladder.
- Rule 55: gear you do not maintain is gear you do not have. Nearly everything abandoned failed of neglect, not use — rust, rot, perished rubber, leaked cells.
- Hone edges often and lightly rather than heavily and late, because reground steel does not come back. Keep steel dry and thinly oiled, and never store a blade in leather, which holds damp against it.
- Put nothing away wet or folded damp, because rot eats the cloth itself and needs only damp and time. A season in direct sun weakens canvas and rope without changing their look.
- Take cells out of anything not in use today, or a flat one leaks and crusts the contacts. Store rubber slack and dark. Fuel dies soonest: petrol in months, lamp oil longest.
- Buy what you can mend: visible fastenings, failure you can see coming, materials somebody still makes. Carry needle, waxed thread, a file, wire and two patches.
