# Chapter 9: Scavenging Is a Profession

> Rule 21: Stockroom, not showroom.

Amateurs go to the front of the shop. The front is where things were kept for being looked at, which is why it has been empty for years. The stockroom is where they were kept for being stored, the maintenance closet is where the man who kept the building running kept his things, and the roof is where the plant was bolted down. In twenty years I have very rarely found anybody ahead of me in any of the three. That is not courage. It is method, and method can be taught in an afternoon.

I am qualified because of one autumn in my sixth year in which I went through two roofs. The first went at the valley where a flat extension met the main pitch, where the sheeting had been ponding water for a decade and the joists under it had gone soft as wet bread. The second I went through a fortnight later, having decided the first roof had taught me everything a roof could teach me, so I crossed the middle of the span instead of walking the line of nails, which is where the joists are; the joist I was not standing on held perfectly, and I went in to the shoulder and hung by the armpits over somebody's stairwell.

I was fetching that autumn for Vera Ott, a mechanic, who had no patience with me whatsoever and the best list I have ever been handed. Nothing on it was exciting. It is why I know what is worth carrying.

## Nothing In There Is Worth Being Trapped In There With

Nobody is going to tell you whether a building is safe, so you read it from outside first, all four sides if the ground allows. None of the signs below is a measurement and I am not giving you one; they are things you can see. Two together means walk away, because there are a great many buildings.

- **Walls.** Sight up a wall from the corner the way you would sight a plank. One that bows, bulges or leans is not carrying its load straight down any more, which means something else is, and you do not know what.
- **Cracks.** Old hairline cracks, dirty and painted over, have usually finished moving. Stepped cracks out of the corners of doors and windows, cracks you can get a finger into, and cracks with clean edges or fresh dust under them have not.
- **The roof line.** A dipping ridge or a sagging roof has been letting water in for years, so assume everything under it is wet, including what holds the building up. My first roof looked fine from the street.
- **Fire.** A burned building is finished, whatever is in it. Char is wood that has left, so a beam is thinner than it looks and nobody can tell you by how much, and steel that sagged in a fire does not come back.
- **The ground.** Water standing against the base, soil washed from under a corner, a slab that has dropped. A building is only as good as what it stands on.

Inside, listen from the doorway before you put weight on anything; a building that creaks, drops dust or ticks is telling you to leave the way you came. Walk the edges of a floor, where the joists sit on their bearing, because a joist is strongest where it lands and weakest mid-span, and read the ceiling of the room below first, because a bulge, a sag or a brown stain is the best report on that floor you will get. Load the bottom tread before you trust a stair, keep your hand on the wall rather than the handrail, which is four screws into plaster, and go up one at a time. Never below ground level with a lamp or a fire until you have asked chapter 1's second question — **can I breathe where I am standing?** — and chapter 5 is the long version of why basements, pits and tanks kill people who felt fine going in. Do not search at night, and do not move debris that is visibly holding something up.

**Rule 31. Know the way out before you go in.** Two ways out where you can get them, wedged open, and somebody outside who knows which building you are in and when to come looking. Nothing in there is worth being trapped in there with.

## One Direction, One Room At A Time

A treasure hunt is a man walking into the middle of a floor and turning round. A sweep is boring and it is the whole job.

1. **Pick a direction at the door and never reverse it.** Left-hand wall, say: room to room along it, and when you are back at the door you are finished. Not for tidiness: in a building with no lights and all the doors alike, it is the only way to tell which rooms you have had.
2. **Finish a room before you open the next.** Half-searched rooms are how a sweep turns back into a treasure hunt.
3. **Round the walls one way, then the middle, then high, then low,** opening everything that opens and looking behind everything with a behind. High is the tops of cupboards, the backs of upper shelves, lintels and the ceiling hatch. Low is under everything, taped to undersides, the plinths, the floor of the cupboard under the sink.
4. **Carry out of a room before you open the next,** or stage it at the door, because a heap left on a floor is a heap you walk into in the dark.
5. **Mark the building on the way out.** Which is the next section, and the half people skip.

That autumn is also when we got into the Tarn Street cold store that same October. The stairs were cleared bottom to top, which is the only order there is, because clearing downward drives people into a stairwell with one end to it. Three of us had wet boots out of the flooded lift pit, and I have carried spare socks in the bag ever since, which is the most useful thing I took out of that building.

> **— T.** Eleven words. I have counted them twice: *we got into the Tarn Street cold store that same October*. What came out of Tarn Street that October fed four settlements through a winter, we call it the Opening, and children here are taught the date before their letters. There is no other account by anybody who was inside the building. There is this one. It gives the whole of it eleven words, and then it gives me the order of the stairs, and the lift pit, and whose boots were — no. I have begun this sentence four times and I am leaving it where it is. What he says about the stairs is right. Clear upward.

## Mark What You Clear

A mark at the door costs ten seconds and saves the next person an afternoon, and about a quarter of the time the next person is you, with no memory of the building at all. **Rule 47. Mark what you clear.**

Charcoal or chalk, at eye height, on the right-hand side of the door you would use, on a face the weather does not reach — in most streets the inside of the frame rather than the outside — and where there is no dry sheltered wood or stone, scratch it instead of drawing it. Three marks, and that is the whole alphabet.

- **One stroke, with your initial and the month under it.** I went in, I came out, the floor held, and nothing is left worth your daylight.
- **Two strokes crossed.** The building is bad — floor, stair or roof — and nobody should be inside it. Draw this one big, because it is doing a job for somebody you will never meet.
- **One stroke with a ring round it.** Somebody is living here. Leave it alone.

The initial and the month matter more than they look, because a sound floor in March is not a sound floor after a wet winter, and a building nobody was living in last year may have people in it now. I give a mark about a year, then read the building again and put the new mark beside the old one, because two marks a year apart tell the next person what one cannot.

## Where Nobody Has Been

The same five places, building after building, year after year.

- **Utility and janitorial spaces** — the cleaner's closet, the plant room, the boiler cupboard, the space under the stair. Nobody looted a mop store in the first year, because nothing in it was wanted then: wire, buckets, hose, soap, a stepladder, screws and a tap.
- **Vehicle trunks, and under the trunk floor.** People emptied the glove box and the seats and hardly ever lifted the boot floor, so the spare wheel well is the most reliable hole I know.
- **The backs of appliances** — hose with the fittings still on it, hose clips, a copper tail, bearings, a motor, sheet steel you can cut.
- **Pharmacy stockrooms rather than shelves.** The shelves went in the first week, to people after one specific thing; the stock sat in boxes in the back room, with the dressings and the sachets for rehydrating children.
- **Office first-aid cabinets** — a green box on a wall by a door, in a building nobody thinks of as having anything in it. Dressings, tape, gloves, eye wash, a blanket. Chapter 3 is what they are for.

## What A Thing Is Worth In The Third Year

In year one everybody takes food, drink and the shiny thing. By year three the food is gone or ruined and what is left matters in a different order. Mine, roughly in order.

**Fasteners.** Screws, bolts, nails, nuts, washers, hose clips. You cannot make a screw and no thread you cut by hand will fit anything, so everything you build for the rest of your life is held together by somebody's dropped box of them.

**Containers that hold liquid.** Cans, drums, bottles — anything with an intact gasket and a lid that seals. Carrying water is the whole of some days, and the can is worth more than whatever is left in it, so check the gasket and the threads, not the outside.

**Intact rubber hose and belting.** Chapter 8 is the long version of what air and light do to rubber, so you will reject most of what you find: flex it hard and watch for cracks opening across the bend.

**Medical supplies.** Dressings, tape, gauze, gloves, soap: all light, and all in the two places above.

**Salt.** **Rule 19. Salt is money.** It is also the only preservative you will find in quantity, it keeps indefinitely, and nobody took it in the first year because there was still salt in the shops.

**Needles and thread.** Nobody takes the needles. I have walked into houses stripped back to the plaster, the stair gone and the pipework out of the walls, and found the sewing tin where it lived. A needle mends the coat, the bag, the boot and the tent, it weighs nothing, and nobody is making more. Take all of them.

## Who You Take From

I will keep this short, because I do not enjoy being lectured either.

I take from the dead. There is a great deal of it, it does nobody any harm, and the man whose bracket I opened my shin on in chapter 8 had been dead twelve years.

I do not take from the living, and not as a kindness. A place being used has somebody coming back to it, and taking from them buys you an enemy with local knowledge, in a landscape where the only thing anybody has is who will open a gate for them.

I do not strip a place a family is still using, even when they are out. Washing on a line, a cleared path, a mended fence, animals, smoke, a tap that works: that is somebody's house, the ring mark goes on the frame and I go elsewhere. Chapter 7 is how you read that from outside.

And I leave water. A sealed bottle on the step, where it can be seen from the door, in any building I have searched and marked. It costs me a litre, and twice I have come back to a building I marked in a bad week and been glad the bottle was still there.

## What It Costs You

Method costs you speed, visibly. The man with the television is down the street while you are still doing the janitor's closet, and he looks like he is winning. He is not, but the arithmetic takes about two years to show up.

Marking costs you cover: it says somebody works this street, and which way he was going. I pay it, because the alternative is re-searching my own buildings and leaving a stranger to find out about a bad floor with his weight already on it. That is my judgement and not a fact, and somebody in worse company might weigh it the other way.

The structural rules cost you the best haul in any town. The building with the sagging ridge has everything still in it, precisely because everybody before you read it the same way and went home, and one day you will stand outside one and want it badly. I have been inside two and have the roofs to show for it, and was lucky both times. Lucky is not a method.

## The Short Version

- Rule 21: stockroom, not showroom. The front of a building was emptied in the first month; the stockroom, the maintenance closet, the plant room and the roof usually were not.
- Read a building from outside first: leaning or bulging walls, stepped cracks with clean edges, a dipping ridge, fire damage, ground washed from under a corner. Two signs together, walk away.
- Inside, walk the edges of floors where the joists bear, read the ceiling below before walking the floor above, load the bottom tread before trusting a stair, and go up one at a time.
- Rule 31: know the way out before you go in. Two exits wedged open, somebody outside who knows which building you are in, no night searching, nothing below ground until you have asked whether you can breathe where you are standing.
- Sweep, do not hunt: one direction chosen at the door and never reversed, one room finished before the next, and in each room the walls, then the middle, then high, then low.
- Rule 47: mark what you clear on sheltered wood or stone at eye height — one stroke cleared, two crossed unsafe, one ringed occupied — with your initial and the month under it.
- Reliably unsearched: janitorial and plant rooms, vehicle trunks and the spare wheel well beneath them, the backs of appliances, pharmacy stockrooms rather than shelves, office first-aid cabinets.
- By the third year the value order is fasteners, containers that seal, uncracked rubber hose, dressings, salt by Rule 19, and needles and thread, which nobody else takes, so take them all.
- Take from the dead and not the living, leave a place a family is still using, which chapter 7 teaches you to read from outside, and leave a sealed bottle of water on the step.
