# Chapter 18: On the Manufacture of Gods

> Rule 73: A god with no chore attached is a story.

In my eleventh year I carried a crate of lamp glass up to the store and was told to stop there in case anything else wanted shifting, so I stood at the back wall for four hours with the crate in my arms, there being nowhere to set it down that was not somebody's chair.

Tobin Arr came in first, having the key. Then Lise Hadden, down off the second terrace, already annoyed about the barrels and saying so before she sat. Then the Bellwright, who asked for the door to be shut and then asked twice more, the latch being a thing I had been meaning to see to. Then Nessa Quill, last, charcoal to the elbow.

The foundry was the business, and nobody would take it: two shifts and a banked fire fed in the night, cold work on your own in the dark with nothing to show in the morning but that the thing is still warm. Its rota had been written four times and had failed four times. Lise said two years of telling people it mattered was enough and could they try something else. Nessa said she was not doing a fifth winter of it on her own. By the second hour the crate had got heavier and I had it on my hip.

The story part I have put further down, it being a procedure. What came out of the afternoon was a name, a rota under it, a short piece to say at the start of a shift, and a mark for the door. I painted the mark and wrote the rest up that evening under *foundry rota*, the heading it is still under in my book.

Inside the month the foundry had nine people on it and an argument about who got the night ones, and it has not gone cold in the thirty years since, so the rota was well designed. The rest of this chapter is how they designed it; they said every bit of it out loud in front of me and I was holding a crate and listening.

> **— T.** The ninth of the eighth month is her day. Every forge and kitchen between here and the water goes cold from dusk to dusk and is lit again at first light from one carried flame; the children sing the one about the hand in the fire, and its second verse is the cold country; and there is not a living soul in this country who doubts that she made the world, holds it together, and will let go of it if we stop feeding her. I am obliged to set down beside all of that the only account of her making that exists. It is the minute of a long meeting about a foundry rota, taken by a man holding a crate. Who was *holding a crate*. I have sat at this desk for— no. I do not know what to do with this page. I shall copy it exactly as he wrote it, because I am not qualified to do one other thing to it.

## What A God Is For

Three things, and these are what they talked about first.

**It keeps a chore from lapsing.** A rota holds while the person who wrote it stands in the yard, and about a winter after. Tobin Arr said it while he was looking for a pencil: **a god with no chore attached is a story.** Rule 73. The chore is the point; the god is what makes it outlive the person who cared whether it got done. That rota had failed four times, and the fifth has had thirty years out of it.

**It takes a rule out of a person's hands.** Lise Hadden said this one twice, the second time slowly, the Bellwright not having looked up: **a rule with a name on it goes when the name does.** Rule 66. A rule belonging to a person is argued with them, excepted for them when they are tired, and buried with them. That rota had the Bellwright's name on it, so everybody wanting off a night shift went to him, and about half got off it. I took the point for a point about the rota. It is a point about the rota.

**It gives fear a shape.** In a bad year people are frightened all day with nothing to do about it, and a thing you do with your hands at a stated time, that finishes, turns some of that into something that finishes. Why that works I could not tell you, and I would not trust anybody who said they were certain. What I have is a great many frightened people stood about, and the ones with a job at a fixed hour were easier to be near, myself included.

## The Five Things They Settled

1. **Attach it to a practice that has to happen anyway.** With no chore under it you have made a story, and a story will not get a furnace fed at three in the morning. Pick the job nobody will take and that the place dies without — the fire, the water, the wall, the seed — and build on that and nothing else.
2. **A domain narrow enough to be useful and wide enough to matter.** Mother Furnace got fire, metal and anything that has to be kept going; not the weather, the dead or the harvest. Narrow is not modesty. A god with everything in its domain has nothing particular to ask you for, and what you wanted was somebody banking a fire.
3. **Demands that are cheap, frequent and physical.** A minute, with your hands, at the start of a shift, every shift. Not money, not a day, not going without, nothing anybody ever has to be excused from — because the moment a demand is big enough that somebody has to be let off it, somebody is being let off it. You will hear that the dearer a demand is the harder it binds people; I have watched that go both ways and go wrong oftener than it went right, and would not build on it. Cheap and daily got that furnace fed and dear and rare never did. And physical, because a thing you only think gets thought instead of done. Say it and do something. Theirs was a flat hand on the furnace door and six words, and then you work.
4. **An origin story that is short, can be seen, and can be said by a child.** That is the next section.
5. **A form that is an object and not a face.** Mother Furnace is a wheel and not a woman, and that took a minute: somebody said a face would have had to look like somebody, and nobody wanted to be in the room when that got decided. Six spokes and a furnace door in the hub. I painted the first of them, nine inches across on the store door and the foundry gate, in rust and ochre ground into boiled oil, which is what holds on timber.

## The Story Part, Which Took The Longest

Three hours of the four went on this, and I thought we were waiting for Nessa to go back on shift.

**Three beats.** Three, because three is what a person holds and gives back in the right order. Hers are: she came out of a cold country; she put her hand into a dead furnace and it lit; she said she would stay as long as it was fed. About eleven seconds, said out loud. Anything you add is a fourth beat, and the fourth beat comes off in the retelling, and it will be the one you liked.

**One impossible detail.** The hand in the fire. Nobody can check it and nobody tries, and it tells a person at once that this is a story and not something that happened to somebody's uncle last spring, which people will argue with. Why that stops the arguing I could not tell you. It stops it.

**One true detail somebody can walk up to and touch.** The inside of the foundry's north wall is scorched at about shoulder height, in a patch roughly the size of a hand, scorched years before any of this, off a spill. Nessa Quill said to use it. That patch is why the rest goes down; one checkable thing makes a different animal of a story.

**And a hole, left in on purpose.** They were the better part of an hour on it and ended by deciding to have none. Nobody says what the cold country was, or why she left it. A story people can add to becomes theirs, and a story with no room in it stays the property of whoever said it first. I did not see why it mattered. Four places I have been in since tell the cold country differently, and all at length.

## What It Costs You

**You cannot take it back.** That is first because it is the one with no remedy under it. A rota can be rewritten, a mark painted over, a bad decision about the barrels undone at the next meeting by the same four people in the same room. There is no procedure anywhere for standing a god down. I have never seen it done, I have never heard of it being done, and I have been in eleven groups. So whatever gets settled in your afternoon is settled for people who will never hear your name, and you get the one afternoon. The nearest thing to a remedy I know of is the narrow domain, and that is why it is on the list twice: a place that stops needing that one chore can let a narrow god go quiet, and a wide one has nothing it can be finished with.

**It will be read by people you will never meet, and some of them will read it in a way that would turn your stomach.** You get no vote on that. The hole is left in on purpose and the hole is what makes the story theirs, and theirs means theirs. I have been in four places since that keep Mother Furnace. In one of them the minute at the start of a shift has become the better part of an hour, which is an hour nobody is working. In another it is said over children, which was not in the afternoon, and I cannot point at one person whose fault that is. In a third, her day is a day nobody works, which is the reverse of the whole point of her, and nobody there could know that. Four valleys keep her and no two keep her the same, and that is the hole doing exactly what it was left in to do rather than a thing that has gone wrong.

**Somebody will eventually claim to speak for it, and that person is the problem and not the god.** It does not want a liar, either, and you will not catch it by listening, because what is being said will be correct. It wants somebody who is genuinely good at saying the words, in a place where saying the words has quietly come to count as having done a shift. So watch the rota and not the saying. The question is whether the people who say the words are on the rota under their own names and take the night ones in their turn, and whether the words get said by whoever is on shift that day rather than by one person on everybody's behalf. **Rule 77** from chapter 15 is the test and it is the same test — a rule that stops applying to one person has stopped — and a god is a rule with nobody left to appeal to. And chapter 7 is the long version of leaving, which is what this cost eventually costs somebody. It costs them more here than anywhere else in this book, because a person who has stopped believing a group's god is in a worse position than one who has stopped liking its rota, and chapter 7 is written for the second. For the first I have no better advice than the same advice, taken earlier: name the thing that means you go, and a day to look at it again, before you are in.

**The distance between a god that asks you to feed a furnace and one that asks you to do as you are told is one generation.** Nobody turns it, which is why it is worth writing down: the people who come second inherit the practice without the problem the practice was for. The furnace is warm; it has been warm since before they were born; nobody living remembers it cold, and so the reason goes and the demand stays, and a demand with nothing under it is sitting there for anybody who wants a use for it. If you build one of these, the thing to write down and keep somewhere findable is not the words. It is the chore, and why. The words will look after themselves.

The Ninth Hand had one built on every one of the five things above, and built well: the words were good, the demands were daily, the story had its hole in it and people filled the hole in. Somebody made different choices, and I could not give you a word of difference between the theology of the two, because I never listened properly to either. I swept up after both. At the foundry the people who said the words were on the rota under their own names and took the night shifts in their turn, and I know that because I swept the same floor they did, on the shift after theirs. At the Ninth Hand I swept the cistern yard for two years and the people who said the words did not sweep anything. That is the whole of the difference, and it is a difference anybody can check with a broom.

> **— T.** I shall say this once and then be quiet. There is a man two valleys from here who is called her mouth, and he has a good face and a better voice, and he has never in his life carried a basket of charcoal across a yard. The third thing on this list is him. I have met him; I was glad to be out of the room; and I have nothing to add to this section and nothing to amend in it, because the test the man has set down is the only one there is, and it is not a thing you perform upon the god. It is a thing you check about the people who serve her. Feed the furnace. Watch the rota.

Nessa Quill said the thing I would leave you with and she said it with one arm in her coat and the other half out of the door, because it had run four hours and she was back on shift. Build a god that asks something of you first.

## The Short Version

- Rule 73: a god with no chore attached is a story. Start from the job nobody will take and that the place dies without, and build on that and nothing else. Telling people it matters gets you about a winter; the same chore with something outside any living person behind it got a foundry fire banked for thirty years.
- Rule 66: a rule with a name on it goes when the name does. Move a duty's authority off a person and it stops being argued with them, excepted for them, and buried with them.
- Keep the domain narrow and the demands cheap, frequent and physical: a minute, with your hands, at a stated time, every time, that nobody ever has to be excused from. The excusing is where it goes wrong. Dearer demands are said to bind harder; I have seen that go both ways and would not build on it.
- Make the form an object, not a face. A face has to look like somebody, and then somebody has decided who.
- Build the story in four parts: three beats and no more, one impossible detail nobody can check, one true detail somebody can walk up to and touch, one hole left in on purpose. The hole is what makes the story theirs instead of yours, so other people filling it in is it working, not failing.
- Write down the chore and the reason for it, somewhere findable. The words survive on their own; the reason is what goes, and a demand with no reason under it is there for anybody who wants a use for it.
- You cannot take one back, and it will be read by people you will never meet in ways you would hate. Somebody will eventually claim to speak for it, and that person, not the god, is the problem. The test is the rota: are the ones who say the words on it, under their own names, taking the night shifts in their turn. Rule 77. Chapter 7 is the long version of leaving and this is the hardest place in the book to need it.
- The difference between the two I swept up after was never theology; it was whether the god's demands cost its priests anything. Build a god that asks something of you first.
