# Chapter 5: Two Ways Weather Kills You

> Rule 16: Wet is colder than cold.

For two years I believed cold killed people by being cold, so the thermometer was what I watched and anything well above freezing was not a problem. I was wrong in a very common way.

I got hypothermic at four degrees above freezing. Marta Lunn had a thermometer wired to her bag and that is what it said. I had spent the morning to the waist in a ditch in steady rain, soaked through to the shirt, and by midday I was sitting out of the wind, eating, in an extremely good mood. No gale, no snow: a man having his lunch.

The bread was difficult to tear. I remember being pleased about the ditch, and somebody taking my coat off me, and then a floor and a shirt that was not mine. I do not know who got me dry. That is not modesty: not knowing who is in front of you is one of the symptoms.

Four degrees above freezing, wet, sitting still, cheerful: that is where people who think they understand hypothermia get it. Cold air takes heat off you slowly. Water conducts heat about twenty-five times better than air, which is why a wet layer takes it off you several times faster than a dry one — and it does that whether the wet came from the sky or from your own back. Wind gets there by stripping off the thin shell of already-warmed air your clothes are holding, which is the only thing any clothing does. So the readings that matter are not the temperature: whether you are wet, and whether the air is moving.

Which brings me to mats: the ground is worse than either.

## Loft, Layers, and the Ground

Insulation is not a material. It is trapped air, and everything else is a way of holding some.

So thickness is the thing, not weight, and a crushed coat keeps you nothing. Three loose layers beat one thick one because you can take them off one at a time: thin against the skin to move damp away, loft in the middle, a shell outside to keep wind and rain out of it. Shed one *before* you sweat, not after, because sweat is how you get wet on a day when it is not raining. Cover your head, because it is the part usually left bare and one you cannot easily shut the blood supply down to.

Now the ground, which is where the drain comes back in. Still air is a poor conductor, which is why loft works. The ground is a good one, it touches the whole length of you, and it never tires. Worse, the loft underneath you is crushed flat by your own weight all night, so the underside of a bag does almost nothing. **Something under you before something over you.**

A hand's depth of dry material, compressed, is the minimum: leaves, bracken, straw, a tarpaulin over boughs. Two hands' depth over snow or frozen ground, and more if it is wet, because wet packing is only the ground higher up. Wider than you are, because you will roll; shoulders to hips at least, head to knees if you can. Build it before dark, first job and not last, because it is the job a tired man skips and the one that was keeping him alive.

## Reading It on Somebody

Hypothermia arrives in order.

Fine movement goes first, so you fumble: buckles, laces, a tin lid. Then walking gets untidy and speech mumbles. And somewhere in there the mood changes — flat and withdrawn, or short-tempered, or oddly delighted about a drain — and the decisions go wrong, which is the dangerous part, because a cold person will take a coat off or wander away with an excellent reason. All of it while they are still shivering hard.

Then the shivering stops, and this is the thing to carry out of this chapter. **Shivering stopping is not improvement.** Shivering is the body's own fire and it burns fuel to run; it ends either because you have got them genuinely warm — which you will know, because they will be warm and making sense — or because the body has run out. Stiff, slow, confused and no longer shivering is worse than shivering violently, and the person will tell you they are fine, or that they are hot. Believe the shivering and the speech, never the opinion.

You cannot run this on yourself, because judgement goes too: agree beforehand that the first to fumble twice stops the work.

## Warming Somebody Up Without Hurting Them

Gently, and from the middle outwards.

1. **Out of the wind and out of the wet.** Wet clothing all comes off, which outranks modesty, because soaked cloth keeps taking heat the whole time it is on.
2. **Something under them, then everything wrapped, head included.** Before blankets, before anything, with something that does not breathe outside the lot to hold the warm damp air in.
3. **Heat on the trunk, not the limbs.** Chest, armpits, sides of the neck, groin. Warm, not hot, through cloth and never against the skin, because cold skin cannot feel a burn arriving. Warming arms and legs first opens the cold blood standing in them and sends it to the middle, which drops them further and can stop the heart. Same reason there is no hot bath for anybody who has stopped shivering or making sense.
4. **Warm sweet drinks only if they are fully awake** and can hold the cup and swallow. Sugar is fuel for the shivering. A confused person chokes.
5. **No rubbing, slapping, massaging the limbs or walking them about.** Both reasons matter at three in the morning when it feels like you should be doing something: it drives that cold blood inward, and a very cold heart is twitchy, so rough handling can stop it. And frozen flesh is full of ice, so rubbing tears it from the inside.
6. **No alcohol, and this one you will be offered.** It feels like warmth because it opens the vessels in the skin, and what you feel is heat leaving faster. It also blunts the shivering and the judgement, the two things you want back.

And flesh that has frozen hard all the way through is a separate injury from the cold that caused it: chapter 12 is the long version — what to rewarm, how warm, and why thawing something you cannot keep thawed costs more than leaving it frozen.

And a very cold person can look dead: look and listen for breathing far longer than feels necessary before concluding anything, and keep them wrapped and gently handled while you do. Rewarming takes hours, not minutes, so do not call it done because they have perked up and asked for their boots.

## The Other Direction

Heat kills in two forms, and telling them apart is the thing to memorise: the treatments are not variations of each other.

**Heat exhaustion.** Sweating heavily, skin pale and cool and clammy, pulse fast and weak, breathing fast, headache, dizziness, feeling sick, cramp in the big muscles. And — the part that defines it — **they are still in there.** They know you and they answer sensibly. Stop the work: shade, flat out, clothing loosened, wet them, air moving over them, fluids sipped. Better inside half an hour; if not, treat it as the other thing.

**Heat stroke.** Defined by **altered mental state**: confused, slurred, agitated, staggering, fitting, or unconscious. The skin is hot, and it may be bone dry or soaking, which is why sweat is not the test — somebody who goes down working hard in the heat is usually still pouring with it, and the dry-skin version of this rule kills people by reassuring them. This is the emergency. Cool them now, before anything else and before moving them: into cold water up to the neck if there is any, and failing that, strip them, soak them and fan them continuously, cold to the neck, armpits and groin, re-wetting constantly, because water left sitting on skin stops working. Nothing by mouth while they are confused. Keep cooling until they make sense, then stop, dry them, keep them shaded and watch, because they can go off again.

Most heat casualties are a timetable problem. Work the cool hours and sleep the hot ones, lying still in shade through the worst three or four hours of the afternoon. Shade from above with air moving under it beats a wall, and a tent or a vehicle in sun is an oven for hours. Drink ahead of thirst — Rule 9 — and eat with your water when sweating hard all day, because sweat takes salt out of you as well as water and water alone does not put it back. Come at hot work gradually over a week or two; the body adapts, but not in one afternoon.

## Shelter Is a Heat Problem

Everybody's first shelter is too big. Mine slept nine, took eleven days, was beautifully made, and never got warm once. I ended up sleeping by the door on the ground. That is how bad the building was, and it says nothing good about the ground.

A shelter is not architecture. It is a box you heat with your own body, and a person sitting still puts out roughly a hundred watts: nothing for a hall, quite a lot for a box. So: **Rule 34. Build small. Heat is the rent.** Big enough to lie down in and sit up in and not a hand's breadth more, floor insulated as above, entrance small and offset from the wind, ventilated low and high, always: a fire in a box that small is the worst case in this book.

## Fire, and Why It Should Be Small

Fire is five grades of fuel, each one's job being to light the next, and the skill is gathering all five before you strike anything: tinder that catches off a spark, kindling the thickness of a match, then a pencil, then thumb-thick, then wrist-thick. Take more of each than you think, particularly the fine grades, because the standard way a fire dies is a man going for the next size up while it is dying.

Then keep it small, and the real reason is fuel: every log is somebody's walk, and a big fire eats a whole day, forever.

A fire indoors is a different animal. I have lost more people to enclosed fires than to the cold they were escaping, so the flue is the first thing and the fire is the second. Carbon monoxide has no colour, no smell and no taste, and anything burning makes it — most of all anything burning badly: a starved stove, a brazier, an engine, and charcoal, which is the worst and must never be lit indoors. What it does to you is a headache, then sickness, then weakness, then confusion, then an enormous sleepiness. Read that list again, because it is exactly the list for a cold, wet, exhausted person at the end of a hard day. That is why it kills indoors rather than out: the symptoms are indistinguishable from the reason you came inside, so the person feeling them lies down, which is also the last decision they make.

So: an opening low for air in, an opening high for smoke out, both unblocked, and still unblocked at three in the morning when somebody is cold. Never charcoal, never a brazier, never an engine or generator in a room or a doorway. A headache indoors with a fire burning is enough on its own. One person is enough: get out into open air. Two at once and everybody goes, including whoever feels fine, and the place is aired before anyone goes back. This is chapter 1's second question — *can I breathe where I am standing?*

## What It Costs You

Cold costs you fuel and daylight, permanently. Somebody fetches wood every day for the rest of their life, and in a bad week that is wood instead of work that would have improved your position.

Wet costs you a standing job. Drying clothes and bedding is daily work, needs a fire and somebody turning things, and a camp that does not dry its things gets colder for reasons it blames on the weather.

Heat costs you half the day: everything takes twice as long and the hard jobs get done tired. There is no pushing through it; that version is in the paragraph about altered mental state.

And ventilation costs you heat, deliberately, every night, forever. That is the hardest bill here, because paying it feels exactly like stupidity: letting out the warmth you just spent a day's wood making. Pay it anyway. The people who stopped paying it are the ones above, and they were all warm.

## The Short Version

- Rule 16: wet is colder than cold. Water conducts heat about twenty-five times better than air, so wet clothing takes heat several times faster than dry, and wind strips the warmed air away. Soaked at four degrees above freezing will do it.
- Insulation is trapped air, so thickness is everything: nothing tight or crushed, damp-moving inside, loft in the middle, windproof outside. Vent before you sweat. A shelter is the same problem and you are the heater: Rule 34, build small, heat is the rent.
- Something under you before something over you: the ground takes heat faster than air, and your weight crushes the loft beneath. A hand's depth of dry packing, two over snow, shoulders to hips at least.
- Hypothermia is fumbling hands, stumbling, mumbling, a changed mood, bad decisions. Then the shivering stops, and that is worse, not better, especially with confusion. They will say they are fine.
- Rewarm gently: wet clothes off, insulation under and over, head covered, gentle warmth to chest, armpits, neck and groin, warm sweet drinks only if fully awake and swallowing.
- No alcohol — it opens the skin, and the warmth you feel is heat leaving. No rubbing or walking them about: it drives cold blood inward, and rough handling can stop a very cold heart.
- Heat exhaustion — sweating, pale, clammy, still making sense — is shade, rest, cooling, fluids. Heat stroke is altered mental state: cool hard now, cold water if you have it, otherwise strip, soak and fan, re-wetting. Nothing by mouth.
- Work the cool hours and lie still through the hot ones, under shade with air moving beneath it. Drink ahead of thirst, Rule 9, and eat with your water when sweating all day.
- Carbon monoxide is odourless: its headache, confusion and sleepiness are what a cold tired person feels. Ventilate indoor fires low and high; no charcoal indoors. One headache indoors is enough to go out, two at once and everybody goes, including whoever feels fine.
