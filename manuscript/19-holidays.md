# Chapter 19: Holidays, Songs, and Other Load-Bearing Nonsense

> Rule 59: Put the holiday at the worst part of the year.

In my tenth year I carried eleven barrels up to the store for the Bell Country's first winter feast and dropped the ninth of them on my right foot in the doorway.

The feast was Lise Hadden's arithmetic. Two cellars had taken water in the September, a terrace of apples had come in bruised, and a quantity of food in that place was not going to see the spring whatever anybody did. Her position, said at the store door to whoever was passing, was that eating it in one evening was less waste than watching half of it go soft over three months. Nobody argued.

The barrel was a short one and about two-thirds full, and it came off my hip rather than out of my hands, which is worse, because a barrel off the hip arrives with the whole of itself. I got the boot off before the foot swelled, which is the one thing I did right, and spent the evening at the end of the table with my foot in a bucket. The nail came off a fortnight later and grew back wrong.

It was a very good evening. I had the seat nearest the door, so I missed most of what went on at the far end. What I have of it is the order the barrels came up in and somebody's dog under the table, not to be got out.

They did it again the next winter, and the one after. They are still doing it in places that never heard of the Bell Country, and I have eaten at four of them.

So: the days a group keeps, the songs it sings, the names it hands out, and what people swear. None of it mends a roof, and all four held places that had better reasons to come apart.

## A Year You Can Find Your Place In

A calendar's first job is telling people where they are, which sounds like nothing until you have wintered somewhere without one. A person who cannot say whether this is the hard middle of it or the tail end makes worse decisions about food and is frightened at the wrong times. Mark the year into named stretches with fixed days between and everybody has a position: this far in, this much left of it.

The second job is taking rest out of the negotiation. **Rule 61** from chapter 16 says a day off is maintenance, and maintenance gets argued every time it falls due, by tired people, against work that is visibly waiting. A day with a name on it is not argued. Nobody makes the case for the shortest day. The shortest day arrives.

The third I would not have believed. **Rule 59: put the holiday at the worst part of the year.** Not the slack week with plenty in the store, which is when everybody wants it and when it does least. Put it where the year is worst: the long end of winter, the fortnight when the pile has gone down and nothing has come up. That is the stretch people stop getting out of bed in, and a fixed day in the middle gives them a date to walk towards. The looking forward does as much work as the eating, which is also why it has to be cheap enough to keep in a bad year: the bad year is what it is for.

## Building One That Holds

Three things, and I have watched the lack of each kill one.

1. **Make it cheap.** One pot, one evening, whatever is going over anyway. That feast was made out of spoilage every year it ran, which is why it survived two bad years that cancelled everything else in the calendar. A day that costs a store has to be afforded, and what has to be afforded gets argued every year until the argument goes the other way.
2. **Let it be inherited, not announced.** Nobody instituted that feast. It happened, and happened again, and by the fourth time it had a shape nobody could remember deciding on. I have twice watched a holiday announced from the front of a room, with reasons, and both lasted two years. A day people have been handed is stronger than one they have been sold, and the only way I know to hand one on is to do it a few times and not explain it.
3. **Give it one piece of required nonsense that serves no purpose at all.** Every day that has lasted has one thing in it that cannot be defended: a food nobody likes, a thing said in an order, something carried round a yard. It is the load-bearing part.

Here is why, and I saw it in the barrels. Everything useful about that feast could be argued with, because everything useful about it had a reason, and a reason is a thing two people can disagree about. The food would spoil anyway — so somebody says the cellar is drier this year, and they may be right, and now you are having a meeting. It lifts people in a bad month — so somebody says we are busy and will do it in the spring. Every sensible part of a day has a sensible argument against it, and sensible arguments win in the end, because the people making them are not wrong.

The shoe has no reason in it. There is nothing to answer. You cannot tell somebody the shoe is inefficient, because they already know, and knowing was never what held it up. So the useless thing goes down the years untouched while the useful things get argued away one at a time, and it carries the evening on its back, and the evening carries everything else. If you are making one of these, put something stupid in it on purpose and do not write down why. There is no why. That is the whole mechanism.

The one at the winter feast is the shoe. Everybody takes one off at the door and comes in with one foot bare, and it was there the first year and every year since. Two people have told me it keeps the snow out of the house, which it does not, the other boot coming in with all of it. The best answer anybody has is that it is what you do, which is the right answer.

> **— T.** I have taken one shoe off at midwinter every year of my life. I was taught as a child that it was required — that one does not go barefoot and one does not go shod, and that the left is the correct one — and I have never once been given a reason. I have asked three people. One said it was for the cold. One said it was for luck. One was put out with me for asking. It is his foot. I am sitting here with my shoe in my hand, and it is his foot.

On which day to pick, which is the other half of inheriting: tie it to something that happens whether anybody is organised or not. A solstice is unarguable — the shortest day arrives on its own, can be marked with a stick and a wall, and needs no man with a book to announce it. So do the first hard frost and the harvest in. A day tied to a date somebody has to remember gets lost with that somebody.

## Songs, Which Are Tools

I cannot sing and I have no ear, and a group without songs is still carrying weight it does not have to.

**Work songs are for getting several people to pull at the same instant.** Two people can watch each other; six cannot, and six out of time on a rope is worse than four in time, because the ones pulling against the rhythm load it at the wrong moment and get nothing back for it. A song puts the pull on a beat everybody can hear without looking up, which matters most facing away from each other or down in a trench. One person calls and the pull goes on the answer, not the call, so everybody gets a half-bar to ready in.

**Counting songs are for handing a procedure to somebody who cannot read.** A rhyme with a tune on it is the most reliable way anybody has found to put a fixed sequence into a person's head and get it back in the right order years later. The tune holds the words in place, the rhyme tells you when one has gone missing, and it survives being learnt off somebody who learnt it off somebody else. I will not pretend to know why. I have heard children give back a seven-step job in order, note for note, who could not read it back.

So **Rule 18: teach the children the boil time as a song.** Chapter 4 is the long version of treating water and nothing here replaces it. The song carries two things: that it is a rolling boil and not steam — moving, breaking the surface — and how long you hold it there. Chapter 4 gives one minute low down and three higher up, because water boils cooler the higher you go, and says three minutes costs only fuel, so boil three if you do not know how high you are. A child does not know how high the hill is. So the song teaches three, which is chapter 4's own answer for anybody who cannot say, and is the easier thing to sing:

> Steam is not boiling, and warm is not clean;
> wait for the water to leap.
> Three minutes of leaping, and count them out loud,
> then cover it over to keep.

The counting out loud is not decoration. Somebody slow sets the pace the first few times and they count together, which drags it long rather than short, and long is the side to be wrong on. I have never known one of them get it wrong, and I have stood over the pot and checked, because getting it wrong is the likeliest thing in this book to kill a house.

## Naming Things

Name your children, and name your places. Places get named anyway; the job is to write each down once in one hand, because two names for one ford sends somebody to the wrong ford in the dark.

The one worth doing on purpose is naming the years — not numbering them from anything, since nobody agrees what to count from, but giving each a name off what happened in it. The wet year. The year of the second well. A minute at the turn of the calendar, said out loud so it is the same name in every house.

What it buys is a group that can discuss its own past without arguing about it. People who can say *that was two years before the wet year* can compare two winters and notice the ford has taken the bank twice in eleven years. Without names there is only this year and a fog, and in the fog every problem is new. I saw it done in two places, both doing other things right as well, so I claim no more than that. It is a minute a year and I would do it.

## Oaths

An oath makes a promise cost something to break, which is the only reason anybody wants one. It works on the person swearing rather than on anybody listening: said out loud, in front of people, it is harder to quietly not do.

Three conditions, and all three or none.

**Short.** One sentence, one specific thing, in words a frightened person could repeat. An oath you need a page for is a contract, which is a different thing.

**Public.** In front of the people it is owed to. The watching is where the force comes from.

**Bounded.** This is the one that matters and the one that gets left out. An oath needs an end in it: a season, a job, until the wall is up, renewed out loud when it runs out. A promise with no end is not a bigger promise. It is a different object.

So if you are asked to swear something, listen for the end of it. That is the whole of the test and you can do it in the moment, with people looking at you. Is there a date, a task, a condition — anything that finishes it? If not, what is being asked for is not a promise about a thing. It is a promise about yourself, for good, measured against everything you do afterwards by somebody who is not you. It turns changing your mind into a thing you confess to, and nobody need argue you out of leaving, because you have already sworn you would not. I have watched that used.

I could not tell the people who do it apart from the front, and neither can you, which is why the test is about a sentence and not about them. Chapter 15 is the long version of seeing a place go wrong from the yard, and **Rule 31** from chapter 7 is what to settle before you go in anywhere: name the one thing that means you go, and a day to look at it again.

## What It Costs You

The day off is a day's work gone out of the worst fortnight in the year, and there is no clever answer. It is the point. You are spending work you can feel to buy a thing you cannot. I have wintered in five places with a fixed day in the bad month and six without, and I know which still had everybody in the spring. That is not evidence; it is what I have.

The feast costs food, and the discipline is that it comes out of what was going over anyway. A day funded out of the reserve will be taken at the reserve's expense in the year the reserve is all there is.

The required nonsense cannot be explained later and cannot be amended, which is the same fact as its value. Once it is in it is in, and it gets kept by people given a wrong reason who then defend the wrong reason.

And a song is the most durable thing in this chapter, which cuts both ways. A number put into a tune outlives anybody who could be asked about it, so a wrong number in a song is permanent and gets sung confidently by people with no way to check it. It is why the boil-time song teaches three rather than one: the safer number is the one you can afford to have sung for a hundred years by people who never read chapter 4.

## The Short Version

- Rule 59: put the holiday at the worst part of the year, not the easiest — the long end of winter, the gap before anything comes in. The looking forward to a fixed date is half of what it does.
- Mark the year into named stretches with fixed days between them, said out loud, so everybody knows how far in they are and how much is left.
- Rule 61: a day off is maintenance, and a day with a name on it is not argued over each time it falls due, by tired people, against work that is waiting.
- Tie the day to something unavoidable — a solstice, a frost, the harvest in — so nobody has to remember a date and nobody owns it, and keep it cheap enough to hold in a bad year, because the bad year is what it is for.
- Let it be inherited rather than announced, and give it one piece of required nonsense that serves no purpose. The useless part is the part that holds, because a rule with a reason can be argued out of and a rule with no reason cannot.
- Use work songs to put the pull on a beat when more than about four are hauling and cannot watch each other. One person calls; the pull goes on the answer, not the call.
- Rule 18: teach the children the boil time as a song. It must carry a rolling boil — moving, breaking the surface, not steaming — and three minutes, which is chapter 4's own answer for anybody who cannot say how high up they are, and children cannot. Count out loud and err long. Chapter 4 is the long version.
- Name the years off what happened in them, out loud, the same name in every house, so people can compare two winters instead of meeting every problem as new.
- Keep oaths short, public and bounded, and listen for the end of what you are asked to swear — a date, a task, a condition. An unbounded oath is not a bigger promise, it is a trap, and nobody has to argue you out of leaving. Rule 31; chapter 7 is the long version of getting out, chapter 15 of seeing it coming.
