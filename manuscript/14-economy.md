# Chapter 14: The Economy of Nothing

> Rule 68: Reputation spends everywhere and cannot be minted.

In my twelfth year a man called Oren Tass tried to give the Bell Country money, and I carried it up three flights for him.

He had a press — a flat-bed thing, cast iron, the weight of two full buckets and all of it in the wrong place — eleven boxes of paper, and an idea. The notes had his face on them and the word ONE, because he said the backing was him. I did not follow that part. I took the press up first, which was the error: it wanted two men and the paper wanted four trips, and if you spend the two men on the press you carry the paper alone.

Then there was an argument, and it went nine days. It began as four people in a yard about whether a note was worth a thing. By the fourth day, the day it rained, they had it indoors and were doing it by the list: a day's water against a day's digging, a tin against a pound of flour, boots against boots. Two of them stopped speaking for the rest of their lives over a dozen eggs, which made the rota awkward for about a year.

On the ninth day it finished and I took the lot back down. Paper first, four trips, a box under each arm; the press last with somebody on the other end, heavy end leading down the turns, and we never once set it on a tread. That is the right order and I had it wrong going up. Eleven boxes of paper is heavier than a press, and what hurts you is not the weight but the number of times you change grip on a stair.

> **— T.** That argument did not stop when the paper went back down the stairs. Everything in this country is priced off what came out of it. The list hangs on the wall of the room I sit in; every market between here and the water keeps to it; its measures are the ones those people shouted across a yard for nine days, and what it means that he stood in that room for the whole — no. There is no putting that next to a paragraph about load distribution. He has given me the stairs. What he says further on about weighing and counting is right, and we teach those same tells in that order.

## What Makes A Thing Money

Four qualities. A thing needs all four, or it is only a thing somebody likes.

**It lasts.** A year in a tin and it is still itself. Anything that rots, rusts, leaks or goes rancid cannot be money, because the day it begins to spoil everybody holding it wants rid at once.

**It divides.** You must be able to pay a little bit. A goat is valuable and is not money, because nobody buys a morning's help with a tenth of a goat: the man with one goat pays the whole goat.

**It carries.** A week's worth goes a day's walk on your person — weight against worth, which is why a sack of grain is a commodity and a fist of salt is a currency.

**Everybody agrees.** The one that cannot be arranged. The first three are properties of the object and your hands can check them; the fourth is a property of other people, and it exists already or it does not. Nothing printed on paper makes it. That is the nine days in one sentence, and nobody put it that way at the time.

Look at what people hand over and it is salt, ammunition, fuel and medicine, roughly in that order of how far it travels. Nobody chose them: they pass all four tests, which is also why each is priced by people who need it badly. **Rule 19. Salt is money** — chapter 6 is where I paid for that. I am not pleased about the other three. It is only where the list came out.

## Trading Without Being Had

**Price is what the two of you agree in a yard on a Tuesday. Value is what the thing does for you afterwards.** People lose everything confusing them: a good price on a thing you will never use is a loss, a bad price on the tool that gets you through the winter is cheap. Decide value before you go, alone, where nobody can watch your face.

Then **Rule 26. Never name the first number.** Your number tells them how much you need it, and they know their own supply better than you do. I wanted a file once and said four pounds of beans before he had said anything at all; he heard a man who needed a file, it cost nine two days later, and I had carried the beans about the whole time where everybody could watch me do it.

Ask what they are short of instead: it costs nothing, tells you what you are actually holding, and moves the talk off the thing you came for. If neither of you will open, trade something small first so there is a number on the table nobody invented (**Rule 12**, chapter 7, which also shows whether they keep an agreement).

**Bundle.** One thing against one thing is a fight over a single number; three against three is a conversation about who wants what, and you nearly always want different parts of it — a blade, a coil of cord and an afternoon's digging against their salt and a lamp. Both come out ahead, each having paid in what he had too much of.

**Do not trade away tools**, at a bad price or a good one, which is the harder half. A tool makes things; everything else is a thing. A saw for a month of food trades all your winters for one fortnight, and the price looks excellent until you need a saw and are renting one off a neighbour for life. Food, fuel and cloth are stock and meant to go; anything that cuts, bores, holds water or holds heat is capital, sold only to eat this week and knowing what you have done.

The exception is **Rule 55**: a tool you cannot keep in work is already gone. A second of something, or one you cannot sharpen or fit a handle to, goes to whoever will keep it running — you have not lost a tool, you have arranged to live near a working one.

## Debt, Which Is The Real Economy

All of that is for strangers. Inside a group almost none of it happens. I have belonged to eleven groups and can count the trades on one hand, and not because anybody was generous: a small group runs on favours, and favours are faster and cheaper than trade.

You do things for people without a price, they do the same for you, nobody writes it down, and everybody knows roughly where they stand. It looks like kindness and it is not. It is a store. The eleven days I spent on a man's roof in my fifteenth year were eleven days put where I could get at them later, and they came back next spring as four people and a cart when my own wall went.

Then the uncomfortable part, which I would not have believed at thirty: **favours keep better than objects.** Objects rust, get wet, get taken and have to be carried. A favour does not spoil, weighs nothing, cannot be stolen, and grows, because the man you helped tells people and you end up with credit at houses you have never been to.

One condition, and it is the whole of it. **It is only good while the group is.** A favour is a claim on particular living people in a particular place, so if they scatter or the place comes apart you hold nothing and there is nobody to present it to. I have been insolvent that way twice. Surplus goes into favours; salt is what you keep anyway, because the salt does not care what happens to the valley.

Three habits keep that from becoming a grievance economy, all learned by getting them wrong. **Say the size out loud when the help is asked for, not when it is done** — not a price, a size, *that is a day, maybe two* — because unspoken sizes are the whole of every falling-out I have watched, both men quietly keeping a different number. Repay in kind, not in a hurry with whatever was handy, which reads as paying a man off and closes an account you wanted open. And keep the ledger in your head: a man who tells you where you stand with him has started lending instead, which is a worse thing to be to your neighbours.

## What People Will Try On You

Here is the spotting. None of it needs you clever — only slow, in public, and counting your own things.

**Diluted and relabelled.** The commonest by a long way, because it does not feel like theft to the man doing it: salt cut with ash or sand, flour with chalk or sawdust, water in the fuel, another man's label still on the tin. Every test is only the honest version of the thing. Salt dissolves away to nothing and leaves a clean cup, so grit in the bottom is not salt and never was; good flour rubs smooth between finger and thumb and does not grind on the teeth; fuel and water separate if the jar stands a minute, water at the bottom. Taste, feel and a minute of standing still catch nearly all of it. The one you cannot test is the one to refuse outright: **anything loose, meant to go inside a person, in a container not its own, is not medicine.** There is no test for it in a yard, and the failure is somebody who believes they have been treated and has not.

**The scales.** A balance is only as honest as what you check it against. Bring a weight you know — a stone you have used a year does — and weigh that first; then weigh the goods, swap the pans round and weigh again, because a true balance gives the same answer both ways and a bad one does not, and you need to know nothing about why. The rest of the tells are the man, not the machine: a balance that never leaves its exact spot, a pan with something in it before the trade starts, a reading taken with his hand still on the beam, any annoyance at all about your stone. Honest traders like the stone, because it makes their word unnecessary.

**The short count.** Sacks, rounds, tins, eggs — and it is done to you while you are talking, which is why it works on friendly people. Count it yourself, into your own hand, in one go, in silence, and again before you walk. Anything already tied, already boxed, or counted fast by the man selling it is not counted. Say so pleasantly and count it.

**The too-good trade.** An offer far better than the going rate is information, and it means one of two things: they do not know what they have, which you fix in one sentence by telling them — it costs the margin and buys you the rest of this chapter — or you are the thing being traded, and the deal is a way of getting you somewhere, or your hands full, or you deciding fast. The markers travel together: a hurry with a reason attached, somewhere off the path, a second buyer arriving just then, a reluctance to let you count or weigh, a condition that you not mention it. Any two together and the trade is the bait. Walk, in daylight, the way you came.

## The Thing You Are Actually Spending

**Rule 68. Reputation spends everywhere and cannot be minted.** It passes all four tests and is the only currency you cannot make: it lasts your life, divides down to being trusted with one small job, weighs nothing, and goes up the road ahead of you. It takes years to build and an afternoon to lose, and it does not come back. Nobody tells you it has gone: trades take longer, people want things settled the same day, a thing is already promised.

I do not trade in Cadder. In my twenty-second year I hauled four sacks of seed grain in there for a man I was working for, and the grain was the year before's and dead, with chaff in it to make the weight up. I did not know. I was the man who carried it in and set it down and said what I had been told to say, and mine is the face they have. There has been no putting it right in eighteen years, because there is nothing to hand back and no sentence that helps, and the only two people who could say I did not know are the man who sold it and me. So I go round. It is about half a day.

Which is the honest reason for all of the above: count slowly, bring your own stone, tell the man who does not know what he has, settle before you leave. Not because you are good, but because your buying power is what the next valley has heard about you.

## What It Costs You

Trading carefully makes you slow and faintly insulting. Weighing a friend's salt against your own stone, counting eggs in front of the woman who raised the hens — a real cost, and some of it does not come back. I have annoyed people I liked over a count of nine.

Refusing the too-good trade means walking away from good ones too, because from outside they look identical. Several of mine were honest and I will never know which.

Never naming the first number makes conversations longer and sometimes kills them: some people want a figure and read a man who will not give one as a man with something to hide, and they are not wrong to.

Keeping tools means being hungrier than you have to be, in a particular week, with a saw on the wall. I have got that wrong in both directions.

And the favour economy costs you the ability to leave, because credit is only good where it was earned, so every year in one place makes the next valley dearer. That is not a reason to stay, only something to know you are paying.

## The Short Version

- Money is whatever lasts, divides, carries and is already agreed on. The first three your hands can test; the fourth is other people, and printing does not arrange it.
- Salt, ammunition, fuel and medicine pass all four, so all four become currency. Rule 19: salt is money, never spoils, and nobody makes it at home in a hurry.
- Price is what you agree on a Tuesday; value is what the thing does for you afterwards. Decide value alone, in advance, before anyone watches your face.
- Rule 26: never name the first number on something you need, because your figure tells them how badly you need it. Ask what they are short of; bundle three against three.
- Do not trade tools even at a good price, because a tool makes things and everything else is a thing. Rule 55 excepts one you cannot keep in work: pass it to somebody who can.
- Favours keep better than objects and are where a group's surplus belongs, but only while the group holds. Say the size when asked, repay in kind, keep the ledger unspoken.
- Salt dissolves clean, so grit in the cup is not salt; good flour rubs smooth; fuel and water separate in a standing jar. Refuse anything loose meant to go inside a person in a container not its own.
- Bring a weight you know, weigh it first, then weigh the goods with the pans both ways round. Count everything yourself, into your own hand, in silence, twice.
- An offer far better than the going rate means either they do not know what they have or you are what is being traded. Hurry, privacy, secrecy and no inspection, any two together, mean walk away in daylight.
