# The Long After

*A Practical Guide for Whoever Finds This*

---

## Author's Note

This book is a comedy.

The survival techniques in it are accurate to the best of our knowledge as of writing, and are drawn from published emergency guidance.

It is not a substitute for training.

Nobody should learn medicine from a novel.

## Editor's Preface

The pages came out of a dry cellar under a fallen roof at the eastern end of Ladderwell, in a tin box that had plainly been made to hold something else. There were two hundred and six sheets. One hundred and ninety-one can be read.

What we lost, we lost to water. The box sat low on one side for a long time and the low corner rotted. The front pages went first, so whatever title he gave this, and whatever he called himself, went with them. He never writes his own name in the body. His people called him the hand, or the old man, depending on how old he was being that week.

The back came through better than I feared. He ends with an appendix of his numbered Rules and that list is very nearly whole; most of its gaps are his own, since he says plainly he had forgotten a good many by the time he sat down. Two gaps are not his. Two pages have been torn out cleanly along a fold, by somebody with a steady hand and a reason, and I would trade a year of grain to know which Rules a person takes with them.

You should also know that the Rules are quoted here daily, in the yard and over the fire, by people who have never held this book and would not read it if you put it in their hands. They quote them wrong. I have stopped saying so.

We have left his spelling exactly as it stands, including the words none of us know. Guessing at them seemed a worse crime than printing them.

He is a careful man and I believe most of what he says, but I will admit where my attention went. In his chapter on keeping food he gives four lines to a thing called a refrigerator: a box that was cold inside, all the time, by itself, in every house. I have read those four lines more often than I have read anything else ever written. Elsewhere he describes glass bulbs that made light out of wire. I have been shown the broken ones. I am prepared to believe in the glass.

His third chapter is about stopping blood, and I confess I skimmed it. Everyone knows how to stop blood.

He addresses his last chapter to an apprentice he calls Ais, who was a girl when he wrote and is only a name to me. He names her seldom, which I find strange in a book written for her, and nowhere else in the world does the name appear. If anyone reading this is descended from her, I would like to hear about it.

— T., Ladderwell
