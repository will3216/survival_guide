# Chapter 7: The First People You Meet

> Rule 31: Know the way out before you go in.

The first stranger I dealt with on my own account was a woman on a track with a stick, in my second year, a month after I left the Spoke. I had been sent out for a bucket and I had the bucket. She took it off me — not fast, not unkindly, the way you take a cup off a child.

I went for her. I got about two steps, caught my heel on the bottom rail of a fence I had not registered, and went over it backwards into nettles, which is the most useful thing that has ever happened to me, because by the time I had my glasses back on neither of us had done anything worse. She stood on the far side of the rail with the bucket, waiting to see what I would turn out to be.

What I turned out to be was a man in a ditch. So we got on.

She kept the bucket. She also spent the next year teaching me how to look at a place from outside before walking into it: what to look at, in what order, and what to do with my hands when I got there. She had been doing it a while and was not making it up.

I am not going to give you her name. She asked me once, flatly, in the middle of something else, not to put her in a book, and that is the kind of promise I still keep. So she is the woman who took my bucket and taught me about fences, and this chapter is hers. Here is how she did it.

> **— T.** I know who she is. Everybody knows who she is. I have her name in three other hands, two of them carved, and I have written it into this paragraph and struck it out, and written it in and struck it out again, and my sheet has it crossed through four times like a man going at a wasp. One word. He could have given us one word. He is keeping a promise made in a lane to a woman seventy years dead, and I cannot get it to come out any way but that he was right and I am furious about it. Nothing below is in doubt: the method that follows is hers and his, it is sound, and it is still taught in this order.

## Reading a Place From Outside It

First, the thing the list will make you forget. **Most people are not a threat.** Treating everybody as one kills you too, only slower: you carry all your own water, mend your own roof, and die of the first job that needed two people. I have seen that far oftener than I have seen anybody come to harm at a gate. So the point of reading a place is not whether to be frightened. It is whether to walk in.

Give it an hour from above, out of the line of a path, before anybody has seen you. Five things.

**The water.** Where it comes from, and the queue: how far they carry it, how many trips, whether it is orderly. Then find where the waste goes and check it is below the water, not above. That one wrong is not wickedness, only illness, and yours too.

**The children.** Whether there are any, and whether they play or work at something a child can do. Children out of doors and bored is the best single sign there is.

**The animals.** Ribs, coats, limping, penned or loose. Thin stock in a fat place means the herd is being eaten down, and a fed dog is a different animal from a hungry one.

**The fences.** A fence says which way the trouble is expected to go. Look at the gate: bar and good hinge inside or out, one gate or three, ground worn on both sides or one. Both sides means people come and go; one side means people come.

**What has been mended.** A patched roof, a taut line, a weeded path, a handle whipped with cord. Mending means time, tools and somebody whose job it is, and nobody mends a gatepost in a place that expects to be gone by spring.

No single sign decides; you are listening for two that disagree, like full barns and thin children. One exception, and the only thing here I will tell you to act on without checking. **Plenty of food and no children anywhere is a place you walk past.** Children are the first thing a settlement spends its slack on, so if there is slack and no children, something has happened to them: they died, they were sent away, or they are kept out of sight. All three answers are about the people, not the harvest, and the only way to find out which is to go in and find out. Take the day's walk instead.

## Walking In

Being boring, early and easy to predict is the whole technique, because a frightened person decides what you are inside two seconds. So: daylight, with the morning left, by the track they expect visitors on. Not over a fence, not at dusk. Stop while you are still a long way off and let them look as long as they like; being seen slowly is the only gift you have to give. Several of you: one goes forward, the rest sit down in plain view where they can be counted.

Hands out of pockets, empty, away from your body, and keep them there when you feel stupid. Never put a hand behind your back, and never reach into a coat without saying what for. Then three things, short: who you are, what you want, how long you mean to stay. Then stop talking.

Do not hide the state you are in. If you are hungry, look hungry. A man obviously in trouble is a problem to be solved; a man concealing it is a man with a plan, and only one of those gets fed. And do not lie about anything checkable: a small lie caught in the first hour costs everything it was protecting.

Here is the part that took me years. **Being unarmed is not the same as being harmless.** Unarmed is a fact about your hands; harmless is their judgement of your intentions, and you do not control it. You can carry nothing at all and still read as a threat by arriving fast, at dusk, from the wrong side, not answering. So spend your effort on being predictable rather than on proving you have nothing, and keep any tool stowed with both hands off it: a shovel in two hands is not a shovel any more.

If they tell you to stop, stop. If they tell you to go, go, the way you came. Leaving a good settlement by mistake costs a day's walking. Pressing on into a frightened one does not cost a day's anything.

## Trade Is the First Conversation

Everybody has the same opening line, which is *I have something you might want.* It works between strangers because it turns you from a question into a transaction, and a transaction has an end in it.

**Rule 12. Small first trade. Resolve it the same day.** And make it slightly in their favour. The three parts do separate jobs.

Small, because what you are buying is not the goods. You are buying a look at how these people keep an agreement — who speaks, whether they check with somebody, whether the one who agreed can deliver — and the lesson should cost a pound of beans rather than everything you own.

Resolved the same day, because an open debt is a rope, and whichever end you hold you are tied to the place. I took a credit once — a sack now against two days' help at harvest — and it was three weeks before anybody agreed it was finished, and I could not argue, because I had eaten the sack. A group that strings out your half of a small deal has told you something enormous for almost nothing.

Slightly in their favour, because the margin is the cheapest goodwill there is and you were going to spend more than that on the walk. But do not open with your best thing: salt is money (Rule 19), and a man who shows all his money at a gate has decided something on behalf of everybody travelling with him.

## Signs to Walk Past

These are not signs of bad people, but the shape a group takes when it starts running on people instead of on work.

- They cannot tell you how anybody leaves. Ask it plainly, and a place with no answer has no leavers.
- Everything you own goes into a common store on the first day, and the store has one keeper.
- They want your boots, your tools or your map: anything that makes walking harder.
- You are never alone with one person.
- One name is in every sentence and nobody disagrees with it in front of you, even about the rota. I have never been anywhere decent where nobody argued about the rota.
- The kindness outruns what you earned, and it stops the day you stop deciding.
- They tell you nobody outside would take you.
- Short nights, long days, thin food, everybody together for all of it. A tired person agrees to things.
- There is a rule everybody keeps except one person.

Any one of those is also a bad fortnight in a decent place. It is the collection that matters, and which way it moves over a fortnight.

I could have said that whole list out loud, in order, the afternoon I walked in through the Ninth Hand's gate with a broom over my shoulder. It was a good gate. The hinges had been greased that month and the bar was on the inside, which I noticed, and I stayed two years, and it was not that bad. Greased hinges are worth a word, because nobody fakes them cheaply: it takes fat to spare and the habit of doing a small job before it is a big one. A dragging gate in an otherwise tidy place means the tidy part is for visitors.

## Leaving

Nobody practises leaving, and it is the harder half, because arriving needs only nerve and leaving needs a plan you made while you were still thinking straight.

**Rule 31. Know the way out before you go in.** Settle four things on the path and say them out loud: where you would go instead, how long it takes, what you would carry, which way out of the gate. Five minutes, and worth more than anything you think of later, because later you will be deciding it tired, in the dark, among people you have come to like.

Then keep the means of leaving — boots, a day of water and food, your route — where you can reach them without crossing a yard, and **never in the common store.** Not because the store is theft, but because everything in it belongs to a conversation, and a conversation is the one thing you will not have time for. Better still, a bag cached outside altogether, looked at once a month.

And this is the most useful sentence in the chapter: **it gets harder every day you stay.** Not because they tighten anything, but because you acquire a job you half own, people you half like, a place at a fire, and a set of reasons for leaving that the people you would be leaving have answered one at a time. Everybody I know who got out, got out on something decided in advance. So name, before you go in, the one thing that means you go, and a day to look at it again.

Leaving well looks dull. Daylight, say so out loud, debts settled, out through the gate, one trip, nothing fetched back. Creeping out at night makes you a thief, and that gives them a reason to follow you they did not have before. If *I am going tomorrow* is a sentence you are afraid to try out loud, you have your answer, and then you go early, quiet, by the route you picked, and you do not stop at the gate to be talked to.

Last thing, which is chapter 1's third question in other clothes: *does anyone know where I am?* Tell somebody outside which place you are walking into and when you expect to be out. A group is a room like any other.

## What It Costs You

Reading a place from outside costs a morning, and sometimes the place: you sit on a ridge being careful while somebody less careful walks in and gets the work.

Being read costs your dignity. You stand off with your hands open and your hunger showing while strangers discuss you, and the alternative is arriving in a way that gets you shouted at.

Keeping the means of leaving costs trust, and that is a real cost and not a trick with no downside. A man with a bag cached outside the gate is holding something back, and good people can feel it. Naming in advance the thing that would make you go costs you the hope that this one is finally home.

And caution itself costs years. I spent four winters being careful about people who would have been glad of the help, and nobody took anything off me in any of them, and I was no safer and a great deal colder.

## The Short Version

- Most people are not a threat; treating everyone as one kills you slowly, because you die of the first job that needed two. Read a place to decide how to walk in, not whether to fear it.
- Read a place from outside for an hour: the water and its queue, the children, the animals' condition, which way the fences face, what has been mended.
- Plenty of food and no children anywhere decides on its own: walk past. Every answer to where the children went is about the people.
- Approach in daylight by the track they expect, stopping far enough off to be seen. Hands empty and visible, one forward; say who you are, what you want, how long.
- Unarmed is a fact about your hands; harmless is their judgement of you. Earn it by being predictable and honest about anything they can check.
- Rule 12: small first trade, resolved the same day, slightly in their favour. You are buying a cheap look at how they keep an agreement.
- Walk past a place that cannot say how anybody leaves, that wants your boots and tools in a common store, where nobody disagrees with one name, or where kindness outruns what you earned.
- Rule 31: know the way out before you go in — where you would go, how long, what you carry — reachable in the dark, never in the common store.
- Leaving gets harder every day you stay, so name the thing that means you go before going in. Leave in daylight, settled up, and tell somebody outside.
