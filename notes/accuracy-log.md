# The Long After — Accuracy Log

Reference document. Lives outside `manuscript/`, is never linted for prose,
but its headings are machine-read by `scripts/lint_manuscript.py`
(`read_accuracy_chapters`), which requires one `## Chapter NN` heading (two
digits, zero-padded) for every chapter listed in `notes/manuscript-order.txt`.
Missing a heading produces an `ACCURACY` error.

This file is the real-world basis for every load-bearing claim in the book —
the technique behind the joke — kept separate from the manuscript so the
voice can stay funny while the underlying advice stays auditable. Section 1
of `notes/style-guide.md` is the reason this file exists: a reader who
follows the book's instructions in an actual emergency must be better off,
not worse. Every instruction with real-world stakes gets an entry here when
its chapter is written.

## Entry format

```markdown
- **Claim as stated in the chapter.** Basis: [source and what it says]. Confidence: high | conservative-choice | vague-on-purpose.
```

- **Claim as stated in the chapter** — quote or closely paraphrase the line
  from the manuscript so the claim and its justification can be checked side
  by side.
- **Basis** — the real-world source: a field, a standard practice, a named
  reference work, or basic physics/chemistry/physiology. Specific enough that
  someone could go check it.
- **Confidence** — one of:
  - `high` — well-established, verifiable technique.
  - `conservative-choice` — multiple valid approaches exist; the book picked
    the safer one on purpose.
  - `vague-on-purpose` — the chapter is deliberately imprecise (e.g. no exact
    dosage) because precision would be irresponsible without context the
    reader can't supply.

Each chapter's section starts as `_No entries yet._`. That string means
exactly one thing: **nobody has written this chapter, or nobody has logged
it yet.** It is a placeholder, not a verdict, and it must not be left in
place once a chapter is written and reviewed for claims.

When a chapter is written, its section gets one of two outcomes, and
leaving it ambiguous between them is the bug this format exists to prevent:

- **Claims exist.** Replace `_No entries yet._` with the bulleted list of
  entries, one per claim, in the format above.
- **No load-bearing claims exist.** Replace `_No entries yet._` with
  `_Reviewed — nothing load-bearing to log._` This string means a human
  checked this chapter for real-world claims and found none worth an
  entry. It is not the default and must never be left in place without
  that check having happened.

`_No entries yet._` must never be the final state of a written chapter's
section. If a chapter is done and its section still reads `_No entries
yet._`, that is a sign the accuracy review was skipped, not that the
chapter made no claims.

## Chapter 01

- **"Three minutes without air. Three hours without shelter... Three days without water. Three weeks without food" — presented explicitly as a mnemonic for the *order*, not a schedule.** Basis: the "rule of threes" as taught in wilderness first-aid and search-and-rescue instruction; it is a teaching aid for priority ordering, and published wilderness-medicine guidance stresses that actual survival times vary enormously with environment, exertion and individual condition. The chapter states the caveat in text. Confidence: vague-on-purpose.
- **Air is first; loss of breathing is a matter of minutes.** Basis: basic physiology — irreversible hypoxic brain injury begins within a few minutes of circulatory or respiratory arrest, which is why resuscitation guidance treats it as the most time-critical condition. Confidence: high.
- **"Cold and wet and windy can finish a healthy adult in well under three hours."** Basis: standard hypothermia guidance — wet clothing and wind dramatically increase conductive and convective heat loss, and cold-water immersion in particular can cause incapacitation and death far faster than three hours. The chapter deliberately takes the pessimistic side of the mnemonic. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Heat and hard work can wreck you in well under three days" (water).** Basis: dehydration and heat-illness guidance — fluid loss through sweat under heat and exertion can produce incapacitating dehydration and heat stroke within a day or less; the three-day figure assumes temperate rest. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"You will be stupid and clumsy and bad-tempered a long time before you are starving."** Basis: starvation and semi-starvation research (notably the documented cognitive, emotional and physical effects of prolonged caloric restriction) showing marked degradation in concentration, mood, irritability and physical capacity well before life-threatening starvation. Confidence: high.
- **Acute stress narrows vision, degrades hearing and fine motor control, distorts time perception, and reduces the ability to compare options.** Basis: the acute stress (sympathetic/catecholamine) response, well documented in aviation, diving, law-enforcement and emergency-medicine incident analysis: perceptual narrowing ("tunnel vision"), auditory exclusion, loss of fine motor dexterity, time distortion, and reduced working-memory capacity for option generation. Confidence: high.
- **Countermeasure: prolonged exhalation (breathe out longer than in, roughly five breaths) reduces arousal.** Basis: slow, exhalation-weighted breathing increases parasympathetic (vagal) tone and lowers heart rate; used in clinical anxiety management and in operational stress-inoculation training. It reduces arousal; it does not eliminate it, and the chapter says so. Confidence: high.
- **Countermeasure: complete one small, concrete, ordered physical task, named aloud, before deciding anything.** Basis: the same principle underlying written emergency checklists and read-aloud procedures in aviation, surgery and fire service — externalising sequence offloads impaired working memory under high arousal; verbalising intended actions is standard crew-resource-management practice. Confidence: high.
- **"Am I bleeding?" is asked before airway/breathing.** Basis: this is a deliberate adoption of the trauma-care ordering used in modern tactical and civilian bleeding-control teaching (massive haemorrhage before airway — the MARCH sequence, and the public "stop the bleed" message), which prioritises life-threatening external haemorrhage because it kills in minutes and is correctable by direct pressure alone. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Treatment given: look as well as feel, press hard and directly with whatever is at hand, and do not repeatedly lift to check.** Basis: standard bleeding-control guidance — firm, continuous, direct pressure; intermittent release disrupts clot formation. The chapter gives no dosage, device or tourniquet instruction and defers to chapter 3. Confidence: high.
- **Injured people frequently do not notice their own injuries in the immediate aftermath.** Basis: well-documented stress-induced analgesia and attentional narrowing in acute trauma; self-check-after-the-fact is standard first-aid practice. Confidence: high.
- **Invisible indoor air hazards: anything burning or running in an enclosed space, a cellar, a stairwell, a shaft, a long-shut room; a thickening headache, sickness and sleepiness that began and worsened indoors mean get out into open air and stay out.** Basis: carbon monoxide poisoning guidance — CO is colourless and odourless, early symptoms are headache, nausea, dizziness and drowsiness, and the instruction is to move to fresh air and not re-enter. Confidence: high.
- **"Do not go down after somebody who went down before you. A great many of the dead in enclosed spaces are the second person in, and some of them are the third."** Basis: confined-space entry safety guidance, which records that a large share of confined-space fatalities are would-be rescuers who entered unprotected. The chapter gives the conservative instruction (do not enter) rather than any rescue technique. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **If people know roughly where you are, staying put and being findable is usually right; if nobody knows, nobody is coming.** Basis: search-and-rescue practice — a known last-seen point and a stationary subject enormously shorten searches, and lost-person guidance generally favours staying put and increasing visibility when a search is expected. Confidence: high.
- **A systematic area search — dividing the ground into squares and finishing each one before starting the next — beats sweeping the same ground repeatedly.** Basis: standard search methodology (search-and-rescue sector/grid searching, and the same principle in evidence and underwater search): dividing an area into bounded segments and clearing each to a defined endpoint gives known coverage, whereas unstructured sweeping produces both repeated and missed ground. Described in the chapter's opening as how he searched the floor. Confidence: high.
- **A counted check in a fixed order beats an unstructured sweep (including headcounts).** Basis: standard practice in inventory, pre-flight and casualty accounting — ordered enumeration with a defined endpoint reduces omission errors, and expectation-driven scanning produces known miscounts. Confidence: high.

## Chapter 02

- **Stay put when the structure is sound, you have water or a certain way to get it, the hazard is outside and passing, and a route out still exists; leave when the structure is compromised, the hazard is present and worsening, or you have no water.** Basis: standard emergency-management guidance on shelter-in-place versus evacuation (civil-defence and national emergency-preparedness advice), which keys the decision to whether the hazard is inside or outside the building, whether the building remains habitable and structurally intact, and whether essential supplies — water above all — are available for the expected duration. Confidence: high.
- **The decision should be made early, stated aloud to another person, and acted on with daylight remaining.** Basis: emergency-management evacuation practice and crew-resource-management habit — pre-committing to a decision in front of others reduces the drift and re-litigation that produce late departures, and darkness degrades navigation, visibility and casualty handling. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Leaving late, not choosing wrong, is the dominant way people die of this decision.** Basis: post-incident analysis of wildfire and hurricane evacuations, which repeatedly finds that a large share of deaths occur among people who delayed and were then caught in the open or in vehicles on congested routes, rather than among people who sheltered in an appropriate structure. Late evacuation is recognised in wildfire guidance as more dangerous than either early evacuation or well-prepared sheltering. Confidence: high.
- **The two errors are asymmetric: leaving too early costs a wasted day and embarrassment, leaving too late can cost everything — so the decision should be biased toward going early.** Basis: standard asymmetric-risk reasoning as applied in official evacuation advice, which advises leaving early and explicitly accepts the cost of unnecessary evacuations against the cost of late ones. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Pre-set the departure trigger in advance as an observable condition rather than a feeling (e.g. "if the water reaches the step").** Basis: trigger-point planning, used in wildfire and flood evacuation planning and in incident-action planning generally — pre-defined, observable decision thresholds set while calm, because judgement degrades under acute stress (see chapter 01's entries on the acute stress response). Confidence: high.
- **Go-bag contents, in priority order: water, light, warmth, documents, medication, a knife; food last.** Basis: published emergency "go kit" / grab-bag guidance from national preparedness agencies and red-cross-type organisations, which consistently lists water, a torch with spare power, warm and dry layers, copies of identity and medical documents, prescription medication, and a multi-tool or knife, with non-perishable food included but ranked below water. The ordering also follows the chapter 01 priority order (air, shelter, water, food). Confidence: high.
- **"A litre of water weighs a kilogram" and water will be most of the bag's weight; household storage guidance is roughly four litres per person per day, and you will not carry three days of it.** Basis: the density of fresh water is 1 kg per litre by definition of the metric system; the storage figure corresponds to the widely published one-gallon-per-person-per-day household emergency recommendation (approximately 3.8 litres). The chapter states plainly that this quantity is not portable for multiple days and defers sourcing to the water chapter rather than inventing a carry figure. Confidence: high.
- **Carry water in more than one container.** Basis: standard redundancy practice in emergency kit design — a single container is a single point of failure (puncture, loss, contamination). Confidence: high.
- **Light: hands-free if possible, with a backup that does not share a battery type with the primary.** Basis: common practice in caving, mountain rescue and utility work — a head torch keeps hands available, and a second light on a different power source removes the shared failure mode of one dead battery type. Confidence: high.
- **Warmth: a dry spare insulating layer kept in a waterproof bag, plus a hat and a windproof layer; wet and wind are what kill.** Basis: hypothermia prevention guidance — wet clothing and wind greatly increase conductive and convective heat loss; keeping a dry insulating layer genuinely dry is standard mountain and cold-weather practice, and significant heat is lost from the uncovered head. Detailed treatment deferred to chapter 05. Confidence: high.
- **Medication: carry the written names of each medication, not just the pills, and rotate stock so it is not expired.** Basis: standard emergency-preparedness and travel-medicine advice — a written medication list (drug name, dose, frequency) lets any other provider continue treatment if containers are lost, and stored medication must be rotated before expiry. The chapter gives no doses, by design. Confidence: vague-on-purpose.
- **Documents: copy them and keep the copies separate from the originals.** Basis: routine emergency-preparedness guidance on identity, insurance and medical records; separation of copies is standard protection against a single loss event. Confidence: high.
- **Everyone's first bag is too heavy; test it by carrying it up and down one flight of stairs, and a load much past about a third of body weight degrades the carrier.** Basis: military and occupational load-carriage research, which documents progressive degradation of speed, agility, endurance and injury risk as load approaches and exceeds roughly 30 percent of body weight, and standard pack-fitting practice of loaded walk-testing before relying on a pack. The one-flight-of-stairs test is a practical proxy, not a measurement; the chapter presents it as such. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Heavy items (water) packed low and close to the back panel, not high and soft; "a bag that stays still weighs less than a bag that does not."** Basis: standard load-carriage and pack-packing practice — keeping dense mass low, close to the spine and near the body's centre of mass reduces torque, limits load sway and improves balance and economy of movement. Stated in the chapter's opening and shown in its go-bag plate. Confidence: high.
- **Roads are fast and watched; crowds and trouble concentrate at pinch points such as bridges, cuttings and junctions.** Basis: basic terrain and movement analysis used in land navigation and in evacuation traffic planning — transport corridors concentrate movement, and constriction points (bridges, defiles, interchanges) are documented congestion and interdiction locations in both evacuation studies and route-security practice. Confidence: high.
- **Water courses navigate themselves: following a drainage downhill reliably leads toward lower ground and habitation, and gives a landmark that can be relocated.** Basis: standard land-navigation technique — using a linear feature as a "handrail", and the long-established search-and-rescue and wilderness-navigation heuristic that drainages lead downhill toward larger watercourses and human settlement. Confidence: high.
- **But water routes are longer, wetter, sometimes impassable, and subject to rising levels; do not wade moving water whose bottom you cannot see, because moving water is stronger than it looks and will take your feet at shallow depth.** Basis: flood-safety and swiftwater-rescue guidance, which advises never entering moving floodwater on foot and notes that relatively shallow fast water is sufficient to knock an adult down, plus hypothermia risk from immersion. The chapter takes the conservative instruction (do not wade) rather than teaching a crossing technique. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Rule 11 — the second floor is not safer, it is only harder to leave: going up reduces you to one exit; most building drownings occur above the ground floor after an upward move with no second move; if you go up, know the way down and a second way out of the upper room.** Basis: flood-rescue and fire-escape guidance — flood advice warns specifically against entering an attic or closed upper space without a means of exit to the roof or outside, because rising water can trap occupants, and fire-escape planning requires two means of escape from every upper room. The chapter gives the conservative instruction and the two-exits requirement. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Leave written word of your departure: who you are, how many, when you left, and where you are headed.** Basis: search-and-rescue practice — a last-known point, party size, timing and intended route transform a search's size and duration; leaving an itinerary is standard advice for any party moving in remote or disrupted conditions. Follows directly from chapter 01's third question. Confidence: high.

## Chapter 03

- **Severe external bleeding is treated before airway and breathing.** Basis: the massive-haemorrhage-first ordering used in modern trauma teaching (the MARCH sequence) and in the public bleeding-control campaigns derived from it, on the grounds that uncontrolled external haemorrhage kills in minutes and is correctable with bare hands. Continues the ordering chapter 01 adopted. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Direct pressure done properly: locate the exact bleeding point; a cloth or dressing between hand and wound if one is to hand, bare hand if not; heel of the hand on that one spot with the second hand stacked on top, arms locked and shoulders over, so body weight does the work; press harder than feels comfortable; do not move the hand or the spot.** Basis: standard first-aid and bleeding-control teaching on firm, localised, continuous direct pressure; the stacked-hands, locked-arms, body-weight posture is how pressure is taught to be sustained without fatigue in first-aid and bleeding-control courses. Confidence: high.
- **Hold pressure continuously for ten minutes at least, timed, and never lift off to check; bleeding through the dressing is treated by adding more material on top rather than removing what is there.** Basis: standard guidance that clot formation takes several uninterrupted minutes and that intermittent release disrupts it; published first-aid instruction gives ten minutes of uninterrupted pressure as a working minimum for serious bleeding and instructs against removing a soaked dressing. The chapter takes the longer, more conservative hold and gives no upper figure. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Handover of pressure: the relieving pair of hands goes on top before the first pair comes away, so the site is never unpressed.** Basis: routine practice in first-aid and pre-hospital care for maintaining uninterrupted haemostatic pressure during changeover. Confidence: high.
- **Wound packing: for deep extremity and junctional wounds only (thigh, buttock, shoulder, armpit, groin), push dressing material into the wound down to the bleeding source, keep feeding it in until the cavity is full and tight, then hold firm pressure for a good three minutes without moving; leave the packing in place.** Basis: Stop the Bleed / tactical-casualty-care wound-packing instruction — pack gauze (haemostatic or plain) directly into the wound to the source, fill the cavity completely, then maintain at least three minutes of direct pressure, and do not remove the packing in the field. The chapter notes that most people under-pack, which is the documented common error, and gives no fraction for it because no published figure supports one. It also states in text that three minutes is the earliest the wound may be looked at, not leave to stop pressing, so the shorter figure cannot be read against the ten-minute direct-pressure hold. **Caveat, and the reason for the tag: the neck was deliberately removed from this indication list.** Neck packing appears in some tactical and advanced curricula but lay civilian bleeding-control teaching is genuinely split on it, the constraints that make it survivable are not teachable in a chapter read once by a non-clinician, and the chapter's own rule is that uncertainty is written vaguer rather than wronger. Neck bleeding is handled under direct pressure instead (own entry below). Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Do not pack wounds of the chest, abdomen, head or eye.** Basis: standard wound-packing contraindication in bleeding-control teaching — these sites offer no compressible base and packing risks further injury; the eye is added on the same reasoning and on standard eye-injury first aid, which is to cover and never to press into or onto the globe. Such wounds are managed by other means and are beyond bare-hands care. Confidence: high.
- **Neck bleeding is treated with direct pressure only: on the one side it is coming from, never both sides at once — and the prose now states the reason, that both sides at once stops the blood to the brain — never packed, and with nothing wrapped or tied around the neck. Where breathing is failing, the chapter gives precedence as *position*, not release: let them sit up and find the way that works, keep pressing while they do, and lift the hand for nothing.** Basis: the conservative lay instruction common to first-aid teaching — digital or palm pressure over the bleeding point, explicitly not circumferential dressing (which obstructs the airway and venous return), and explicitly not bilateral pressure, which risks bilateral carotid occlusion and cerebral hypoperfusion. The bilateral reason is carried in the prose rather than the log alone because it is the prohibition a panicking responder is most likely to break, the obvious move when one hand is not working being to add the other, and because the adjacent airway reason attached to the circumferential prohibition would otherwise be mis-transferred to it. **Precedence was reframed deliberately:** the usual airway threat here is an expanding haematoma compressing the trachea, which bare hands cannot relieve, so an instruction to yield priority without a named action would read as licence to lift pressure off a bleeding carotid for no available benefit. Sitting up is the only action a bare-handed responder has, and it is compatible with continued pressure. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **An open chest wound: cover it with CLOTH and explicitly not plastic, do not pack it, allow whatever position they breathe best in (usually sitting up) rather than laying them flat — including once shock begins — and if the breathing becomes faster, shallower, more frightened or greyer *after the cover went on*, lift a corner; if lifting helps, leave the cover loose, and a cover that has been lifted twice stays off. The cover is laid on and explicitly not leaned on, and the passage ends by stating that this is the limit of what hands can do.** Basis: first-aid and pre-hospital management of open chest wounds — cover the wound, position of comfort — together with the recognised hazard that an occlusive cover can convert an open pneumothorax to a tension pneumothorax. **The chapter does not instruct an airtight seal and must not be read as doing so.** Current lay guidance has moved away from lay-applied occlusive dressings for exactly this reason, and the vented chest seal that makes sealing reasonably safe is a manufactured device this setting does not have; cloth is specified as the material precisely because it will not seal, and the prose states that reason in the same sentence. This also resolves a cross-section contradiction created by an earlier fix round: `## What It Costs You` tells the responder to take any waterproof barrier already within reach, and a reader following the book in printed order could otherwise have held plastic over a sucking chest wound under body weight. The burp trigger is anchored to the cover rather than to the injury, and given a termination condition, so a responder cannot cycle indefinitely between relieving and re-creating the condition and read the cycle as deterioration. **Force limit, added deliberately:** the chapter's own direct-pressure procedure four paragraphs earlier teaches locked arms and body weight, so a reader filing a chest wound as a bleed could lean through a wet cloth onto a chest — which impedes ventilation and is the one way cloth does seal, defeating the choice of material. "Lay it on. Do not lean on it" closes that. The passage also now ends on the honest terminus rather than a procedural rule, so a reader whose casualty deteriorates for reasons the cover is not causing — progressive tension, haemothorax from the underlying lung injury — is told they have reached the end of what hands do rather than left to keep adjusting a dressing. The chapter gives no decompression technique, no device, and does not claim the cover is a treatment. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Pressing bare-handed carries a bloodborne-exposure cost: take any waterproof barrier already within reach rather than hunting for one, and wash well afterwards.** Basis: standard infection-control practice in first aid — barrier precautions where available, hand hygiene after contact with blood or body fluids — balanced against the chapter's prior instruction that delay to find a barrier is itself dangerous. The chapter states the trade explicitly in `## What It Costs You` and notes the absence of the medicines that once answered for an exposure. **Scope note:** this clause licenses a waterproof barrier for *pressure on a bleeding wound only*. It is not a licence to seal a chest wound, and the chest passage now names cloth and its reason so the two cannot be combined by a reader working in printed order. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Tourniquets are for limbs only — never the neck, shoulder or trunk — and are indicated when pressure will not hold the bleed, when the responder must move, or when casualties outnumber responders.** Basis: tourniquet doctrine in tactical and civilian bleeding-control guidance: tourniquets apply to extremity haemorrhage only, and are indicated for life-threatening limb bleeding that direct pressure cannot control or where tactical/resource constraints prevent sustained pressure. Junctional and truncal bleeding is explicitly not a tourniquet site. Confidence: high.
- **A manufactured windlass tourniquet is preferred; improvised tourniquets use a wide strap and a strong rod, never a cord or wire.** Basis: published guidance preferring commercially manufactured windlass tourniquets over improvised ones, and the long-standing instruction that an improvised tourniquet must be broad (narrow cord or wire concentrates pressure, damages tissue and often fails to occlude). Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Placement: high and tight on the limb between wound and body, a few inches above the wound, higher acceptable, never over a joint.** Basis: standard tourniquet application guidance — placement proximal to the wound (commonly given as 2–3 inches / 5–8 cm above it, or high on the limb when the site is unclear), never across a joint because occlusion fails there. The chapter gives "a few inches" rather than a precise figure and states the conservative fallback (higher is acceptable, too low is not). Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Tighten until the bleeding stops — that is the only endpoint; the pain is expected and is not a reason to loosen a turn. If bleeding continues, apply a second tourniquet immediately above the first rather than adjusting the first.** Basis: tourniquet guidance that the application endpoint is cessation of bleeding (and loss of distal pulse), that tourniquet pain is expected and routinely causes under-tightening, and that a second tourniquet placed side by side or just proximal to the first is the standard response to continued bleeding. Confidence: high.
- **Write the time of application down immediately — on the strap, on the skin, or on a label.** Basis: universal tourniquet practice — application time governs every subsequent clinical decision about the limb, and marking it on the device or the casualty is standard in both military and civilian protocols. Confidence: high.
- **The person who applied the tourniquet never loosens or removes it; if no one with greater capability exists, it stays on and the application time is handed over.** Basis: standard civilian and tactical guidance that a tourniquet applied for life-threatening haemorrhage should not be periodically released or removed by a lay responder, and that conversion or removal is a clinical decision. Periodic loosening — once taught — is specifically warned against. The chapter takes the strictest position available and explains why. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Airway and breathing come after haemorrhage control: ten seconds to assess for normal breathing; an occasional noisy gasp is not breathing.** Basis: resuscitation-council guidance on assessing breathing for no more than ten seconds and on agonal gasping being frequently mistaken for normal breathing. Confidence: high.
- **An unresponsive but normally breathing casualty is turned onto their side (recovery position) and monitored; this is done even where spinal injury is suspected, because airway obstruction is the more immediate threat.** Basis: first-aid and resuscitation guidance on the recovery position for unresponsive breathing casualties, and the standing principle that airway management takes precedence over spinal-motion restriction when the two conflict. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Not breathing normally — and the chapter states the comparator in the same breath as the order to continue, that the alternative is nothing, because the reader's live question during prolonged solo CPR is whether to continue rather than how: chest compressions, heel of the hand on the centre of the chest with the other on top, arms straight, hard and fast, allowing full chest recoil, at a rate between 100 and 120 per minute, continued until breathing returns, relief arrives, or the rescuer cannot continue.** Basis: current resuscitation-council and AHA basic-life-support guidance: compression rate 100–120 per minute, hand placement on the centre of the chest, full recoil between compressions. The 100–120 figure is a published guideline range and is the only numeric rate in the chapter. The chapter deliberately gives no depth figure and no compression-to-ventilation ratio. Confidence: high.
- **Head tilt and chin lift before the breathing assessment: a hand on the forehead, two fingers under the point of the chin, tip the head gently back, on the grounds that in an unconscious supine person the tongue is the commonest obstruction and that many who appear not to be breathing are only positionally obstructed.** Basis: the head-tilt/chin-lift airway-opening manoeuvre as taught in basic life support by the resuscitation councils and the AHA, performed before assessing breathing, because posterior displacement of the tongue is the usual cause of airway obstruction in the unresponsive supine patient. Added as the chapter's step 2, between clearing the visible mouth and the ten-second look, because with clearing moved to step 1 the reader's attention was on the mouth and nothing told them to relieve the obstruction the chapter's own recovery-position step names. No jaw-thrust or airway-adjunct variant is given. Confidence: high.
- **Remove only visible, reachable obstruction from the mouth; no blind finger sweeps. This is the chapter's first airway step, before the head tilt, the breathing assessment and compressions.** Basis: long-standing first-aid instruction against blind finger sweeps, which can drive an obstruction further in; the ordering follows the general principle that an obstruction visible in the mouth is cleared before ventilation or circulation is assessed, so that a reader working down the numbers cannot begin compressions with a removable obstruction still in place. Confidence: high.
- **Shock recognition: pale, grey, clammy skin; cold hands; weak rapid pulse; rapid shallow breathing; thirst; nausea; restlessness and agitation progressing to quietness and confusion — deterioration in conscious level being the more serious sign.** Basis: standard first-aid and pre-hospital descriptions of hypovolaemic shock, including altered mental state (anxiety and restlessness progressing to confusion and unresponsiveness) as a late and serious sign. Confidence: high.
- **Shock treatment: treat the cause first (usually haemorrhage); lay the casualty flat and keep them still — with the stated exception, carried in the shock step itself, that an open chest wound or a neck bleed says otherwise — a chest-wound casualty stays sitting where they breathe and a neck-bleed casualty may sit up to keep an airway, both of which the relevant passages also state in text, so no two sections contradict each other; insulate from the ground as well as covering them; nothing by mouth; loosen constrictive items other than a tourniquet; monitor and record changes.** Basis: first-aid shock management — control bleeding, keep the casualty lying down, warm and still, and withhold oral fluids because of the possibility of surgery and the risk of aspiration. Insulation from the ground reflects hypothermia-prevention practice, conductive loss to the ground exceeding convective loss to air. **Noted tension, deliberately unresolved in the prose:** the published rationale for withholding oral fluids is impending surgery and aspiration risk, and this chapter's own last section states there is no surgery to come. Prolonged-field-care and austere-medicine guidance is accordingly less absolute and admits cautious oral fluids for some conscious casualties under some conditions. The chapter takes the stricter line anyway, because the conditions that make the exception safe (conscious, no abdominal injury, no deteriorating consciousness, hours of observation) are exactly the judgements a frightened lay reader cannot make, and aspiration remains a near-term killer whether or not a surgeon exists. The absolute instruction is a choice, not an oversight. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Leg elevation ("shock position") is deliberately not recommended, and the chapter says why.** Basis: current first-aid guidance has largely withdrawn routine passive leg raising for shock for want of outcome evidence, and resuscitation-council first-aid reviews treat it as of uncertain benefit. The chapter takes the conservative position of flat-and-still and states the reason in text rather than giving a contested manoeuvre. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Fractures: splint in the position found, without straightening, realigning, or replacing a protruding bone.** Basis: standard first-aid fracture management — immobilise in the position found, do not attempt reduction, do not push protruding bone back; realignment in the field risks neurovascular and soft-tissue injury. Confidence: high.
- **Immobilise the joint above and the joint below the fracture; pad generously; the splint must be rigid and long enough; secure above and below the break, never over it; firm but not tight.** Basis: standard splinting practice in first-aid and wilderness-medicine teaching — a splint must span and immobilise the joints proximal and distal to the injury, with padding and with ties placed away from the fracture site. Confidence: high.
- **Check circulation, sensation and movement distal to the splint before applying it, after applying it, and again later; cold, white, blue or numb tissue past the splint means loosen it at once.** Basis: standard first-aid and wilderness-medicine instruction to assess and re-assess distal circulation, sensation and movement (including capillary refill) before and after splinting, because a splint applied or swollen too tight can cause ischaemic injury. This is the chapter's one and only instruction to loosen anything. Confidence: high.
- **An open fracture is covered with the cleanest available material and the bleeding managed by pressure around rather than directly over the protruding bone.** Basis: standard first-aid management of open fractures — cover to reduce contamination, control bleeding with pressure applied around the wound, do not press on exposed bone. Confidence: high.
- **What cannot be fixed by hands: internal haemorrhage; a head injury that progressively deteriorates; abdominal injury. The signs given — worsening shock with no accounting wound, a rigid tender abdomen, truncal bruising; and for head injury a lucid interval followed by increasing drowsiness, vomiting, confusion, difficulty rousing, unequal pupils and worsening headache — identify conditions requiring surgical care.** Basis: standard trauma teaching. Internal haemorrhage is not amenable to external compression and requires surgical control. The described head-injury pattern is the classic deteriorating intracranial haemorrhage picture (including the "talk and deteriorate" course and pupillary asymmetry), for which the definitive treatment is neurosurgical decompression. Blunt or penetrating abdominal trauma likewise requires surgical management. The list is scoped to exactly these three and the chapter offers no technique for any of them. Confidence: high.

The chapter states this plainly and claims no treatment effect for it. Confidence: conservative-choice.

## Chapter 04

- **"Published emergency guidance puts it at roughly four litres per person per day — a gallon — for drinking and basic washing, roughly half of it drinking," rising with heat, hard work, illness, pregnancy, small children and nursing; "in real heat with real work you will need far more than you expect."** Basis: US FEMA/Ready.gov and CDC emergency water-storage guidance, which specifies one gallon (≈3.8 L) per person per day for drinking and sanitation, roughly half of it for drinking, and states that hot weather, children, nursing mothers, the sick and pregnant women require more. Kept numerically consistent with chapter 02. The chapter gives no multiplier for heat, because the cited sources give none; an earlier draft said "twice the figure" and that figure was removed rather than sourced loosely. Confidence: high.
- **"Do not ration it. Your body does not bank water... losing a couple of per cent of your weight in water measurably takes your attention and your temper, which are the tools you find water with."** Basis: the dehydration-and-cognition literature, in which fluid loss of roughly 1–2% of body mass produces measurable decrements in vigilance/attention and in mood and irritability; and the desert-survival finding (Adolph, *Physiology of Man in the Desert*, and the doctrine derived from it) that water should be drunk rather than conserved, because the body cannot store it and voluntary dehydration degrades performance. The claim is deliberately limited to attention and temper: effects on short-term/working memory at this level of deficit are less consistently replicated, so that clause was dropped rather than asserted. Confidence: high.
- **The sustained-load figure: "a third is where degradation starts, not a load to plan on, and what a person walks under day after day without coming apart is nearer a fifth: fourteen kilos at seventy."** Basis: the load-carriage literature already cited for chapter 02, in which loads beyond roughly 30% of body mass produce marked degradation in gait economy, speed, balance and injury rate — i.e. one-third is an onset-of-degradation threshold, not a sustainable working load. Sustainable long-duration civilian load-carriage practice (long-distance walking and portering guidance) puts a repeatable load nearer 20% of body mass, and occupational over-use injury rates rise with repeated days under load. The chapter therefore uses a fifth as the planning figure and a third as the degradation line, and says which is which. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **The water-carry figure, derived in text: a litre weighs a kilogram; four litres a day is four kilograms; a fifth of seventy kilos is fourteen; chapter 02's six-item bag takes six or seven of that; so the honest budget is seven or eight litres, which at four a day is under two days. "Three days of water is twelve kilos, and twelve with the bag on top is eighteen or nineteen — past the load you can repeat day after day, and closing on the one that degrades you outright."** Basis: the density of water (1 kg/L) plus the two load figures above. This is arithmetic from sourced premises, not an independent claim, and the chapter shows the working so a reader can check it; the arithmetic closes in both directions — 14 − 6/7 = 7/8 litres, and 12 + 6/7 = 18/19 kg, which is above the 14 kg repeatable load and **below but approaching** the 23 kg (one third at seventy) where chapter 02's degradation begins to show. An earlier draft of both chapter and entry described 18–19 kg as being inside the degradation band, which is arithmetically false and is the error this wording corrects. The chapter's conclusion — that the operative number is distance to the next water, not a volume — is the conservative framing. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Filtering is not treating." A filter removes particulates and, at a fine enough pore, larger organisms, but does not remove viruses, dissolved chemicals, fuel, salt or agricultural run-off; filters also clog and fail silently.** Basis: CDC and WHO guidance on household water treatment, which distinguishes filtration from disinfection and states that most portable filters do not remove viruses because virus particles are far smaller than achievable pore sizes (sub-0.1 micron), and that filtration does not address dissolved chemical contamination. The chapter's order — filter or settle first, then disinfect — is the sequence both bodies recommend. Confidence: high.
- **The improvised barrel filter, taught in prose as well as in Figure 4.2: coarse gravel in the bottom of a cut drum over a spout, a deep bed of fine sand above it (the sand is the working layer and is usually made too thin), charcoal on top, doubled cloth over the mouth, poured slowly — and "it removes nothing living you can rely on," so the pot on the fire under the spout is not optional.** Basis: slow-sand and improvised-filter practice as described in WHO and field water-treatment literature: a deep, fine sand bed with slow flow does the particulate and some biological reduction, gravel supports the bed and drains to the outlet, activated or wood charcoal adsorbs some taste and organics, and cloth pre-screens coarse matter. The load-bearing claim in the chapter is the negative one — that an improvised sand-and-charcoal column has no verifiable pathogen-removal performance and must be followed by boiling or chemical disinfection — which is the standing position of both bodies on improvised filters. The chapter claims no removal percentage and no pore size for it. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Bring it to a rolling boil... hold it one minute; above about two thousand metres, three, because water boils cooler the higher you go... Three minutes costs only fuel, so boil three if you do not know how high you are."** Basis: CDC "Making Water Safe in an Emergency" and WHO guidance: rolling boil for one minute; at elevations above 6,500 feet (approximately 2,000 m), boil for three minutes. The stated reason — reduced boiling point at altitude — is the published rationale. Defaulting to three minutes when altitude is unknown is the conservative choice and costs only fuel. Confidence: high (with the default-to-three a conservative-choice).
- **"Cover it while it cools."** Basis: WASH guidance on recontamination of treated water — treated water must be stored covered, because the commonest route back to contamination is handling and exposure after treatment. Confidence: high.
- **"What it does not do is remove chemicals, salt or metal; it concentrates them... right for a pond and wrong for floodwater: a pond's problem is alive, floodwater's is not."** Basis: basic physical chemistry — evaporation removes water and leaves non-volatile solutes behind — and the standing public-health instruction never to boil water suspected of chemical or fuel contamination. Confidence: high.
- **Chemical treatment: unscented household bleach, sodium hypochlorite at five to nine per cent with no scent, colour-safe additive, detergent, cleaner or thickener; read the label and do not use it if the label does not state the contents.** Basis: **CDC, "Making Water Safe in an Emergency,"** and **US EPA, "Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water,"** both of which restrict the method to plain unscented sodium hypochlorite bleach in that strength band and warn against scented, colour-safe and additive-bearing products. Confidence: high.
- **The dose, given metric-primary: an eighth of a teaspoon per four litres of clear water, about two drops a litre, with "four litres being the gallon the guidance is written in"; doubled for water still cloudy after settling (a quarter teaspoon per four litres, four drops a litre); stirred, covered, stood thirty minutes.** Basis: the same CDC and EPA documents, which give 1/8 teaspoon (≈8 drops, ≈0.75 mL) of unscented 5–9% sodium hypochlorite per US gallon of clear water and 1/4 teaspoon for cloudy, with a 30-minute contact time. The chapter renders this per four litres and per litre because the book is otherwise metric and a reader who takes "gallon" as imperial (4.55 L) would under-dose by about a fifth; the per-four-litre rendering is within about six per cent of the per-US-gallon figure, errs by at most that much on the dilute side, and the mandatory chlorine-smell check and re-dose step catch an under-dose. Where the two sources differ in presentation (drops vs. millilitres, and EPA's separate 8.25% table) the chapter takes the simpler lower-strength CDC framing. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"An hour in cold water, below about ten degrees, and two near freezing, because chlorine works far more slowly cold."** Basis: chlorine disinfection kinetics — inactivation rate falls sharply with temperature, and published CT (concentration × time) guidance requires substantially longer contact at low temperatures; field and expedition water-treatment guidance for halogens instructs doubling contact time in cold water, conventionally taken as below about 10 °C, and roughly doubling again toward freezing. The chapter now gives both steps — one hour below ten degrees, two near freezing — because an earlier draft gave a single hour for the near-freezing case the basis says needs two, and left "cold" undefined so a reader in eight-degree water had no instruction. Ten degrees is named as the threshold; no finer table is given. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **The cloudy-water caveat: cloudy water must be strained or settled first regardless; if it is still cloudy after that the dose doubles, because the suspended organic matter consumes the chlorine before it reaches anything alive; and doubling does not rescue genuinely dirty water.** Basis: the same CDC and EPA documents, which both instruct that cloudy water be filtered through cloth or allowed to settle before treatment, and that the dose be doubled for water that remains cloudy; the chlorine-demand mechanism is standard water-treatment chemistry. The "doubling does not rescue it" sentence is the conservative reading added so the guidance cannot be misread as "dirty water just needs more bleach." Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"So settling pays twice: settled water takes the ordinary dose, and only water still cloudy after settling takes the doubled one. Doubling is never a way round settling. And the ordinary dose is a floor — nothing earns you less, however clear the water looks."** Basis: the published doses are minima for the stated clarity condition; no CDC or EPA guidance authorises reducing the clear-water dose for any degree of clarity. An earlier draft said settled water "takes half the bleach," which would have licensed dosing below the floor; the claim is now that settling returns you from the doubled dose to the ordinary one, and that the ordinary one cannot be reduced. The clause was further reworded after a draft that read "unsettled the doubled one," which gave the last word to the reading the preceding guard forbids — the doubled dose is for water **still cloudy after** settling, never for water settling was skipped on. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"A parasite common in surface water is not reliably killed by chlorine at any dose you would willingly drink. Boiling kills it; so does a bought filter rated a micron absolute or finer, which a barrel of sand and charcoal is not."** Basis: CDC guidance that *Cryptosporidium* is highly resistant to chlorine at household disinfection doses and contact times, and that boiling or filtration with an **absolute** pore size of 1 micron or smaller is required — a nominally rated filter does not reliably exclude the cyst, which is why the chapter says "absolute"; *Giardia* also requires longer contact than the standard thirty minutes. The chapter does not name the organism (the narrator would have no name for it) but states the operational consequence, defines the filter by its rated absolute pore size, and says explicitly that an improvised sand-and-charcoal barrel does not qualify. The two branches are kept apart: having fuel means boil (and boil regardless of filtering, for standing water or water from where people and animals go); having neither fuel nor a bought filter means settle, treat, and know the gap remains. An earlier draft instructed boiling and bought-filter use inside the branch defined by having neither. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Stored household water sources: the hot-water tank (power or gas off first, a tap opened upstairs to admit air, drawn from the drain cock at the bottom), the pipes (highest tap opened to break the vacuum, caught at the lowest), the lavatory cistern tank — never the bowl, "because that side is the drain" — and not if a cleaning block is present, liquid in tinned food.** Basis: FEMA/Ready.gov and American Red Cross emergency-water guidance, which lists exactly these household sources with these cautions, including the toilet-tank-not-bowl and no-chemical-block conditions and the instruction to shut off power or gas to a water heater before drawing from it. The reason given for the bowl prohibition is the plumbing: the bowl is on the waste side of the trap. Confidence: high.
- **A filled bath, and anything else that has stood in an open container, is washing and flushing water until treated.** Basis: FEMA/Red Cross guidance treats water stored in an open, uncovered or unsanitised container as non-potable without disinfection, and WASH practice treats open storage as a primary recontamination route. Chapter 01's bath is therefore cited here as a water source, not as a drinking source — the conservative position, corrected from an earlier draft that listed it as drinkable. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Water never to be drunk: floodwater, seawater and urine; and pool or spa water is for washing and flushing, not drinking. A smell of fuel or chemical means refusing that water outright, treated or not.** Basis: public-health flood guidance that floodwater must be assumed to contain sewage, fuel and household chemicals that treatment cannot remove; the physiology of seawater and urine, whose solute loads require more water to excrete than they supply; CDC/FEMA guidance that pool and spa water may be used for sanitation but not as drinking water; and the standing instruction that water suspected of chemical or petroleum contamination must be discarded rather than treated, because neither boiling nor chlorination removes it. Confidence: high.
- **Rain catchment: divert the first few minutes of a downpour before catching, because that is the roof washing itself.** Basis: standard rainwater-harvesting practice ("first flush" diversion), recommended to discard the initial run-off that carries roof debris, droppings and deposited particulates. Published guidance specifies a diverted depth or volume rather than a duration, so the chapter says "the first few minutes" rather than naming a figure it cannot source. Confidence: vague-on-purpose.
- **Settling and decanting as pre-treatment: strain through doubled cloth, stand undisturbed for hours or overnight, then decant the middle band with a tube rather than by tipping, or ladle off the top with a cup held flat where there is no tubing; repeat passes with two dedicated containers, one marked dirty.** Basis: plain sedimentation, the oldest step in water treatment and the pre-treatment method recommended by WHO household-water-treatment guidance where no filter is available; cloth straining is documented as removing coarse particulates only. The cross-contamination rule (dedicated clean and dirty containers) is standard WASH practice for stored household water. Confidence: high.

## Chapter 05

- **"I got hypothermic at four degrees above freezing... wet, sitting still, cheerful: that is where people who think they understand hypothermia get it."** Basis: wilderness and occupational medicine consistently place most accidental hypothermia in cool, wet, windy conditions well above freezing rather than in extreme cold — the commonly described range for non-immersion wilderness hypothermia is roughly freezing to about ten degrees Celsius, with wet clothing and wind as the dominant contributors. The chapter gives no incidence figure and no onset time, because the sources give none that is transferable. Confidence: high.
- **"Water conducts heat about twenty-five times better than air, which is why a wet layer takes it off you several times faster than a dry one."** Basis: thermal conductivity of water is about 0.6 W/m·K against about 0.026 W/m·K for air, a ratio of roughly twenty-three to twenty-five. The twenty-five is therefore a conductivity ratio and is stated as one; the consequence for a person is given separately and vaguely, as "several times faster", because whole-body heat loss in water is usually cited at a much lower multiple (around five times) once convection, clothing and body composition are included. An earlier draft of this chapter wrote "water takes it off you twenty-five times faster", which asserted a cooling rate no source supports, and was corrected in review. The same correction was made in the matching Short Version bullet. Confidence: high.
- **"Wind... strips off the thin shell of already-warmed air your clothes are holding, which is the only thing any clothing does," and "Insulation is not a material. It is trapped air."** Basis: textbook convective heat transfer and clothing-insulation theory — garment insulation is a function of trapped still air, i.e. of loft thickness, and forced convection removes the warmed boundary layer, which is the mechanism behind wind chill. Wind chill is correctly treated as affecting the rate of heat loss, not as lowering the air temperature. Confidence: high.
- **"Three loose layers beat one thick one... Shed one *before* you sweat, not after, because sweat is how you get wet on a day when it is not raining."** Basis: standard cold-weather layering doctrine (wicking base, insulating mid, wind and water-resistant shell) and the well-documented danger of sweat-wetted insulation; venting before sweating is the conventional instruction in military and mountaineering cold-injury guidance. Confidence: high.
- **"Cover your head, because it is the part usually left bare and one you cannot easily shut the blood supply down to."** Basis: the often-repeated claim that 40–45% of body heat is lost through the head is a myth; measured head heat loss is broadly proportional to its share of body surface area (roughly 7–10%), though scalp vasoconstriction is less effective than in the limbs. The chapter states the instruction, gives the real reason, and explicitly refuses a percentage. Confidence: high.
- **"The ground... touches the whole length of you, and it never tires... the loft underneath you is crushed flat by your own weight all night, so the underside of a bag does almost nothing. Something under you before something over you."** Basis: conductive heat loss to the ground is a major route in a lying person, which is why sleeping pads are rated by R-value and why compressed insulation under the body loses most of its value; standard wilderness-medicine and mountaineering practice is to insulate from the ground before adding covering. Consistent with chapter 03's shock instruction. The chapter gives depths as hand's-breadths rather than a fabricated R-value. The depths themselves are given as body measures — a hand's depth after compression, two over snow or frozen ground — on the basis of standard debris-shelter and bough-bed practice, in which bedding is built to roughly that depth and deeper over frozen ground, and deliberately not as an R-value, because no source supports a numerical R-value for improvised vegetation. Confidence: high.
- **"Hypothermia arrives in a fixed order": fumbling, untidy walking, mumbled speech, mood change and poor decisions, all while still shivering hard.** Basis: the "umbles" (stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles) taught in wilderness medicine; mild hypothermia presents with shivering, loss of fine motor control, dysarthria, apathy or irritability and impaired judgement — including the documented behaviour of removing clothing or refusing to stop. Confidence: high.
- **"Shivering stopping is not improvement... Stiff, slow, confused and no longer shivering is worse than shivering violently."** Basis: shivering thermogenesis fails as core temperature falls (it is typically lost in the moderate range, around 30–32 °C) so cessation of shivering together with altered mental status marks progression, not recovery; this is explicit in Wilderness Medical Society hypothermia practice guidelines and in standard staging. No core temperatures are quoted in the chapter because a reader cannot measure one. Confidence: high.
- **"A very cold person can look dead: look and listen for breathing far longer than feels necessary before concluding anything, and keep them wrapped and gently handled while you do."** Basis: profound hypothermia produces extreme bradycardia, shallow and infrequent respiration and absent peripheral pulses, so vital signs can be undetectable in a living patient; this is the ground of the standard maxim that a hypothermic patient is not dead until warm and dead, and of the guidance to extend the breathing and pulse check (commonly a full minute) before concluding. The gentle-handling instruction is carried over from the arrhythmia caution below. Added in review as the one major hypothermia caution the chapter had omitted. Confidence: high.
- **Rewarming: wet clothing off; insulation under and over; head covered; a non-breathing outer layer.** Basis: the "hypothermia wrap" in Wilderness Medical Society guidelines — remove wet clothing, apply a vapour barrier, insulate, and insulate from the ground. Confidence: high.
- **"Heat on the trunk, not the limbs... Warming arms and legs first opens the cold blood standing in them and sends it to the middle, which drops them further and can stop the heart."** Basis: active external rewarming is directed at the trunk (chest, axillae, neck, groin) in standard guidance; peripheral rewarming promotes vasodilation, central return of cold blood and afterdrop, with hypotension and arrhythmia risk. Heat sources are applied through cloth because cold, anaesthetised skin burns readily. Confidence: high.
- **"No hot bath for anybody who has stopped shivering or making sense."** Basis: contested area. Warm-water immersion is accepted for mild hypothermia in an alert, shivering patient, but is advised against for moderate-to-severe hypothermia because of vasodilation, afterdrop and hypotension. The chapter takes the conservative position and sets the dividing line at signs a lay rescuer can actually read — shivering and sense — rather than at a temperature. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Warm sweet drinks only if they are fully awake and can hold the cup and swallow... A confused person chokes."** Basis: standard guidance permits warm sweet oral fluids for alert hypothermic patients (carbohydrate fuels shivering) and prohibits oral intake where consciousness or swallowing is impaired, because of aspiration risk. Confidence: high.
- **"No rubbing, slapping, massaging the limbs or walking them about... a very cold heart is twitchy, so rough handling can stop it. And frozen flesh is full of ice, so rubbing tears it from the inside."** Basis: three separate established cautions — gentle, horizontal handling of the hypothermic patient because the cold myocardium is irritable and rough movement or exertion can precipitate ventricular fibrillation; massaging the extremities drives cold peripheral blood centrally; and frostbitten tissue must never be rubbed because ice crystals cause direct mechanical injury. Each is given with its reason in the same breath as the prohibition. Confidence: high.
- **"No alcohol... It feels like warmth because it opens the vessels in the skin, and what you feel is your heat leaving faster. It also blunts the shivering and the judgement."** Basis: ethanol causes cutaneous vasodilation (increasing heat loss while producing a subjective sensation of warmth), impairs shivering thermogenesis and thermoregulatory behaviour, and is a recognised contributor to hypothermia deaths. Confidence: high.
- **Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating; pale, cool, clammy skin; fast weak pulse; fast breathing; headache; dizziness; nausea; cramp in the big muscles — and the person still mentally intact. Treatment: stop work, shade, lie flat, loosen clothing, wet them, air moving, sip fluids.** Basis: CDC, NHS and OSHA heat-illness guidance, which lists these signs for heat exhaustion and prescribes rest in a cool place, loosened clothing, cool cloths or a cool bath, and sips of water. Confidence: high.
- **"Better inside half an hour; if not, treat it as the other thing."** Basis: public guidance commonly says to seek medical help if heat exhaustion does not improve within about an hour, or if the person vomits or worsens. The chapter deliberately halves that window because the escalation it prescribes is cooling — which is harmless in heat exhaustion and life-saving in heat stroke — and because no reader of this book can summon an ambulance. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Heat stroke. Defined by altered mental state... The skin is hot, and it may be bone dry or soaking, which is why I will not let you use sweat as the test."** Basis: CDC and OSHA both define heat stroke by high body temperature with altered mental status (confusion, slurred speech, agitation, seizure, loss of consciousness), and both note skin may be hot and dry *or* damp; exertional heat stroke characteristically presents with profuse sweating, so the "hot dry skin" rule of thumb is a known cause of missed diagnosis. Confidence: high.
- **Active cooling: cold water up to the neck if available; otherwise strip, soak and fan continuously with cold applied to neck, armpits and groin, re-wetting constantly; nothing by mouth while confused; cool before moving.** Basis: cold-water immersion is the established first-line treatment for exertional heat stroke, with the "cool first, transport second" principle; where immersion is impossible, continuous evaporative cooling (wetting plus forced air movement) with ice or cold packs to the neck, axillae and groin is the accepted substitute, and still water on skin ceases to cool once it equilibrates. Oral fluids are withheld from anyone with altered mental status. Confidence: high.
- **"Keep cooling until they make sense, then stop, dry them, keep them shaded and watch, because they can go off again."** Basis: heat-stroke protocols stop active cooling once the patient is cooled rather than continuing indefinitely, to avoid overshoot into hypothermia, and keep the patient monitored because temperature can rebound and because organ injury evolves after the initial episode. The chapter sets the stopping point at a sign a lay rescuer can read — the person making sense — rather than at a core temperature. Added in review; the chapter previously gave cooling no endpoint. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Work the cool hours and sleep the hot ones... Drink ahead of thirst — Rule 9 — and eat with your water when sweating hard all day, because sweat takes salt out of you as well as water... Come at hot work gradually over a week or two."** Basis: OSHA/NIOSH heat guidance — schedule heavy work for the cooler parts of the day, use shade and work/rest cycles, drink before thirst, replace electrolytes through normal meals rather than water alone, and acclimatise progressively over roughly one to two weeks. "The worst three or four hours of the afternoon" is the same guidance stated as a body clock rather than a shift pattern: peak heat load lags solar noon by a few hours, so the hottest part of the day falls in the early-to-mid afternoon, which is the window the published work/rest schedules protect. The chapter gives no litre-per-hour figure, because the published ones are workplace-specific. Confidence: high.
- **"A tent or a vehicle in sun is an oven for hours."** Basis: well-documented rapid interior heating of closed vehicles far above ambient temperature, with the interior remaining dangerously hot long after direct sun is off it; the same applies to closed tents. No temperature figure is given. Confidence: high.
- **"A person sitting still puts out roughly a hundred watts: nothing for a hall, quite a lot for a box," and Rule 34, build small.** Basis: resting adult metabolic heat output is conventionally taken as about 100 W (the standard figure in human physiology and in HVAC load calculation). The practical conclusion — that body heat can meaningfully warm a small insulated enclosure and cannot warm a large one — follows from that output against enclosure volume and surface area. Confidence: high.
- **Shelter: small, floor insulated, entrance small and offset, ventilated low and high.** Basis: standard survival-shelter doctrine; the oversized unheatable debris shelter is the documented beginner error, and low-and-high openings give the convective path needed both to clear combustion products and to carry out moisture. Confidence: high.
- **Fuel laddering: tinder, match-thick, pencil-thick, thumb-thick, wrist-thick, all gathered before ignition.** Basis: conventional fire-craft, in which graded fuel is prepared in advance because the common failure is a fire dying while its builder goes looking for the next grade up. Confidence: high.
- **"Carbon monoxide has no colour, no smell and no taste, and anything burning makes it — most of all anything burning badly: a starved stove, a brazier, an engine, and charcoal, which is the worst and must never be lit indoors," and "Never charcoal, never a brazier, never an engine or generator in a room or a doorway."** Basis: CDC and NHS carbon-monoxide guidance. CO is a colourless, odourless, tasteless product of incomplete combustion; charcoal grills, fuel-burning stoves, heaters, lanterns and engines or generators must never be used indoors or in enclosed or **partially enclosed** spaces. The doorway is named in the prohibition on purpose: a doorway is the partially enclosed case the sources call out, and it is the one people think is ventilated. An intermediate draft carried the doorway only in the sources list, where it was description rather than instruction, and the brazier was then the one source with no room-or-doorway prohibition attached; corrected in review. Confidence: high.
- **"What it does to you is a headache, then sickness, then weakness, then confusion, then an enormous sleepiness... exactly the list for a cold, wet, exhausted person at the end of a hard day... so the person feeling them lies down."** Basis: documented CO symptoms are headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea and vomiting, confusion and drowsiness, explicitly described in public-health guidance as flu-like and easily mistaken, and victims frequently lose consciousness before recognising the cause — which is why poisoning is so often fatal indoors and at night. The chapter makes the mimicry explicit rather than leaving the reader to infer it. Confidence: high.
- **"An opening low for air in, an opening high for smoke out, both unblocked... A headache indoors with a fire burning is enough on its own. One person is enough: get out into open air. Two at once and everybody goes, including whoever feels fine."** Basis: CO guidance directs anyone with suspected symptoms to leave immediately for fresh air and not to re-enter until the space has been ventilated and the source dealt with — the threshold is one person's symptoms, not two people's. Simultaneous symptoms in several occupants is a confirmatory indicator of an environmental rather than individual cause, which is why it escalates to moving everybody including the asymptomatic. An earlier draft stated only the two-person case, which left a lone occupant, or the first of several to feel it, able to reason that one headache was below the threshold; corrected in review in both the prose and the Short Version. The chapter-01 cross-reference to *can I breathe where I am standing?* was restored at the same time, because it is the reader's route to that chapter's enclosed-space rule. Confidence: conservative-choice.

## Chapter 06

- **"A grown person lying still and warm needs somewhere around two thousand to two and a half thousand calories a day to hold their weight. That is a figure for a person in a chair."** Basis: national dietary-guideline estimated energy requirements for adults, which fall roughly between 1,600 and 2,400 kcal/day for sedentary women and 2,000 to 2,600 for sedentary men. The chapter gives a single rounded band rather than sex- and age-split figures, because the reader cannot look up a table, and it labels the band explicitly as the sedentary case. Confidence: high.
- **"Dig, haul and walk all day and you are into three to four and a half thousand, and people working hard in cold weather under a load have been measured higher again."** Basis: occupational and military energy-expenditure measurements (doubly labelled water studies) for heavy manual work, which commonly give total daily expenditures in the 3,000–4,500 kcal range, with cold-weather load-carrying operations and expedition work measured substantially above that (figures above 5,000 kcal/day are documented). The chapter gives no upper figure at all, only "higher again", because the published high values come from specific populations and are not transferable. Confidence: high.
- **"A working survivor needs something like half again to twice what a hiding one needs, so if the stores are short the lever you pull is the work."** Basis: this is the arithmetic of the two bands above (roughly 2,000–2,500 against roughly 3,000–4,500), stated as a ratio rather than a new number. The practical conclusion — reduce workload rather than attempt heavy labour on a deficit — follows from established findings on semi-starvation (notably the Minnesota Starvation Experiment) that work capacity, strength and judgement degrade with sustained energy deficit, and that lost lean mass is regained slowly. Confidence: high.
- **"A fit person with water goes weeks without food."** Basis: documented survival in total starvation with water available is measured in weeks to months depending on starting body composition; clinically significant impairment arrives much earlier than death. The chapter deliberately gives no number of days and makes no promise, and pairs the claim with the warning that the person is "stupid and miserable" — i.e. it is used only to rank food below water, never as reassurance. Confidence: vague-on-purpose.
- **"Whole dry grains and whole dry legumes... Kept properly they last years," and "Milled things go sooner, because the oil in flour and meal has been opened to the air: months, where whole grain kept years."** Basis: standard food-storage guidance. Whole dry cereal grains and dry legumes stored cool, dry, dark and sealed against moisture, insects and rodents retain edibility for years (long-term storage guidance commonly cites many years for wheat, rice and dry beans, with legumes hardening and needing longer cooking as they age). Milled flour and meal have a markedly shorter life because milling exposes the germ oils to oxidation — months rather than years at room temperature, and whole-grain flours shorter than refined. The chapter gives ranges by order of magnitude ("years", "months") and no specific shelf-life figures. Confidence: high.
- **"Fats go first, and fat is what you will want most. Oil, rendered fat, nuts and seeds all carry their own oil and all go rancid — months rather than years, faster in warmth, light and air."** Basis: lipid oxidation and hydrolytic rancidity are accelerated by heat, light, oxygen and metal contact, which is the standard basis for storing oils cool, dark, airtight and in a container filled to the top to minimise headspace oxygen; nuts and seeds spoil on the same mechanism because their oil content is high. Fat is the most energy-dense macronutrient (about 9 kcal/g against about 4 for protein and carbohydrate), which is why it is the scarce thing in a grain-heavy diet, and protein-only or very low-fat subsistence is a documented failure mode of lean-meat diets. The chapter asserts the craving as experience, not as physiology, and gives no figure. Confidence: high.
- **"Salt keeps forever... You cannot do heavy work in heat without replacing it, it makes dull food eatable, and it is the only preservative you can carry and trade."** Basis: sodium chloride is a stable mineral and does not spoil; sweat losses of sodium during sustained heavy work in heat are substantial and water replacement alone can produce hyponatraemia, which is why heat-illness guidance directs electrolyte replacement through food or electrolyte drinks (this is the same point chapter 05 makes with "eat with your water"); salting and brining are among the oldest and most equipment-free preservation methods, working by reducing water activity. No quantity of salt per day is given, because the published figures are workplace- and climate-specific. Rule 19 is a statement about scarcity value, not a nutritional claim. Confidence: high.
- **Tins: do not open one that is bulging, domed or springy at the ends; leaking, sticky or rusted through; or damaged across the seam. If it spurts, foams or spits on opening, if the liquid is milky or the contents slimy or discoloured, or if it smells wrong at all, stop.** Basis: this is the standard list in food-safety guidance (FDA, USDA, CDC and NHS all publish versions of it): swollen or bulging ends, leakage, heavy rust or rust-through, cracked or damaged side seams and end seams, spurting liquid on opening, foaming, off odour and unnatural appearance are the recognised signs of a compromised or spoiled can, and *Clostridium botulinum* growth in a low-acid canned food is the specific hazard behind the gas and the bulge. Dents are treated by location in the published guidance: dents on seams or sharp dents that may have broken the seal are the ones that condemn a can, while shallow dents in the body are not. Confidence: high.
- **"Do not taste it to check. Tasting is not a test: the amount that hurts you is smaller than the amount you can taste."** Basis: botulinum toxin is among the most potent toxins known, with an oral human lethal dose in the microgram range, and food-safety authorities state explicitly that suspect canned food must never be tasted. Confidence: high.
- **"A tin that looks fine is not evidence that it is safe, only evidence that it has none of the faults you can see. The worst thing in a tin is invisible, has no smell and does not change the look of the food, so there is no inspection that clears a tin, only one that condemns it."** Basis: botulinum toxin is odourless and tasteless and contaminated food may show no visible or olfactory sign, which is why published guidance frames the signs as reasons to discard rather than their absence as a clearance. This is the chapter's conservative framing of an asymmetric test and it is stated as such. The chapter deliberately does **not** offer the "boil for ten minutes to destroy the toxin" route, which is real (botulinum toxin is heat-labile and inactivated by boiling, though the spores are not) but which requires confidence in the boil, the altitude and the food, and which in a post-collapse setting invites exactly the chancing the section exists to prevent; the conservative position and the reason are given in the same breath. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"A suspect tin does not go on the fire, because a sealed tin in a fire bursts, and it does not go to the dog or the hens... Bury it deep, away from water and from anywhere people or animals dig, unopened if it still is, and wash your hands and whatever it touched."** Basis: sealed containers heated in a fire rupture from internal pressure (the standard warning against heating unopened cans); animals are susceptible to botulism, and veterinary guidance treats spoiled canned food and carrion as a source, so feeding suspect food to livestock or pets is not disposal; public-health guidance on suspected botulism directs that the container be handled as hazardous, disposed of so that people and animals cannot reach it, and that hands and contaminated surfaces be washed. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"The return is poor: wild greens are mostly water and fibre, and you can pick all day and come back with less than the walk cost you. What carries real calories is nuts, seeds, roots and tubers."** Basis: wild leafy greens are high in water and indigestible fibre and very low in energy density (typically in the tens of kcal per 100 g), so net energy return after foraging effort can be negative; ethnographic and nutritional-ecology accounts of foraging diets consistently place the calorie load on nuts, seeds, starchy roots and tubers, and on animal fat, rather than on greens. The chapter gives no kcal figures, because the numbers depend entirely on species and season. Confidence: high.
- **"Getting it wrong is occasionally fatal — the common way to die of a plant is eating something that closely resembles something edible, in the wrong season, in a hurry. Nibbling a corner to see is not a safe test; some of the bad ones work in an amount you would not notice swallowing."** Basis: poison-centre and toxicology literature on plant poisoning: serious and fatal cases are dominated by misidentification of a toxic species as an edible lookalike (the recurring examples are deadly *Amanita* mushrooms taken for edible species, *Conium*, *Cicuta* and *Oenanthe* taken for wild carrot, parsley or parsnip, and *Colchicum* taken for wild garlic), and lethal doses for several of these are small. The "universal edibility test" of tasting a small amount is rejected by toxicologists precisely because small amounts of the dangerous species can still poison and because onset may be delayed by hours. The chapter names no species at all. Confidence: high.
- **Rule 42, and the refusal to teach plant identification: "There are no pictures here, I do not know what grows where you are standing."** Basis: botanical identification is local, seasonal and feature-specific, and reliable teaching requires specimens or good images plus regional range; a text-only description in a general-purpose book cannot support safe identification, and the depth-over-breadth instruction (learn a few species thoroughly, in every season, from someone competent locally) is the standard advice of foraging instructors and of poison-control education. This entry exists to record that the chapter's refusal is deliberate and is itself the safety measure. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Soaking does that part for free, with no fuel, while you are asleep" — eight to twelve hours, overnight; shorter or with changed water in hot weather; soak water thrown away.** Basis: standard dry-legume practice. An overnight soak rehydrates the seed and substantially shortens boiling time, which is a direct fuel saving; published guidance gives roughly eight to twelve hours for a cold soak, warns that a long soak in warm conditions ferments or sours the beans, and directs discarding the soaking water. The reason for discarding it is now stated in the chapter as well as here, because it is a prohibition a hungry reader will want to break and bean water looks like food: the soak water carries the oligosaccharides (raffinose and stachyose) that leach out of the seed and cause flatulence, and, in kidney beans, a share of the leached phytohaemagglutinin — which is also why the chapter says to cook in fresh water rather than reusing it. Added in review; the first draft gave the instruction with the reason in this log only. Confidence: high.
- **"An open pot spends most of its fuel turning water into steam; a lid keeps the heat where you paid for it," and "A full pot holds heat far better than a half-empty one."** Basis: the latent heat of vaporisation of water is very large relative to the energy needed to raise it to the boil, so uncovered boiling loses most input energy as steam — the standard physical reason behind the universal fuel-efficiency advice to cook with a lid on. Heat loss from a vessel scales with its surface area while stored heat scales with the mass of contents, so a fuller pot retains usable heat for longer, which is also why retained-heat cookers specify a full pot. Confidence: high.
- **"Boil hard for ten minutes at the least. A real rolling boil, not a simmer. Some dried beans, red kidney beans worst of all, are genuinely poisonous until they have had that hard boil, and holding them warm but below the boil makes that worse rather than better."** Basis: raw and undercooked red kidney beans contain the lectin phytohaemagglutinin, which causes acute vomiting and diarrhoea; published food-safety guidance (UK FSA and US FDA documentation of kidney bean poisoning) directs soaking, discarding the soak water and **boiling for at least ten minutes**, and specifically records that heating at temperatures below boiling — the classic slow-cooker case, around 80 °C — can increase toxicity by several times relative to raw beans. This is the direct and necessary safety caveat on any retained-heat method, which is why the chapter makes the hard boil a step that may not be shortened and says so in the same breath as the reason. Confidence: high.
- **"Keep acid — tomato, vinegar, sour fruit — out until the beans are soft, or the skins stop giving."** Basis: acidic cooking liquid slows the softening of legume skins and cotyledons (pectin and cell-wall chemistry), which is the standard instruction to add tomato, vinegar or lemon late. The chapter deliberately does **not** repeat the common companion claim about salt, because the evidence there points the other way — a salted soak tends to soften skins rather than toughen them — so the caution is stated for acid only. Confidence: high.
- **The insulated-pot method: full lidded pot straight off a hard boil, nested in a hand's depth of dry packing with as much underneath as over, unopened, beans three to five hours and whole grains one to two.** Basis: retained-heat cooking — the haybox, and its modern form the insulated "wonder bag" — is a long-established and well-documented technique: a pot brought to a full boil and immediately insulated stays within cooking range for hours and finishes the food without further fuel, with documented fuel savings of a large fraction of normal cooking energy. Insulation under the pot matters as much as over it because conduction to the supporting surface is a major loss path, which is the same physics as chapter 05's ground-insulation point. Times are given as ranges judged by the food, not as precise figures, because they depend on pot size, insulation and the food. Confidence: high.
- **"Do not open it to look. Every look costs about an hour's worth of heat."** Basis: opening the insulated enclosure releases the accumulated warm air and the pot's lid, which is the whole stored-energy reservoir of the method, and retained-heat-cooker instructions uniformly say not to open the box during cooking. The "about an hour's worth" is given as an approximation with a hedge, because no source gives a transferable figure for an improvised box. Confidence: vague-on-purpose.
- **"Eat it that day. It comes out warm rather than hot, having cooled slowly for hours, which is the condition in which food goes bad fastest. This cooks a meal; it does not keep one. Anything left over is boiled hard again before eating, once."** Basis: food-safety guidance on the temperature danger zone (roughly 5–60 °C) and on cooling cooked food quickly rather than slowly; a retained-heat pot by design spends hours falling through the upper part of that range, so the food is safe as a meal eaten the same day and is a poor candidate for keeping. Reheating guidance directs heating leftovers thoroughly and reheating only once. This is the one safety caveat retained-heat cooking literature most often leaves implicit, and the chapter states it explicitly. Confidence: conservative-choice.

## Chapter 07

- **"Most people are not a threat."** Basis: disaster sociology. The Disaster Research Center tradition (Quarantelli, Dynes, and later Tierney's work on disaster myths) finds repeatedly that the dominant behaviour of ordinary people in catastrophe is prosocial — improvised rescue, sharing, and the formation of emergent groups — and that mass panic, widespread looting and predation on strangers are over-reported relative to what is observed. The chapter uses this only to set the base rate before giving the reader a list of warning signs, and does not claim that no one is dangerous. Confidence: high.
- **"Treating everybody as one kills you too, only slower: you carry all your own water, mend your own roof, and die of the first job that needed two people," and the closing cost, "caution itself costs years."** Basis: two things, and the chapter is honest that the second is the narrator's own. First, the epidemiology of social isolation: meta-analyses of prospective studies (Holt-Lunstad and colleagues) find social isolation and loneliness associated with substantially increased all-cause mortality, comparable in effect size to several conventional risk factors. Second, the specific mechanism the chapter names — that solitary survival removes redundancy for tasks that require two people — is the narrator's experience and is logged as such rather than as evidence. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Reading a settlement from outside: the condition of its water and queue, its children, its animals, its fences, and what has been mended.** Basis: this is the structure of a real rapid-assessment practice. Humanitarian assessment frameworks (the Sphere Handbook's WASH and food-security indicators, and multi-sector initial rapid assessment tooling) treat distance to the water point and queuing time, the condition and activity of children, livestock condition, and the state of shelter and infrastructure repair as the primary observable welfare indicators of a population, precisely because they can be read from outside without interviewing anybody. The chapter gives no thresholds or numbers, only directions of inference. Confidence: high.
- **"Then find where the waste goes and check it is below the water, not above."** Basis: standard sanitation siting guidance — latrines and waste disposal are sited downhill and downstream of water sources and a stated minimum distance away (commonly 30 m in Sphere and WHO guidance), with the pit base above the water table, because the hazard is faecal contamination of the drawing point. The chapter deliberately gives direction only and no distance, because the real figure depends on soil, slope and water table and the reader cannot survey any of them. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Children out of doors and bored is the best single sign there is," and the animals' ribs and coats.** Basis: child presence, play and age-appropriate activity are used as proxy indicators of household and community stability in child-protection and nutrition assessment, and body condition scoring of livestock is a standard veterinary and livestock-extension method for reading a herd's recent nutritional history. Both are read as trends rather than verdicts in the chapter. Confidence: high.
- **"Plenty of food and no children anywhere is a place you walk past."** Basis: this is the narrator's experience and is logged as such, not as an evidence-based screening rule. Its real-world relative is the protection-assessment principle that a missing age cohort is a question that must be asked rather than an observation to be passed over: humanitarian and anti-trafficking assessment treat the absence of children or of young adults from a population as a prompt to investigate mortality, separation, recruitment or trafficking. The chapter's recommended action is the one with no downside — walk away without investigating — which is why it is the only item in the chapter the narrator says to act on without checking. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Approach technique: daylight, by the route they expect, stopping far enough off to be seen, hands empty and visible, one person forward, three short facts stated and then silence.** Basis: de-escalation and first-contact practice. Crisis de-escalation training (the widely taught models used in healthcare and law enforcement) consistently specifies: approach slowly and from the front, keep hands visible and open, maintain distance, announce who you are and what you want in short simple statements, avoid sudden movement, and then allow silence for a response. Humanitarian security management's acceptance approach rests on the same mechanics for approaching wary or armed communities — be announced, be predictable, move in daylight, never arrive unexpectedly — because surprise, speed and ambiguity are the three things that raise a threat appraisal. Confidence: high.
- **"Being unarmed is not the same as being harmless. Unarmed is a fact about your hands; harmless is their judgement of your intentions."** Basis: threat appraisal is a judgement about both intent and capability, not about visible weapons alone, which is why acceptance-based security practice invests in transparency and predictability rather than in demonstrating disarmament. The chapter's practical conclusion — spend the effort on being predictable, keep any tool stowed with hands off it — follows from that and from the standard de-escalation instruction not to hold an object during a first approach. Confidence: high.
- **"Do not lie about anything checkable: a small lie caught in the first hour costs everything it was protecting."** Basis: research on trust and trust repair finds that trust damaged by detected deception is markedly harder to restore than trust damaged by incompetence, and that early breaches are the most durable, because they revise the other party's model of intent rather than of ability. Confidence: high.
- **Rule 12: "Small first trade. Resolve it the same day," slightly in their favour.** Basis: three established strands. Incremental exchange is the standard mechanism for building trust between parties with no shared history — the trust- and investment-game literature (Deutsch's early experiments, and Berg, Dickhaut and McCabe's investment game) shows trust built by small reciprocated stakes, and the norm of reciprocity (Gouldner) is why a margin in the other party's favour buys disproportionate goodwill. Second, small test transactions before large ones are ordinary commercial practice between strangers. Third, the same-day settlement rule is the countermeasure to tied credit: debt bondage and advance-against-labour arrangements are listed among the ILO's indicators of forced labour precisely because an unresolved obligation is a control mechanism, which is the narrator's three-weeks-for-two-days anecdote in miniature. Confidence: high.
- **The signs that a group is not safe to join: no account of how anybody leaves, possessions into a single-keeper common store on the first day, interest in boots, tools and maps, never being alone with one person, one name nobody contradicts, kindness arriving faster than it was earned and stopping when you stop deciding, being told nobody outside would take you, and short nights with long days and thin food.** Basis: these are recognition items drawn from two established literatures. Lifton's criteria for thought reform and the subsequent clinical literature on high-control groups name milieu control, the absence of private conversation, the dispensing of existence (only insiders are legitimate), demand for purity and the unquestionable leader. The ILO's forced-labour indicators and anti-trafficking screening tools name confiscation or pooling of possessions and documents, restriction of movement, isolation from outsiders, debt bondage, excessive hours, and withholding of food and rest. Sleep restriction and energy deficit are independently documented to degrade judgement and increase suggestibility, which is why that item is on the list. **The chapter states these only as things to notice and as reasons to leave; it gives no instruction that would help anybody run, recruit for or hold a group, and that omission is deliberate.** Confidence: high.
- **Rule 31: "Know the way out before you go in" — settling the four things out loud in advance, keeping boots, a day of water and food and the route reachable and never in the common store, a bag cached outside, and naming the one thing that means you go before going in.** Basis: this is the structure of a safety plan as taught in domestic-abuse and high-risk-exit advocacy: decide the destination and route in advance, keep the means of leaving accessible and separate from shared or controlled property, cache essentials outside the household where possible, keep a trusted outside contact, and fix the trigger for leaving before the situation degrades. The pre-commitment half has independent support in research on implementation intentions (Gollwitzer), where specifying an if-then plan in advance substantially raises follow-through on an intention compared with holding the intention alone. Confidence: high.
- **"It gets harder every day you stay," and "everybody I know who got out, got out on something decided in advance."** Basis: partly mechanism, partly the narrator's observation, and logged as both. The mechanism is well established: escalation of commitment and the sunk-cost effect, effort justification and dissonance reduction after costly investment, and the loyalty term in Hirschman's exit-voice-loyalty account, all predict that the longer and more costly the membership, the higher the threshold for exit. The exit literature on high-control groups reports that planned departures with outside contact and material means are more survivable than unplanned ones. The claim that everybody he knows who left had decided in advance is his and is not an evidence claim. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"Tell somebody outside which place you are walking into and when you expect to be out."** Basis: the ordinary trip-plan and check-in practice of wilderness, maritime and lone-worker safety, and the outside-contact element of a safety plan. This is chapter 01's third question applied to a group rather than a room, and the chapter re-invokes it in chapter 01's exact wording. Confidence: high.
- **Greased hinges and general repair as signals that cannot be faked cheaply.** Basis: maintenance consumes surplus labour and materials, so visible upkeep is evidence of slack and of assigned responsibility; the general principle is costly signalling, where a signal is credible in proportion to what it costs to produce. The specific inference — a dragging gate in an otherwise tidy place means the tidy part is presentation — is the narrator's own and is logged as experience, not as evidence. Confidence: conservative-choice.

## Chapter 08

- **The two load figures: "a fifth of your body weight is what you can walk under day after day without coming apart," and "a third is where degradation starts to show."** Basis: inherited from chapters 02 and 04 and not re-derived here; the underlying basis is logged under those chapters (military load-carriage research, where fighting loads are commonly set near 30% of body weight and performance decrement rises with load fraction, against general backpacking guidance of roughly 20% for a load carried repeatedly). The chapter's only new work is keeping the two straight and saying that the one-third figure is a threshold rather than a plan. Confidence: high.
- **The six categories that always earn their weight: a container you can put on a fire, a cutting edge, cordage, a means of fire needing no fuel of its own, light, and a way to carry water.** Basis: this is the long-standing core-kit convention of field craft, closest to the widely taught "five Cs of survivability" — cutting tool, combustion device, cover, container, cordage — with two deliberate differences stated here for the record. Cover is absent because chapter 05 owns shelter and the ground mat, and light and water carriage are added because they are the two things the narrator's own experience puts above cover in an inhabited landscape. The list is a framework, not a factual claim, and nothing in the chapter turns on the count being six. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **"It boils water, which chapter 4 says is the stage everybody skips."** Basis: chapter 04's logged position — filtration clarifies and does not reliably remove pathogens, and bringing water to a rolling boil is the field treatment that does. WHO and standard emergency-water guidance treat boiling as the reference household treatment. Re-cited here, not re-derived. Confidence: high.
- **Rule 6: "Two methods, not two objects," because "two of the same thing fail of the same thing."** Basis: this is common-cause failure, and the countermeasure is diversity, both of which are established reliability-engineering practice. Identical redundant units share design, age, batch, environment and consumables, so a single cause can take all of them; safety-critical design therefore uses dissimilar or diverse redundancy (diverse hardware channels in aviation and nuclear instrumentation, N-version programming in software) specifically to break the shared failure mode. The chapter's practical form — a lamp paired with a candle rather than with a second identical lamp, a spark rod paired with sealed matches — is that principle applied to a kit, and the narrator's two-lamp anecdote is a textbook shared-consumable common-cause failure. Confidence: high.
- **"Hone a little and often rather than a lot and late, because every time you bring a properly dull edge back you take off steel that does not come back."** Basis: sharpening removes metal; restoring a badly dulled edge requires regrinding the bevel and removes materially more steel than realigning or lightly honing an edge that is only beginning to go. Frequent light honing or stropping is standard practice in trades that depend on edges (butchery, joinery, barbering, surgery instrument care) for exactly this reason, and it keeps the tool in its working range instead of cycling between sharp and blunt. Confidence: high.
- **"Iron needs water and air and nothing else," and the oil film, and "do not store a blade in a leather sheath."** Basis: iron corrodes by electrochemical oxidation requiring both oxygen and water, so corrosion control in practice is drying, a barrier film (oil, grease, wax) and humidity control; corrosion rates rise sharply above roughly 50–60% relative humidity. Museum and arms-conservation guidance specifically warns against long-term storage of steel in leather, because vegetable-tanned leather is hygroscopic, holds moisture against the metal and retains acidic residues and sulphur compounds from tanning that promote corrosion — this is well documented in conservation practice for edged weapons and tools. Confidence: high.
- **"Once it pits the pit is permanent, and a pit is where the thing will one day break."** Basis: pitting corrosion causes permanent loss of section and the pit acts as a stress concentration and a crack initiation site, which is standard fracture-mechanics and failure-analysis knowledge; removing surface rust does not restore the lost metal. Confidence: high.
- **"Rot is alive, it eats the cloth itself, and it needs only damp and time" — nothing stored wet or folded damp.** Basis: cotton, linen and other cellulosic fabrics and natural-fibre ropes are degraded by cellulolytic fungi and bacteria, which require moisture; textile-care and tentage guidance is uniformly to dry canvas fully before storage because mildew attacks the fibre and the strength loss is not recoverable. Confidence: high.
- **"A season standing in direct sunlight takes real strength out of cloth and rope without changing how either looks."** Basis: photodegradation. Ultraviolet exposure breaks down both natural and synthetic fibres and causes measurable tensile-strength loss; rope and webbing manufacturers and climbing and arborist safety guidance all treat UV exposure as a retirement criterion, and strength loss can be substantial before visible change. The chapter gives a season and no percentage on purpose, because the real figure depends on latitude, fibre, dye and exposure hours, none of which the reader can measure. Confidence: vague-on-purpose.
- **"Thread goes before cloth."** Basis: seams are the usual failure point in textile goods, because stitching concentrates load at perforations and the thread is often a different and finer fibre than the fabric, and abrasion at the seam line is higher. This is ordinary sailmaking and tentage repair practice. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Rubber: "it hardens and cracks from the air and the light, first and worst wherever it has been held stretched," stored slack, dark, cool and away from a running motor.** Basis: elastomer ageing is oxidative and ozone-driven; ozone cracking in particular appears on surfaces held under tensile strain, with cracks running perpendicular to the strain, which is why storage guidance for rubber goods (ISO 2230-type recommendations for rubber-product storage) specifies cool, dark, low-ozone conditions and avoidance of stress, stretching and compression during storage. Electric motors and other sources of electrical discharge generate ozone, which is the basis for keeping rubber away from them. Confidence: high.
- **"Tyres and belting were given something like six to ten years when there was anybody to ask, and after that the tread tells you nothing useful."** Basis: tyre-ageing guidance in the real world is a range rather than a figure, and the chapter reproduces the range honestly: several vehicle manufacturers recommend replacement at six years from the date of manufacture regardless of wear, while tyre-industry and standards bodies more commonly cite up to ten years as a maximum service life, and regulators (NHTSA among others) note that ageing degrades tyres independently of tread depth. The chapter asserts only the range and the inference that tread depth is not an age test. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Cells: "a cell left in runs itself down and then leaks, and what comes out crusts the contacts," and "the good dry ones were sold as keeping years rather than decades."** Basis: alkaline primary cells leak potassium hydroxide electrolyte, and leakage is strongly associated with deep discharge and with cells left installed in unused or partly used devices; manufacturers' own instructions say to remove batteries from equipment that will be stored. The leaked electrolyte corrodes contacts and carbonates to a crust. On shelf life, manufacturers' stated storage lives for alkaline cells are typically in the range of five to ten years at room temperature, with lithium primary cells longer and rechargeables shorter; the chapter deliberately says "years rather than decades" rather than a number, both because the spread across chemistries is wide and because a reader forty years after manufacture cannot apply a dated figure to a cell of unknown history. Confidence: vague-on-purpose.
- **Fuel: "petrol goes gummy in months rather than years even sealed," diesel keeps longer "and then grows things in the water at the bottom of the drum," and lamp oil keeps best.** Basis: gasoline degrades by loss of light volatile fractions and by oxidation to gums and varnishes; common stored-fuel guidance gives roughly three to six months of practical storage life untreated, extended to one to two years with stabiliser, and ethanol-blended gasoline is shorter-lived and prone to water uptake and phase separation. Diesel is cited more commonly at six to twelve months untreated, longer with care, with its characteristic problems being water accumulation and microbial contamination at the fuel–water interface (so-called diesel bug) plus oxidation sludge. Kerosene and paraffin lamp oil are the most stable of the three in clean, dry, sealed storage. The chapter gives orders of magnitude and no single numbers, and its operative conclusion — prefer an ignition method needing no liquid fuel — is the conservative one. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **Dry tinder comes from "wood still standing — a dead branch snapped off at head height — rather than anything off the floor, because the floor is where the water is."** Basis: standard fieldcraft and fire-behaviour practice. Dead wood still attached and off the ground sheds water and dries in wind and sun, while ground-contact fuel absorbs soil moisture and holds it; fuel-moisture modelling in wildland fire science treats ground-contact and shaded fuels as persistently wetter than elevated dead fuels. This point was dropped from chapter 05 for budget and is picked up here. Confidence: high.
- **"Nearly everything I have ever picked up off a dead man's shelf failed of neglect and not of use," and Rule 55.** Basis: this is explicitly the narrator's own tally and the chapter says so in the text ("that is my tally and not a measurement"), so it is logged as his observation rather than as evidence. Its real-world relative is well established: corrosion and storage deterioration, not wear in service, account for an enormous share of materiel loss and cost — military and industrial corrosion-cost studies run into the tens of billions of dollars annually in the United States alone — and reliability practice treats preventive maintenance and controlled storage as the primary determinants of equipment availability. No proportion is claimed in the chapter. Confidence: conservative-choice.
- **The three repairability tests, and "anything whose working part is a sealed cell or a ground lens you are borrowing from a world that stopped."** Basis: this is the repairability criteria used by real repair-scoring schemes and right-to-repair assessment — disassemblability with common tools and non-permanent fasteners, availability of spare parts and materials, and the presence of sealed or glued sub-assemblies as the main barriers to repair. The failure-mode half (gradual, inspectable degradation versus sudden failure of a sealed unit) is the ordinary condition-monitoring argument for choosing equipment whose deterioration is observable. Confidence: high.
