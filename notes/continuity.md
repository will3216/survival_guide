# The Long After — Continuity Bible

Reference document. Lives outside `manuscript/`, is never linted. Read this
before naming anything in a chapter — it is the single source of truth for
who is alive, who is dead, what year it is, and which Rule number means what.

Every name recorded here is a name that will appear in `manuscript/`, so
nothing here may collide with `notes/forbidden-terms.txt`. Checked on write.

**Standing note — chapters 1 and 2 are the voice template.**
`manuscript/01-triage.md` and `manuscript/02-run-or-dig-in.md` were rewritten
in the redesigned voice on 2026-11-18 and are now the template chapters 3–20
are written against. Read them for register before writing a chapter: he does
not grade his own past, does not defend his credentials, and does not pause to
be sad. Chapter 01 is carried by bodily consequences, chapter 02 by proximity
to glory facing the wrong way; each carries two T. interventions, at least one
of them exasperated. Nothing of the retired elder-founder voice survives in
either file.

## Narrator

Unnamed throughout the book. People call him by role (the hand, the old man)
or by whatever the current group calls him — never by a fixed name, and the
manuscript never supplies one. Roughly sixty when he writes this. He was an
adult, barely, when the Collapse happened.

**He was a grunt before the Collapse and a grunt after it.** He carried things
for a living. The book never elaborates, because he does not find the question
interesting and changes the subject to whatever he was carrying. He was never
in charge of anything, never asked to be, and has no theory about why. He has
belonged to eleven groups over the years; six are named in the book. In every
one of them he fetched, hauled, dug, queued and held things.

**He is explicitly non-military.** No service, no rank, no unit, no training,
no jargon, no acronyms, no formations, no chain of command. He has never been a
soldier of any kind and never claims a day of it. This is a hard constraint:
anything in that register is wrong and must be cut rather than softened. If a
chapter needs organised group behaviour, it is a work crew, a water rota or a
queue.

**Everything he knows, he learned afterwards, by trial and a great deal of
error.** He has no pre-Collapse expertise to draw on. Every technique in the
book exists because it went badly for him first, usually more than once, and he
will tell you which time.

**He was present for the famous events and understood none of them as they
happened.** He was forty feet away, holding something, facing the wrong way,
sent for a bucket. He does not know there was a story — not that he was a minor
character in one, that one existed at all. He was living his life. It happened
to be the end of the world.

**He is comically upbeat and it never cracks.** See `notes/style-guide.md`
sections 2, 4 and 6.

**Glasses canon — unchanged, and permanent.** He has worn glasses since he was
nine. The left lens cracked on the first morning of Y0, during the hour he spent
patting a floor for them instead of filling a bath (chapter 01), and he is
wearing those same glasses at Y40, the year he writes the book. The crack has
never been repaired and the glasses have never been replaced. There is no
duration to interpret here: assume the cracked left lens at any point from Y0 to
Y40, including while he writes. Nobody is making lenses any more and he knows
it. This is permanent canon from chapter 01 and is available to every later
chapter — chapters 08 (gear), 09 (scavenging), 12 (slow deaths) and 17
especially. Do not give him good eyesight and do not quietly replace the
glasses.

**He does not know the Rules became folklore.** He thinks they are things he
tells himself so he does not repeat a mistake. He never calls them famous, never
mentions anyone else quoting them, and never treats them as a legacy. Only T.
knows otherwise, and T. says so only in the preface. He is the de facto
authority on staying alive and **will never find out**.

**Maintenance note on the first-use column — read this before you add a row or
name a place.** The column is a claim about the whole manuscript, not about the
chapter you are writing, and nothing mechanical checks it. It has now been
wrong twice: once about chapter 13 against chapter 14, and once about the Bell
Country, when chapter 10 named the place in a clause years after the row said
chapter 13 would introduce it. A row goes stale silently, and the writer it
misleads is the one who reads it and believes they are introducing a place that
is already in the book. So: **whenever a chapter names a place that already has
a row here, check that row's first-use claim and correct it in the same commit
as the chapter** — the entry in the What-it-is column as well as the number, so
the prose and the column cannot disagree. Naming a place earlier in manuscript
order than its row says is allowed and is often right; leaving the row saying
otherwise is not.

**Ladderwell is off-limits to the narrator.** He never visits it and never
names it — it belongs to T.'s era, two generations after his own, and
chapters 1 through 20 are all his voice. **No chapter from 1 to 20 may
mention Ladderwell.** This is exactly the collision this table exists to
prevent: a chapter writer reaching for a place name should check here
first.

## Debts owed by later chapters

Obligations the front matter (`manuscript/00-front-matter.md`) created for
chapters not yet written. Check this section before finishing the chapter
named.

- **Chapter 3 owes the long version of direct pressure.** Chapter 01 puts
  "Am I bleeding?" ahead of airway on purpose, gives only the minimum
  technique (look as well as feel; press hard, directly, with whatever is
  already in your hand; do not keep lifting off to check) and says in text
  that "Chapter 3 is the long version and you should read it before you need
  it." Chapter 03 ("The Body Is Your First Vehicle") must therefore carry the
  full treatment of controlling serious external bleeding — direct pressure
  done properly, packing, and whatever it chooses to say about tourniquets —
  or chapter 01's promise to the reader goes unpaid. Chapter 02 also points a
  go-bag line at it ("a small roll of what you use to stop bleeding").

- **The refrigerator debt is paid (chapter 13).** Chapter 13 carries exactly four lines, inside its
  `## Keeping It` section, immediately before the drying method and given as the reason drying is worth
  learning properly: the white box about chest high standing in the kitchen; cold not because you put ice
  in it but because it made itself cold, by itself, all day and all night, for years, while nobody watched
  it; meat in on the Monday eaten on the Thursday and a jug of milk kept a week, gone away from and come
  back to with the cold still going; one in every house he ever walked into and not a working one left
  anywhere he has been. There is no nostalgia in the passage and he does not pause over it. T.'s single
  appreciative beat follows immediately, counting four lines for the box against the rest of the page for
  the rack. **No later chapter may describe a refrigerator again, and none may have him remark on it.**

- **Chapter 13 owed four lines about a refrigerator (original entry, now paid above).** The editor's
  preface says the narrator gives four lines, in his chapter on keeping
  food, to "a thing called a refrigerator: a box that was cold inside, all
  the time, by itself, in every house" — and that the editor has read
  those four lines more often than anything else ever written. Chapter 13
  is "Growing Things, Keeping Things." It must contain four lines
  describing a refrigerator that read, to someone two generations later,
  like the kind of thing worth re-reading with longing — not a throwaway
  mention. The preface has already promised the reader this passage
  exists.

- **Chapter 08 or 12 owes frostbite.** Chapter 05 mentions frozen flesh once,
  inside a prohibition — "frozen flesh is full of ice, so rubbing tears it from
  the inside" — and that single clause is currently the book's entire position
  on frozen tissue. It is a reason for not rubbing, not guidance for treating a
  frozen hand or foot, and a reader could reasonably take it for the whole
  answer. **Whichever of chapter 08 (gear, where boots and gloves live) or
  chapter 12 (the slow deaths) reaches it first must carry the real treatment**
  — what to rewarm and how, what not to do, and the refreeze problem — and say
  plainly that it is the long version of chapter 05's clause. Chapter 05 does
  not point forward at it, because the chapter it should point at is not yet
  assigned; whichever chapter takes the debt should check whether chapter 05
  wants a pointer added at the same time.

- **The frostbite debt is not paid by chapter 08, and now rests with chapter 12.**
  Chapter 08 as briefed carries no boots, gloves or cold-injury material at all —
  its six categories are a container, a cutting edge, cordage, fire, light and
  water carriage — and it had no room for a real rewarming protocol without
  giving the reader a half-treatment, which is worse than none. So **chapter 12
  (the slow deaths) owes the real treatment of frozen tissue**: what to rewarm and
  how, what not to do, and the refreeze problem, said plainly as the long version
  of chapter 05's single clause. Chapter 12 should also check at that point whether
  chapter 05 wants a pointer added. This note is the record of the decision, so the
  debt is not lost by having been offered to two chapters and taken by neither.

- **The frostbite debt is paid (chapter 12).** Chapter 12 carries the real treatment of
  frozen tissue under its own heading, said plainly as the long version of chapter 05's
  clause: the frostnip-versus-frozen split; **warm the person before the part**; **do not
  thaw anything you cannot keep thawed**, because a thaw-refreeze cycle is far worse than
  being left frozen and is what takes fingers, with walking out on a still-frozen foot
  named as the alternative; rewarming by immersion in water no hotter than comfortable
  (the temperature of a pleasant bath, half an hour, until the tissue is soft and red or
  purple to the end, the pain being the thaw and not a reason to stop); **never dry heat**,
  because frozen flesh cannot feel a burn arriving; no rubbing, no walking on it afterwards,
  no broken blisters, padded and dressed and thereafter treated as a wound. **A pointer was
  added to chapter 05** at the end of its rewarming section, so a reader who meets the
  no-rubbing clause there has a route to the treatment. Chapter 12 deliberately carries no
  drug or dose for it. No later chapter owes frostbite.

- **The malnutrition hand-off from chapter 06 is paid (chapter 12), compactly.** Chapter 14's
  report named chapter 12 as the home for deficiency over months. Chapter 12 pays it inside
  its teeth section rather than as its own heading, because that is where the signal shows:
  gums bleeding all round at once with teeth loose in good sockets is named as diet rather
  than dirt — months of the same food with nothing fresh in it, happening to people whose
  bellies are full — with the intervention given as variety rather than quantity in tiny
  amounts, something green daily, and whole grain rather than milled. The named deficiency
  syndromes are described by their signs only and never by name. A later chapter wanting the
  full account of monotonous-ration deficiency (pellagra, beriberi, vitamin A, anaemia) may
  take it; chapter 13 (growing and keeping things) is the natural place. **The whole-grain-
  rather-than-milled point goes with it**: chapter 12's review found it reading as a remedy
  for bleeding gums, which is a different deficiency, so chapter 12 now names only the
  oral signs and the green-food fix, and chapter 12 no longer points at chapter 06.

- **The full deficiency-syndrome account is paid (chapter 13).** Chapter 12 paid the oral signs only and
  handed the rest on. Chapter 13 carries it under `## The Illnesses Of A Full Belly`, with the cause given
  as **months of the same food rather than too little food**, happening to people whose store is not empty,
  and each syndrome **described by its signs and its food fix only, never named clinically**: old wounds
  opening and cuts that will not close, aching legs and bruising from nothing (the oral one further along,
  fixed by a few mouthfuls of anything green and fresh daily, raw or barely cooked, because long boiling
  destroys it and the rest goes out in the cooking water); on a mostly-maize store, loose bowels, a rough
  reddish rash on skin that gets the sun and later confusion that gets a person treated as mad, fixed by
  beans, eggs, offal and greens **and by soaking the maize in wood-ash water before grinding**; on mostly
  milled grain or polished rice, swollen legs with breathlessness or numb, weak, wasting legs, with
  **whole grain rather than milled** named here as its fix and not as a remedy for bleeding gums — the
  correction chapter 12's review asked for; children unable to see at dusk before anybody else loses the
  light, taking sight for good, wanting anything orange or dark green; and pale, breathless exhaustion on
  no work, wanting meat, offal, beans and greens. The chapter's standing instruction is **three or four
  things rather than one, something green daily, whole grain rather than milled where it is your choice,
  and the cooking water kept** — stated as the one exception to Rule 58, about medicine rather than dinner.
  **One gap is recorded rather than hidden:** the account as first written omitted iodine, and a sixth
  prose sign was added in review — necks swelling at the front, inland and away from the sea, wanting
  sea food of any kind or salt from the sea rather than from rock. With that added the account covers
  scurvy, pellagra, beriberi, vitamin A, iron and iodine by their signs. **No later chapter owes
  deficiency**, but a chapter that finds a further gap should add it here rather than treat the list as
  sealed.

- **Tetanus is covered, and chapter 12 is its only home.** Added in review: the risk
  categories (punctures, crush, bites, anything with soil, dung or rust in it), irrigating
  hard and leaving the wound open as the only prophylaxis now available, recognition days
  to weeks later as jaw and neck stiffness then spasms, and the limit that nothing can be
  done without a hospital. **The passage exists because of a collision:** chapter 12 teaches
  inability to open the mouth as a dental-abscess red flag, and in an unvaccinated population
  trismus after a dirty wound is tetanus, not a tooth — the chapter says so in both places.
  No later chapter should re-teach it, and none may reverse the "left open rather than sealed
  tight" instruction.

## Available canon for later chapters

Facts the front matter established that later chapters may draw on
directly. Use them; do not reinvent or contradict them.

- **The three questions are a book-level device, and their wording is
  fixed.** Chapter 01 establishes them, asked in this order, as the spine
  of the whole book. The exact wording is:

  1. **Am I bleeding?**
  2. **Can I breathe where I am standing?**
  3. **Does anyone know where I am?**

  Any later chapter that re-invokes them — in prose, in a Short Version
  bullet, or in an illustration caption — **must reuse this wording
  verbatim and must keep this order**. Do not paraphrase, do not compress,
  do not modernise ("is anyone looking for me", "can I breathe here",
  "am I hurt" are all wrong). Chapter 02 re-invokes all three, in order and
  with the wording above exactly, in one place: the last paragraph of its
  `## Which Way Out` section, immediately before stepping out of the door.
  It also quotes the third question alone twice — in its opening hand-off
  from chapter 01, and once inside `## Staying Still Is Also a Decision` —
  again with the wording unchanged. It does **not** put any of them in its
  Short Version. That is the pattern to copy: full set at the moment of
  acting, the single question wherever that question alone is the one
  deciding something, question marks kept in every case. Re-invoking them
  is optional; changing them is not permitted.

- **Chapter 02 is the chapter chapter 01 named.** Chapter 01's third
  question, *does anyone know where I am?*, carries the in-text line "This
  question decides whether you move, which is the whole of the next
  chapter." That cross-reference points at chapter 02 ("Run or Dig In"),
  which answers it: the shelter-in-place versus evacuate decision, the
  trap of leaving late, the go-bag, and route choice. Chapter 02 picks the
  hand-off up explicitly in its opening. Recorded here so chapter 01's
  forward reference is traceable and so no later chapter claims the same
  hand-off.

- **The Spoke's evacuation discipline (chapter 02).** The Spoke kept a bag
  room with eleven packed bags in it, weighed, labelled and re-checked
  monthly, and walked the whole group out of the house and back in twice a
  year as a drill. Their rule for the stay-or-go decision was Halla Dorn's
  sentence: decide by noon, say it out loud, and move while the light is
  still on your side. They were boring, unglamorous and alive; a house of
  cleverer people down the same valley deliberated for three days and were
  not. Available to any chapter that wants the Spoke's competence as a
  measuring stick — the Spoke's whole character is that preparation looks
  stupid right up until it does not.

- **The Rules are folk-quoted, and quoted wrong.** The editor's preface
  states the Rules are repeated daily, in the yard and over the fire, by
  people who have never read the book and would not read it if handed it
  — and that they get them wrong, and the editor has stopped correcting
  them. This is available canon for **chapter 15** (How Groups Decide
  Things) and **chapter 19** (Holidays, Songs, and Other Load-Bearing
  Nonsense) — the Rules having outgrown the book and become folklore is a
  gift to both a chapter about group governance and a chapter about
  ritual and oral transmission.

- **Chapter 01's own small canon, now fixed.** Edda Mull's two lines are
  "Fill it now. It stops later." and, when he asked her later how she thought
  of the bath, that she had to do something with her hands or she was going to
  start screaming and the bath was the nearest big thing. He searched the
  floor in tea-tray squares, left to right, finishing each square — the
  technique the chapter teaches and the one thing in the morning he does not
  think worth remarking on. He cut both palms on broken glass on the floor
  and noticed an hour later when he dropped a cup. The glasses were in his breast
  pocket the whole time, folded; the lens cracked in the pocket when he knelt
  on it. Any later chapter may reuse these; none may contradict them.

- **The one-flight-of-stairs load test belongs to chapter 02 and is spent
  there.** Put the loaded bag on, carry it up one flight and down again, and
  whatever you take out at the top was never leaving the house with you. It is
  coined in chapter 02 alongside Rule 23 and the roughly-one-third-of-body-
  weight degradation figure. **Chapter 08 (gear) reuses it rather than
  re-coining it** — refer back to it as something already established, do not
  present it as new.

- **The water-carry debt is paid (chapter 04).** Chapter 02 deliberately gave
  no carry figure and deferred to chapter 04. Chapter 04 pays it by arithmetic
  rather than by a new number, and the arithmetic turns on a distinction
  chapter 02 did not draw: **a third of body weight is where degradation
  begins, not a load to plan on, and the load a person repeats day after day
  is nearer a fifth.** A litre weighs a kilogram; four litres a day is four
  kilograms; a fifth of seventy kilos is fourteen; chapter 02's six-item bag
  takes six or seven of that; so the honest water budget is **seven or eight
  litres, which at four a day is under two days.** Three days of water is
  twelve kilos, and twelve with the bag on top is eighteen or nineteen —
  above the repeatable fourteen and closing on the twenty-three where
  chapter 02's degradation begins to show. The
  conclusion the chapter draws is that the number a person needs is not a
  volume: it is the distance to the next water. Later chapters may cite the
  one-fifth sustained-load figure, the seven-or-eight-litre ceiling and the
  "carry to the next water, not for three days" framing rather than
  re-deriving them. **Chapter 08 (gear) in particular should not re-coin
  this, and should use the one-fifth figure for any sustained-load
  discussion while keeping chapter 02's one-third as the degradation
  threshold.**

- **The clever house down the valley from the Spoke.** They deliberated for
  three days and did not leave. He walked back through in the spring and read
  their plan off the kitchen wall in marker, numbered one to nine, and records
  only that it was a good plan to about step four. Available as a measuring
  stick; the household is dead and stays dead, and he never names them.

- **T.'s era, as chapters 01–02 establish it.** Counting before you move is
  taught to children with a song about fingers; the three questions are said
  over a sick child and in the yard before a journey by people who do not know
  where they came from; T. was taught Halla Dorn's sentence before learning to
  read and has said it over a sick child twice; T.'s own household still walks
  the house twice a year as the Spoke did, and says the drill still works.
  This is editor's-era canon and belongs to T.'s voice only — no chapter from
  1 to 20 may know any of it.

- **Chapter 03's own canon, now fixed.** In his third year, with the Ninth
  Hand, the narrator put his right arm through a window reaching for somebody
  on the far side of it, pressed the bleed with a folded cap and lifted the cap
  off about every ten seconds for twenty minutes to see whether it had stopped.
  An unnamed woman of the Ninth Hand knelt, moved his hand two inches onto one
  spot, put her weight on it and held it there without speaking — the origin of
  **Rule 7**. His right thumb has not bent properly since (the window's doing,
  not hers), his left collarbone sits half an inch high and his right little
  finger points at the next one along, the last two from a cart and from
  splinting without immobilising the joints above and below. He does not notice
  that reaching through a window for somebody was brave; he records it as the
  origin of a procedural error. He also does not know who he pulled through,
  and the manuscript never says.

- **Chapter 03 is the one chapter he had checked.** He says so plainly, once,
  in the opening: he took it to **Isak Roon**, the net-mender, who kept it eight
  days and returned four corrections, all of which are in. This is the only
  place in the book where he submits his own work to anyone, and later chapters
  should not repeat the device. T.'s single appreciative beat in chapter 03
  reads the submission — not the technique — as what makes the chapter
  trustworthy, and then confirms the chapter is what T. was taught, nearly word
  for word, and correct.

- **The bleeding debt is paid.** Chapter 03 carries the long version of direct
  pressure chapter 01 promised (one spot, heel of the hand, second hand stacked,
  arms locked, weight not muscle, ten minutes at least, never lifting off),
  wound packing, and the tourniquet — high and tight above the wound, never over
  a joint, tightened until bleeding stops, **time written down**, and **never
  loosened by whoever applied it**. It also pays chapter 02's go-bag line by
  naming the small bleeding roll as what the tourniquet guidance is for. Later
  chapters may refer back to all of this rather than re-teaching it.

- **Chapter 04's own canon, now fixed.** He carried the bucket at the Ninth
  Hand's nightly water ceremony for most of two years, because he was on the
  rota the month it was invented and nobody told him he was finished. The
  ceremony was handprints in slip and ash on the cistern wall; the house's sign
  was nine fingers, so the print was meant to be the left hand with the thumb
  folded under, and most of the valley — including him, for two years — used
  the right. **Pell Hask** spoke from the cistern lid, which the narrator had
  twice asked him not to stand on because it was cracked across the short way
  and the crack had opened to a finger's width. On the night two valleys still
  hold a ceremony for, he was facing the cistern, looking at the crack. He says
  the theatre was doing real work — four hundred people queued in good order
  nightly for two years and he never saw a fight at that cistern — without
  noticing that he has said anything. He does not know there is a version of
  that night, and the contradiction is never argued. **Rule 28** is coined here,
  and earned: he drank beautiful water out of a stone basin and was no use to
  anybody for nine days. The settling-and-decanting method (doubled cloth, a night standing, decant
  off the middle with a tube, two jars with the dirty one marked) is his, and
  T. confirms in chapter 04 that it is still done exactly that way. The
  improvised barrel filter — gravel, a deep bed of fine sand, charcoal, cloth
  over the mouth — is also his, and the chapter's position on it is that it
  makes water clear and removes nothing living you can rely on, so the pot on
  the fire under the spout is the stage everybody skips. Chapter 04 reuses Edda
  Mull's filled bath from chapter 01 as a water source, but classes it, and
  anything else that has stood in an open container, as washing water until
  treated.

- **Chapter 05's own canon, now fixed.** For his first two years he believed
  cold killed by being cold and watched the thermometer. In his second year he
  got hypothermic at **four degrees above freezing** — a figure he has from
  **Marta Lunn**, who had a thermometer wired to the outside of her bag —
  after a morning to the waist in a ditch in steady rain, soaked through to
  the shirt, sitting out of the wind, eating, in an extremely good mood.
  Somebody got him dry and **he does not know who**, which he offers as a
  symptom rather than a regret. **Rule 16** is coined here and earned by that
  lunch. **Rule 34** is coined here and earned by his first shelter: it slept
  nine, took eleven days, was beautifully made, never got warm once, and he
  ended up sleeping by the door on the ground. He has **lost more people to
  enclosed fires than to the cold they were escaping**, said once and walked
  straight past on the way to the flue; that line is this chapter's dark beat
  and should not be re-used as if it were new. Also his and available to later
  chapters: *something under you before something over you* (ground
  conduction, loft crushed by your own weight, a hand's depth of dry packing
  and two over snow), the shivering-stops-is-worse recognition, the
  trunk-not-limbs rewarming order, and the heat-exhaustion-against-heat-stroke
  distinction turning on **altered mental state**. T.'s single beat here is
  exasperated: T. burns four pages of notes rather than transcribe them, then
  corrects the ditch-versus-drain wording instead of the point. Chapter 05
  re-cites **Rule 9** (chapter 04) for drinking ahead of thirst in heat and
  does not re-derive any water figure. Two things settled in its review round
  and binding on later chapters: **the carbon-monoxide trigger is one person's
  headache, not two** — a headache indoors with a fire burning is enough on its
  own, and two at once escalates to everybody leaving including whoever feels
  fine — and **the twenty-five-times figure is a conductivity ratio, not a
  cooling rate**. Water conducts heat about twenty-five times better than air;
  what that does to a person is "several times faster", and no chapter may
  upgrade the twenty-five into a rate at which a body loses heat. Chapter 05
  also re-invokes chapter 01's second question, *can I breathe where I am
  standing?*, once, verbatim, at the end of its fire section, as the reader's
  route to the enclosed-space rule.

- **Chapter 06's own canon, now fixed.** Before the Collapse the narrator was paid
  to carry **four hundred pounds of dried beans** up an outside stair for **Arno
  Bligh** — a fortnight of afternoons, two sacks a trip — and Bligh starved that
  winter with the lot still stacked, having one pot, no fuel, and nobody in the
  building who knew a dried bean wants hours. The narrator offers that fortnight
  as the reason he is particular about sacks, and his back has not been right
  since the second week of it; the technique he draws out of it is to carry on
  the inside shoulder going up a turning stair. The chapter's deflating thesis is
  that **in the first week food is a morale problem and not a survival problem**,
  and it re-cites **Rule 9** for water rather than re-deriving anything.
  **Rule 19** is coined here and earned: he swapped nine pounds of good beans for
  a fist of salt and walked away certain he had been robbed, and by the next
  summer would have doubled it. **Rule 42** is coined here and earned by the
  twenty plants he had out of a book and could not confidently find one of in his
  own valley, and by the man on the crew who ate a root in the fourth spring that
  looked exactly like the right root — buried on the Tuesday, said once and
  walked straight past. The chapter refuses to teach plant identification at all,
  on the stated grounds that the book has no pictures and he does not know what
  grows where the reader is standing; later chapters should not quietly start
  identifying plants.
  Also his and available to later chapters: the storage five words (**dry, cool,
  dark, sealed, off the floor**), the shelf ordering (whole grains and legumes
  years, milled months, fats first to go and the thing most craved, salt
  indefinitely), the tin rule — **there is no inspection that clears a tin, only
  one that condemns it** — and the fuel-into-time cooking method: soak eight to
  twelve hours, throw the soak water away, **boil hard ten minutes** (the step
  that may not be shortened, because some dried beans are poisonous until they
  have had it and warm-but-not-boiling is worse), then a full lidded pot nested
  in a hand's depth of dry packing with as much underneath as over, unopened for
  three to five hours, **eaten that day**. **Hesper Vane** arrives in a clause
  about lids and never again. T.'s single beat here is appreciative and confirms
  the method is still taught in that order, hard boil before box. Chapter 06 also
  points forward once, without a number, to the later chapter on growing food and
  putting it up (chapter 13).

- **Chapter 07's own canon, now fixed.** The first stranger he dealt with on
  his own account is **unnamed for good** (see the Cast row above): she took
  his bucket on a track in his second year, he went two steps at her and went
  over a fence backwards into nettles, and he offers the fence as the most
  useful thing that ever happened to him because it meant neither of them did
  anything worse. She taught him the settlement-reading method over the
  following year. The method is his and hers and is available to later
  chapters rather than re-derivable: **read a place from outside for an hour,
  from above and out of the line of a path, before anybody has seen you** —
  the water and its queue and where the waste goes relative to it, the
  children, the animals' condition, which way the fences and the gate face,
  and what has been mended; no single sign decides, and you are listening for
  two signs that disagree. **Plenty of food and no visible children is the one
  sign that decides on its own, and the instruction is to walk past.** The
  approach technique is also his: daylight with the morning left, by the
  expected track, stopping far enough off to be seen, hands empty and visible,
  one person forward, and the three short facts (who you are, what you want,
  how long). **"Being unarmed is not the same as being harmless"** is coined
  here — unarmed is a fact about your hands, harmless is their judgement of
  your intentions — and so is the chapter's anti-paranoia position, which is
  **binding on later chapters: most people are not a threat, and the cost of
  treating everybody as one is lethal on a longer timescale.** No chapter may
  reverse that. **Rule 12** is coined here and earned by the credit he took —
  a sack now against two days' help at harvest, which became three weeks
  because he had eaten the sack. **Rule 31** is coined here and is the
  chapter's thesis; its apparatus (the four things settled out loud on the
  path, the means of leaving kept out of the common store, the cached bag
  outside, naming in advance the one thing that means you go) is his.
  **Leaving gets harder every day you stay** is said once and is not to be
  re-coined. The chapter also re-invokes chapter 01's third question, *does
  anyone know where I am?*, verbatim, once, in its last paragraph, as the
  reader's route from a room to a group. Its dark beat is that he could recite
  every sign of a group not safe to join on the afternoon he walked in through
  the Ninth Hand's gate with a broom over his shoulder, stayed two years, and
  says it was not that bad — delivered brightly, walked straight past, and the
  **setup for chapter 15**. The Ninth Hand's gate is canon from here: well
  maintained, hinges greased, bar on the inside, nine-fingered handprints on
  the planks. Chapter 07 cites **Rule 19** for not opening a trade with your
  best thing and does not re-derive it.

- **Chapter 08's own canon, now fixed.** In his third year **Benno Kell** sent him
  down a ladder into a pre-Collapse cache under a garage; he made four trips, came
  down the last three rungs of the fourth on his elbows and opened his shin on the
  bracket, and the shin is from that bracket. The cache held something over eleven
  thousand dollars of equipment by its own receipts and **not one maintained item**:
  cells left in the lamps had wept into the housings and crusted the contacts, the
  canvas had been folded away damp twelve years earlier and came apart in sheets,
  the fuel had turned to varnish. The one thing he kept is the **laminated inventory
  list**, which he has written on the back of in pencil for twenty-odd years, so the
  back is the front now; it is where his own kit list lives and it is available to
  later chapters as an object. The organising principle is his and is not to be
  re-coined: **gear is a loan against your future strength and every item pays rent
  in grams**, and a thing earns its weight only by doing what hands cannot, doing
  more than one job, or saving you carrying something heavier. The **six categories
  that always earn it** — a container you can put on a fire, a cutting edge,
  cordage, a means of fire needing no fuel of its own, light, and a way to carry
  water that is not the pot — are coined here, in that order, and T.'s beat
  establishes that the editor's era teaches exactly that list, in that order, to
  children before they can read it back. **Rule 6** is coined here and earned: he
  carried two identical lamps on the same cells for a year and they went out four
  days apart in the same November. **Rule 55** is coined here and earned by the
  cache. Also his and available without re-deriving: hone little and often because
  reground steel does not come back; no blade stored in a leather sheath; nothing
  put away wet or folded damp; rubber stored slack, dark and cool; cells out of
  anything not in use today; fuel as the shortest-lived thing in a cache; and the
  three repairability tests (can you see how it comes apart, does it fail gradually
  or all at once, is it made of what is still being made). The chapter reuses
  chapter 02's one-flight-of-stairs test and chapter 04's fifth-and-third load
  figures rather than re-coining either, and picks up the standing-dead-wood point
  chapter 05 dropped for budget (dry tinder comes from wood still standing, because
  the floor is where the water is); it leaves chapter 05's reflector point unused.
  **The cracked left lens is in his kit list, between the awl and the spare lace**,
  with the glare worked around by keeping a hat brim between the lens and the light
  and setting the lamp beside or behind him — and with no remark anywhere about
  nobody making lenses. He presents a kit that fits on a spread coat as a mild
  embarrassment and does not connect either it or twenty years of unbroken
  maintenance to his being sixty. **Vera Ott does not appear in chapter 08** despite
  the Cast column; her first appearance is now chapter 09 or later.

- **Chapter 09's own canon, now fixed.** In his sixth year he went through **two roofs in one autumn** — the first at a valley where a flat extension met the main pitch, where ponding water had rotted the joists soft; the second a fortnight later, having decided the first roof had taught him everything a roof could, by crossing the middle of a span instead of walking the line of nails. He went in to the shoulder and hung by the armpits over a stairwell. The autumn gets three sentences, two of them about joists. **Vera Ott first appears here**, in a clause, as the person he was fetching for that autumn. **The Tarn Street cold store** is canon from here (see Places): he names the building in an eleven-word clause — *we got into the Tarn Street cold store that same October* — and reports the order the stairs were cleared in (bottom to top, because clearing downward drives people into a stairwell with one end to it) and whose boots were wet from the flooded lift pit. He never indicates that anyone tells the day differently; T.'s single exasperated beat supplies what came out of it, names it **the Opening**, counts the eleven words and then abandons a sentence mid-way rather than finish it. **Rule 21** is coined here and earned by the roofs; **Rule 47** is coined here. Also his and available without re-deriving: the pre-entry read from outside (bowing or leaning walls, stepped cracks with clean edges or fresh dust, a dipping ridge, fire damage, undermined ground — **two signs together means walk away**, and none of them is a measurement); walking the **edges** of a floor where the joists bear, because a joist is weakest mid-span; reading the ceiling of the room below before the floor above; loading the bottom tread, hand on the wall not the handrail, one at a time on a stair; **nothing inside is worth being trapped inside with**; the sweep (one direction chosen at the door and never reversed, one room finished before the next, walls then middle then high then low); the **three clearing marks** (one stroke cleared, two crossed unsafe, one ringed occupied, with initial and month, charcoal or chalk at eye height on a face the weather does not reach, re-read after about a year); the **five reliably unsearched places** (utility and janitorial spaces, vehicle trunks and the spare wheel well, the backs of appliances, pharmacy stockrooms rather than shelves, office first-aid cabinets); the **third-year value order** (fasteners, containers that seal, uncracked rubber hose, dressings and medical supplies, salt, needles and thread — **nobody takes the needles**); and the ethics, stated without preaching: **he takes from the dead and not from the living, does not strip a place a family is still using, and leaves a sealed bottle of water on the step of everything he searches.** The chapter re-invokes chapter 01's second question, *can I breathe where I am standing?*, verbatim, once, as the reader's route to chapter 05 on below-ground spaces, and re-cites **Rule 31** (chapter 07) and **Rule 19** (chapter 06) rather than re-deriving either. It points at chapter 03 for what a first-aid cabinet is for, chapter 07 for reading an occupied place from outside, and chapter 08 for rubber. **Chapter 05's reflector point is still unused.** The glasses are not mentioned.

- **Chapter 10's own canon, now fixed.** Chapter 10 is **Vera Ott's chapter** and the only place in the book she speaks. Her assessment of the narrator, delivered to somebody else with him standing in the room holding the lamp, is fixed wording: **"a pair of hands with an opinion, and I can work with the hands."** He wrote it down the same night because he thought it was funny, was still holding the lamp six years later, and had stopped having the opinion. He met her a fortnight earlier by being **hit by a motorcycle in her yard at a walking pace, ridden by a man he had twice been told to stand clear of**, which put him across a length of angle iron and took the skin off his forearm from wrist to elbow; he is still enthusiastic about motorcycles and says he would have one now if anybody made tires. **He was her hands for six years** — he held, fetched, and turned things the direction she said to turn them, did not understand most of it at the time, and attributes the whole chapter to her. He has no idea that being her hands for six years is a credential, that she is quoted, or that anyone else learned from her; the reason she comes up is the fuel. T.'s single beat is appreciative and supplies what he will not: the woman who said it taught the man who taught everybody, and the insult is recorded in full with her first name nowhere near it.
  **Rule 38** is coined here and earned by a whole morning spent on a generator that would not turn over, after which she walked past, moved a lever six inches and said nothing. **Rule 64** is coined here and earned by one truck and eleven bicycles.
  Also his and available to later chapters without re-deriving: **fuel is the part with the clock on it**, gasoline the shortest-lived thing in the chain (roughly three to six months in a sealed can in the cool, anything older a maybe rather than a fuel) and diesel about a year kept clean and cool with water, growth and cold-weather wax as its own problems; the storage four words **full, sealed, cool, dark**, each with its reason; contents and month painted on the can, never a tag; never adding a doubtful can to a good one; and the safety set, which no later chapter may soften — **gasoline vapour is heavier than air, runs downhill and pools in the low place, so nothing is poured, pumped, drained or decanted indoors, in a shed or over a sunken place**; **fuel moving through a hose builds static**, so the can stands on bare ground, metal stays in contact and the pour is slow; **never siphon by mouth**; **never store fuel in a drinking container**. It re-cites chapter 05's carbon-monoxide position for not running an engine indoors and chapter 08's rubber-ageing flex test rather than re-deriving either. The diagnostic order **fuel, spark, air, compression**, one change at a time, with **step nothing** before it, is his and hers and is coined here. **Tires are the binding constraint on a vehicle's life**: rubber ages standing still, nobody the reader will ever meet can make one, and the discipline is shade, pressure, slow and light, taking every sound tire off every wreck, and counting the tires before committing to a vehicle. Her spine — most vehicles are not repairable by you, the correct relationship to a machine is maintenance rather than heroics, and a bicycle you can fix outlasts a truck you cannot — is hers and is not to be re-coined. **The chapter contains no weapons content and nothing incendiary, and the fuel section is a storage-and-safety section by design; later machine or vehicle chapters must keep it that way.**
- **Unclaimed significance held for a later chapter (chapter 10).** Chapter 10 says, in one clause inside the tires section, that **"we kept two trucks running for the Bell Country out to the far side of the lake in my eleventh year"**, and the only thing he records about it is an afternoon with a sidewall gone on a slope and nothing to put in its place. **No reveal of what those two trucks were doing, or what that run meant, appears anywhere in chapter 10, and T. does not supply one.** This is deliberate and is the first use of the withheld mechanism that replaces the retired clause-then-immediate-T.-reveal pairing (see `notes/style-guide.md` section 5). Any of chapters 13–19 may pick the run up and give it weight; whichever does should keep the narrator's own account as written — two trucks, the eleventh year, the far side of the lake, a sidewall on a slope — and must not have him notice afterwards. The lake is deliberately unnamed, so no Places row is owed.
