# The Long After — Illustration Style Guide

Reference document. Lives outside `manuscript/`, is never linted as prose.
It is read by two audiences: the twenty chapter writers placing `ILLO` slots,
and whoever eventually runs image generation. `notes/illustration-manifest.json`
(generated by `scripts/collect_illos.py`) points its `note` field at this
document for the preamble and reference-sheet instructions — so if you change
either here, the manifest's promise to the generator is still true.

Generation itself is out of scope for this task. Nothing here produces an
image. This document exists so that roughly sixty illustrations, generated
whenever and by whomever, look like one book.

---

## 1. The fixed prompt preamble

This exact block is prepended to **every** generation prompt, without
exception, verbatim, every time:

> Two-ink civil-defense manual illustration. Flat black line art plus a single rust-orange spot color, printed on cream paper with visible paper grain and slight ink misregistration. Heavy confident outlines, no gradients, no rendered lighting, no texture shading. Cartoonish rubbery figures with simple bean-shaped bodies, oversized hands, small heads, expressive postures. 1950s government pamphlet layout sensibility. Flat, graphic, legible at small size. No background scenery unless the slot prompt explicitly asks for one; default to empty cream paper ground, no horizon, no clutter. Each illustration sits inside a simple single-rule black border, like a boxed figure in a printed manual, never a bleed-to-edge image.

Do not paraphrase it per-chapter. The slot's own `prompt:` value describes the
*subject*; this preamble supplies the *style*. Concatenate preamble + slot
prompt + relevant reference sheet(s) at generation time — see section 8.

---

## 2. Palette

Three colors only. No others, no exceptions.

| Name | Hex | Use |
|------|-----|-----|
| Ink black | `#1A1A1A` | Line art, outlines, text/callout glyphs |
| Rust orange | `#C25B2A` | The single spot color — emphasis and nothing else |
| Paper cream | `#EFE6D4` | Ground |

The orange carries emphasis and nothing else: a callout numeral, a stamped
mark, a single highlighted part. If everything is orange, nothing is.

---

## 3. The do-not list

- No gradients.
- No rendered or cinematic lighting.
- No photoreal faces.
- No text in the image beyond callout numerals.
- No more than three colors.
- No modern logos or brand marks.
- No gore.
- No depiction of weapons or explosives manufacture.

These last two hold regardless of how a chapter frames its subject. A chapter
may describe a dangerous moment in prose; the illustration of it does not
show the blood or the bomb.

---

## 4. The two classes

The spec's human-facing word for the second class is **marginalia**. The
machine token — the only thing that may appear after `class:` in a slot
block — is **`gag`**. They are the same class. Write `class: gag`. Writing
`class: marginalia` is a format for a token that does not exist and will
fail `scripts/lint_manuscript.py`'s id/class check.

**Plates teach.** Numbered callouts keyed to the caption, labeled parts,
correct technique. Composition: single subject, generous white space,
callout numerals in orange circles.

A plate that teaches is reviewed against `notes/accuracy-log.md` exactly as
prose is. An inaccurate plate is worse than no plate — a reader can tell when
a sentence is vague, but a confident diagram reads as settled fact. If a
plate's technique isn't backed by an accuracy-log entry, don't draw it yet.

**Gags are marginalia.** Small, loose, placed in the margin of the argument
rather than the center — Mother Furnace's official portrait, a comparison
chart of tribe silhouettes, Vera Ott's expression mid-rant. Gags are never
load-bearing: a reader who skips every gag in the book loses nothing but
fun. They still obey the do-not list and the palette, but they do not need
an accuracy-log entry, because they assert nothing.

This asymmetry is the whole point of having two classes: scrutinize every
plate as hard as the prose it illustrates; let every gag be purely a joke.

---

## 5. Character and object sheets

The first generation run (before any chapter illustration) produces one
reference sheet per entry below. Every subsequent generation passes the
relevant sheet(s) in as a reference image alongside the preamble and slot
prompt. Silhouette is what survives style drift across sixty images, so each
entry below is written to be concrete enough that two independent generation
runs, months apart, produce a recognizably identical figure.

- **The narrator** — older man, long coat cut from a tarp, flat cap, one bad
  knee. Default: drawn from behind or in three-quarter profile; his face is
  not the subject. Fallback, for the rare slot that genuinely needs him
  front-on (both hands and face visible for a technique): his face may
  appear, but it stays generic and shadowed under the cap brim — no
  distinct features, no expression. No slot may compose a shot that relies
  on his face to carry emotion.
- **Vera Ott** — stocky, overalls, welding mask pushed up on her forehead so
  her face is framed by it. Always holding a tool, always mid-sentence.
- **Ais** — teenager, too-large coat, close-cropped hair, carries a satchel
  of papers.
- **Mother Furnace** — never a person. Always the same six-spoke iron wheel
  with a square furnace door at the hub, rendered as an icon.
- **The Ninth Hand's mark** — a nine-fingered handprint, orange, stamped not
  drawn.
- **The Spoke's mark** — a wheel hub with five spokes and one missing.
- **The go-bag** — the same bag in every appearance: canvas, two buckles, a
  patch on the lower left corner.

Do not redesign a silhouette mid-book because a later chapter wants it to
look nicer. If a chapter needs a detail the sheet doesn't cover (Vera Ott
angry vs. Vera Ott amused, say), that's a pose or expression note in the
slot's own `prompt:`, not a change to the sheet.

## 6. Slot block format

This is the authoritative `ILLO` block syntax. `scripts/lint_manuscript.py`
enforces it mechanically on every chapter — reproduce it exactly as shown
below, because the linter has no tolerance for a near-miss.

Rules the linter checks:

- All five keys must be present and non-empty: `id`, `class`, `caption`,
  `alt`, `prompt`.
- `id` must match `^(\d{2})-(plate|gag)-(\d{2})$` — a two-digit chapter
  number, the literal token `plate` or `gag`, a two-digit sequence number.
  Example: `04-plate-01`, `04-gag-02`.
- `class` must be exactly the token inside the id (`plate` or `gag`) — not
  "marginalia," not anything else.
- The id's chapter prefix must equal the chapter file's own two-digit
  prefix. A slot in `manuscript/04-water.md` must use ids starting `04-`.
- The block must be followed, within the next 3 non-empty lines, by a
  Markdown image link whose target contains `](../illustrations/<id>.png)`.
- Every id must be globally unique across the whole manuscript — no chapter
  may reuse an id another chapter already used, and no chapter may reuse one
  of its own.

**Worked example**, exactly as it must appear in a chapter file (this one
would live in `manuscript/04-water.md`):

```markdown
<!-- ILLO
id: 04-plate-01
class: plate
caption: Figure 4.1 — The three-stage filter, and the stage everyone skips.
alt: Cutaway of a layered gravel, sand, and charcoal filter in a cut barrel.
prompt: Cutaway diagram of a three-layer water filter built in a cut oil
  drum: coarse gravel at the bottom, sand in the middle, charcoal on top.
  Orange callout numerals label each layer and the drip spout. Single
  subject, generous white space, no background clutter.
-->
![Figure 4.1 — The three-stage filter, and the stage everyone skips.](../illustrations/04-plate-01.png)
```

**The `prompt:` trap.** The parser that reads these blocks
(`scripts/illo_blocks.py`) folds any indented continuation line into the
previous key's value — that's how the multi-line `prompt:` above works. It
has **no YAML block-scalar handling**. If you write `prompt: >` or
`prompt: |`, the parser does not treat that as "read the following indented
lines as a block" — it reads the literal characters `>` or `|` as the start
of the prompt text, and the rest silently folds in after it. Never write
`prompt: >`. Either keep `prompt:` on one line, or start the value on the
same line as the key and continue it on indented lines below, as shown in
the worked example.

---

## 7. Budget

Roughly 3 slots per chapter: 1 plate, 2 gags. Across 20 chapters, that is
roughly 60 slots total. This is a target, not a quota enforced by the
linter — a chapter with 2 plates and 1 gag, or 4 slots total, is fine if the
material earns it. Drift far from ~3 per chapter and the total generation
batch (and budget) stops matching what this document describes.

---

## 8. Generation workflow

For when nano banana (or any image generator) is connected:

1. Regenerate the manifest from the current manuscript:
   `python3 scripts/collect_illos.py`. This writes
   `notes/illustration-manifest.json` with every slot's id, class, caption,
   alt text, and prompt — nothing here needs hand-editing to stay in sync
   with the manuscript.
2. Generate the character and object sheets from section 5 **first**, before
   any chapter illustration. These become the reference images passed into
   every later generation.
3. Batch the manifest: for each slot, generate from (a) the fixed prompt
   preamble in section 1, (b) the slot's own `prompt` value, and (c) the
   reference sheet(s) relevant to anything the slot depicts (the narrator,
   Vera Ott, Ais, Mother Furnace, a faction mark, the go-bag).
4. Write each result to `illustrations/<id>.png`. The manuscript's image
   links already point there — landing the file is the only step; no prose
   or slot block needs to change.
5. Before accepting a plate, review it against `notes/accuracy-log.md` the
   same way a paragraph of prose would be reviewed: does the diagram show
   the technique the log actually backs? Gags skip this step — they assert
   nothing, so there is nothing to check them against.
